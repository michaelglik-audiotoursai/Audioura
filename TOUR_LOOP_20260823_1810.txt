Juan Gris embarked on a creative project titled "Au Soleil du Plafond" around 1916-1917, a collaboration with French poet Pierre Reverdy. Gris dedicated himself to this endeavor, producing illustrations to accompany Reverdy's poetry. But Gris's life was unexpectedly cut short in 1927 due to kidney failure, leaving the work incomplete. Despite this, the publication was further developed by others, highlighting the profound impact of Gris's early contributions.This work exemplifies the book as a collaborative art form, where Gris’s illustrations and Reverdy’s verses coalesce, advancing the exhibition's thesis on the revolutionary nature of collaborative artist books. The Torf Gallery, named after its patron, offers this unique insight into their partnership, usually confined to archives but now brought into the light. Around 1916, art dealer Léonce Rosenberg brought poet Pierre Reverdy and painter Juan Gris together to create *Au Soleil du Plafond*, originally planning an illustration by Gris for each of Reverdy's twenty poems. Work halted when Gris died prematurely of kidney failure in 1927 at age forty; while some accounts state Gris had already completed eleven lithographs before his death, others note he left behind original gouaches that the printer Mourlot Frères later translated into lithographs. Nearly thirty years after the project stalled, publisher Tériade revived it alongside Reverdy.

In 1974, Salvador Dalí embarked on a project to create illustrations for Sigmund Freud's 1939 book "Moses and Monotheism." This decision marked a significant departure from Dalí’s previous works, as he sought to visually interpret Freud's controversial ideas that Moses was an Egyptian nobleman who imposed monotheism on the Hebrews. Dalí's illustrations added a new dimension to Freud's text, merging psychoanalytic theory with surrealist art. As a result, these illustrations became a prominent example of how artists can reinterpret foundational texts, and they are now featured in the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston. These illustrations, published by The Hogarth Press, demonstrate the revolutionary form of the book as an artwork. Dalí's choice of illustrations, with their swirling forms and unexpected juxtapositions, reflect Freud's exploration into the psychological depths of religious belief. Through Dalí's visual storytelling, the exhibition argues that the book is not just a vessel for words, but a canvas for artists to explore and expand upon complex ideas.
