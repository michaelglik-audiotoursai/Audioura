Stop 1: Le Lézard aux plumes d’or (The Lizard with Golden Feathers)

Address: Museum of Fine Arts, 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3394, -71.0941

Orientation: Le Lézard aux plumes d’or (The Lizard with Golden Feathers) by Joan Miró, created in 1971, invites viewers into a world where imagination and symbolism intertwine. This piece is part of the Picasso, Miró, Dalí: Unbound exhibition at the Museum of Fine Arts in Boston. Details about its medium or physical orientation remain elusive, but understanding Miró's broader artistic journey enhances its appreciation.

This work captures Miró's hallmark use of surrealism, an art movement that sought to release the creative potential of the unconscious mind, often through dream-like scenes. One can imagine vibrant hues and fantastical creatures populating this realm, as Miró was known for his playful and abstract shapes. The title itself, "The Lizard with Golden Feathers," suggests a fantastical creature, blending the natural world with the mythical. This piece reflects the artist's ability to infuse ordinary objects with whimsy and wonder, a trait deeply rooted in his Catalan heritage. Catalonia, a region in Spain, is known for its rich folklore and vibrant cultural traditions, which frequently influenced Miró's work. In 1971, Miró was already an established figure in the art world, known for his innovative use of line and color. By this time, his work had evolved from the detailed precision of his early pieces to a more abstract and expressive style. The lizard, an often overlooked creature, becomes a symbol of transformation and resilience, adorned with golden feathers that suggest a regal or divine quality. This piece connects to the broader exhibition as it displays the unbounded creativity shared by Miró and his contemporaries, like Picasso and Dalí, who each redefined the boundaries of modern art in their unique ways.

Stop 2: Au Soleil du Plafond

Address: Museum of Fine Arts, 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Orientation: "Au Soleil du Plafond" is a collaborative work by Spanish painter Juan Gris and French poet Pierre Reverdy, dating from 1955. Although the medium of this artwork is not specified in the exhibition details, its creation during the mid-20th century reflects a significant period of artistic and literary interaction.

"Au Soleil du Plafond" combines the visual dynamism of Juan Gris with the evocative language of Pierre Reverdy, a french poet of the surrealist movement. Gris, known for his cubist compositions, often played with geometry and fragmented forms to portray everyday objects in new perspectives. Reverdy’s poetry, characterized by its philosophical depth and sparse language, complements Gris's visual style. This period was marked by a fruitful collaboration between visual artists and writers, reflecting a broader artistic movement that sought to dissolve the boundaries between words and images. The collaboration with Reverdy might have added layers of textual meaning that challenge the viewer to consider the interplay of visual and verbal art. The work likely encourages viewers to explore beyond the literal, inviting them into a conversation between the seen and the read, reflecting the complexity of modernist exchange.


Directions: Your final stop in Museum of Fine Arts, Boston: Moses and Monotheism.



Stop 3: Moses and Monotheism

Address: Museum of Fine Arts, 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Orientation: Stand centrally in front of the "Moses and Monotheism" exhibit, allowing the illustrations to unfurl before you. From this vantage, you can appreciate the interplay of Dalí’s surrealist approach with Freud’s psychoanalytic text, revealing layers of meaning that might otherwise escape notice.

Salvador Dalí's "Moses and Monotheism" comprises a series of illustrations created in 1974, inspired by Sigmund Freud’s exploration of religious origins and the psychological underpinnings of belief. Dalí, renowned for his surrealist style, utilizes vivid imagery to interpret Freud’s text, which examines the formation of monotheistic religions through the lens of psychoanalysis. The illustrations mirror Dalí's distinctive dreamlike distortions and symbolic motifs. One significant compositional decision is Dalí's use of distorted human figures, which symbolize the conflict between religious dogma and personal psyche. This distortion serves to visually communicate Freud's idea that religious belief is deeply intertwined with the human mind's complexities and contradictions. Historically, Freud's theories on religion were groundbreaking, proposing that monotheism was not merely divine revelation but rather a product of human psychological evolution. By illustrating this text, Dalí bridges art and psychoanalysis, inviting viewers to reconsider the origins of faith through an imaginative lens. In the broader context of Picasso, Miró, and Dalí: Unbound, this work underscores the show's theme of breaking traditional boundaries, both in art and thought. Dalí’s illustrations capture the essence of questioning established narratives, a common thread among the featured artists.
