Your first stop is Le Lézard aux plumes d’or (The Lizard with Golden Feathers). Le Lézard aux plumes d’or (The Lizard with Golden Feathers) by Joan Miró captures a surrealist essence, inviting viewers to explore a world where reality intertwines with imagination. Created in 1971, this work is part of a book that features forty lithographs, showcasing Miró's distinctive style and vibrant creativity.

Le Lézard aux plumes d’or is an evocative piece by Joan Miró, a master of surrealism known for his playful and abstract forms. The title, which translates to "The Lizard with Golden Feathers," conjures images of fantastical creatures and landscapes, a hallmark of Miró's artistic vision. The lithographs within the book are said to contain a dynamic interplay of colors and forms, inviting viewers into a world where the ordinary becomes extraordinary. Miró's work is often characterized by its spontaneity and the use of primary colors, which give a sense of buoyancy and movement. This is evident in Le Lézard aux plumes d’or, where each lithograph seems to leap off the page, creating a sense of motion and life. The decision to use lithography, a printmaking technique that captures fine lines and subtle gradations, allows Miró to impart a sense of immediacy and intimacy in his work, drawing viewers into his imaginative realm. The creation of this book was a collaboration with the poet Robert Desnos, who contributed text to accompany Miró's images. Desnos, a French surrealist poet, was known for his imaginative and dreamlike poetry, which pairs seamlessly with Miró's visual style. This collaboration between artist and poet highlights the surrealists' fascination with the subconscious mind and its ability to transcend ordinary perception. With more than 1.2 million annual visitors, the MFA provides a space for audiences to engage with groundbreaking works like Miró's, which challenge and expand our understanding of art and its possibilities.

Stop 3: Moses and Monotheism

Address: Avenue of the Arts, 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Orientation: Stand a few feet back from Salvador Dalí's illustrations for Sigmund Freud's "Moses and Monotheism". From here, you can fully appreciate the intricate interplay between text and image, a collaboration that brings Freud's psychoanalytic exploration of monotheism into vivid, surreal life. Focus on the way Dalí's surrealist style complements Freud's complex theories.

This was Freud's final original work, published in 1939 by The Hogarth Press, which delved into the origins of monotheism and suggested that Moses was an Egyptian priest. Dalí's illustrations capture the psychological depth of Freud's theories, infusing them with his signature style of distorted figures and dreamlike landscapes. By choosing this text, Dalí paid homage to Freud, whom he considered foundational to the Surrealist movement. The illustrations here are not just visual accompaniments; they transform Freud's complex ideas into a tapestry of vivid, surreal images that invite viewers to consider the intersections of culture, belief, and identity.
