  ---

  ### 1. The Spark: Freud, The Hogarth Press, and the 1939 Translation
  The literary foundation of this work begins in the late 1930s. As Sigmund
  Freud fled Nazi-occupied Vienna for London, he completed his final and most
  controversial book, *Moses and Monotheism* (1939). In this radical thesis,
  Freud argued that Moses was not born Hebrew, but was an Egyptian nobleman
  who championed the monotheistic cult of Akhenaten and was ultimately
  murdered in a rebellion by his followers—a suppressed trauma that Freud
  claimed shaped Western religion and culture.

  Freud’s groundbreaking text reached the English-speaking world primarily
  through **The Hogarth Press**, the independent publishing venture founded by
  Leonard and Virginia Woolf. The Hogarth Press became the official publisher
  and distributor of Freud’s translated psychoanalytic works (in partnership
  with the Institute of Psycho-Analysis). The press’s ethos—bridging avant-
  garde literature, psychology, and fine bookmaking—cemented Freud's ideas as
  central catalysts for 20th-century modernist thinkers and artists.

  ---

  ### 2. Dalí’s Obsession with the Father of Psychoanalysis
  Salvador Dalí revered Freud, viewing psychoanalysis as the engine of the
  Surrealist method (specifically his "paranoiac-critical" approach). Dalí
  finally met an aging Freud in London in July 1938, facilitated by the writer
  Stefan Zweig and collector Edward James. During the encounter, Dalí produced
  a famous sketch of Freud, prompting Freud to remark that he was fascinated
  by Dalí's technical rigor and intensity.

  Decades later, in his 1957 *Diary of a Genius*, Dalí reaffirmed his
  fascination with Freud’s daring deconstruction of myth:
  > *“Without hesitation I place Freud among the heroes. He has dispossessed
  the Jewish people of the greatest and most influential of all heroes:
  Moses…”*
