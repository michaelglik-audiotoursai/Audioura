Stand before "Le Lézard aux plumes d’or (The Lizard with Golden Feathers)" to truly appreciate the integration of color and form in Joan Miró's work. From this vantage point, the intricate interplay of lithographs and vellum reveals how the imagery and text harmonize, reflecting the surrealist themes of hope amidst chaos.

Published in 1971 by Louis Broder, "Le Lézard aux plumes d’or" exemplifies the revolutionary collaboration between artist and publisher. Broder's dedication to the livre d'artiste—a limited edition book where visual and literary art merge—was well-known. His decision to work with Miró, alongside the renowned printer Mourlot Frères, a famous printing workshop in Paris, aimed to push the boundaries of printed art. Boris Fridman, a collector passionate about such works, gifted this piece to the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, enriching the museum's collection and emphasizing the importance of collaborative ventures in art history.

Stop 3: Moses and Monotheism

Address: 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3396, -71.0877

Orientation: As you stand before the "Moses and Monotheism" display, position yourself so you can see the entire suite of illustrations. From there, the interplay between Salvador Dalí’s surreal imagery and Sigmund Freud’s radical ideas will become more pronounced, revealing the suspenseful tension within the work.

In 1974, Salvador Dalí embarked on an intriguing project, creating a set of ten illustrations to accompany Sigmund Freud’s controversial text, "Moses and Monotheism." Freud crafted a narrative that challenged the very origins of monotheistic religion, positing Moses as a figure born from Egyptian nobility, rather than Hebrew descent. Dalí’s illustrations, executed as drypoints and lithographs on sheepskin, add a surreal dimension to Freud's provocative exploration of identity and religious origins. In doing so, Dalí underscored the exhibition's thesis that books can be revolutionary art forms, deeply collaborative endeavors involving both visual and textual dialogues. By integrating these boundary-pushing illustrations with Freud's text, Dalí not only amplified Freud’s unconventional hypotheses but also enriched the broader narrative within this exhibition of artists pushing literary boundaries. The choice of sheepskin as a medium adds an antiquarian and sacred feel to the work, aligning with the profound and sacramental themes explored by Freud.
