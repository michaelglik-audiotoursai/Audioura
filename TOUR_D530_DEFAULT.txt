Joan Miró collaborated with art publisher Louis Broder to create *Le Lézard aux plumes d’or*, an illustrated book pairing the artist's poetry with original lithographs. Miró had the entire poem reproduced in lithographic print alongside his artwork, matching fifteen color lithographs with twenty-three pages reproducing his handwritten script. The lithographic plates were printed by the Mourlot workshop in Paris. This action brought to life a limited edition collection of 40 vibrant lithographs, which Miró had created to accompany his own poems. Because Broder published this work, it became one of Miró's most celebrated illustrated portfolios, cementing its place in art history. Miró’s work in this edition exemplifies the synergy between image and text, with each lithograph echoing the surreal poetry that threads through the book. The choice of using publisher’s vellum for the binding not only adds a tactile richness but also reflects the luxurious nature of the piece, emphasizing its status as a collector's item. The Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, proudly displays this work, a gift from Boris Fridman, underscoring the museum's commitment to showcasing pivotal artistic collaborations that redefine the boundaries of traditional media. Here, the book is not just a container of art but an artwork in itself, echoing the exhibition's thesis that such collaborative ventures revolutionized how art and literature coexist.

Fernand Mourlot’s Parisian workshop printed the original color lithographs for Joan Miró’s portfolio *Le Lézard aux plumes d’or*, which was published by Louis Broder. Mourlot also produced the original lithographic exhibition poster to promote the presentation of the suite at the Galerie Berggruen. To standardise and document the project, art historians catalogued the individual prints using Mourlot's name as the official catalog raisonné reference. Broder specialized in publishing limited edition books that married the talents of artists with skilled printers. In this case, the lithographs were printed by the renowned Mourlot Frères, a studio that brought Miró's vision to life with its expertise in color lithography. This collaboration was not just a routine commission; it was part of Broder's larger campaign to elevate the livre d'artiste to new heights. The integration of text, imagery, and material in this work advances the exhibition's thesis, demonstrating that the book is a unified art form. Boris Fridman, a dedicated collector, later gifted this remarkable piece to the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, enhancing its collection of such collaborative works. Fridman's donation underscores the significance of collectors in preserving and sharing these innovative artistic endeavors.
