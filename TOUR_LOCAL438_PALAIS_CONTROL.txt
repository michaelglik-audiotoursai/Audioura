Orientation: You are about to explore the Palais Lascaris in Nice, home to a remarkable collection of over 500 musical instruments, showcasing the evolution of craftsmanship and artistry over centuries. Within this historic palace, you will encounter four exceptional works, including Raquel from the late 16th century, the Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore from Milan in 1696, a Guitar by Antonio de Torres crafted in Almeria in 1884, and a Guitare baroque by Giovanni Tesler from Ancona in 1618. Your first stop is Raquel (panneau, fin du XVIe siècle). Position yourself near the entrance of the Palais Lascaris in Nice's old town to appreciate the historical context that envelops you.

The Palais Lascaris, the music museum established in 2001, constructed in the seventeenth century, became a museum in 1942 when the city of Nice acquired it. Renowned violinist Jacques Thibaud, known for interpreting classical works, performed here, as did symphony conductor Eugene Ysaye. The palace now houses one of France’s most significant musical instrument collections, second only to Paris, in addition to being a decorative arts museum. The palace's interiors feature painted ceilings and murals attributed to Giovanni Battista Carlone, offering not just visual beauty but also a rich history of art and music. The exhibits today continue the tradition of connecting past artistic achievements with present-day appreciation, showcasing how music integrated into aristocratic life and brought communities together through shared experiences.

Stop 2: Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore (Milan, 1696)

Address: Palais Lascaris

Coordinates: 43.6962, 7.2767

Orientation: Stand close to the Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore, crafted in Milan in 1696, to appreciate the fine craftsmanship and the luthier’s unique approach. From this angle, you can observe the intricate detailing that became a hallmark of Testore's work, solidifying his family's reputation in the evolution of instrument making.

The collection includes Testore's Basse de violon, a masterpiece that was originally mislabeled. Evidence illuminated the truth of the instrument’s origins, establishing its rightful place in the luthier’s legacy. Paolo Antonio Testore, a renowned Milanese luthier, crafted this instrument with the precision and dedication for which the Testore family was known. He built upon the foundational teachings of his father, Carlo Giuseppe Testore, who had established the family workshop after studying with Giovanni Grancino, another key figure in Italian string instrument craftsmanship. Carlo's legacy passed to his sons, ensuring the family's impact on Milanese violin making.

Stop 3: Guitar by Antonio de Torres (Almeria, 1884)

Address: Palais Lascaris

Coordinates: 43.6962, 7.2767

Orientation: As you stand here, focus on the "Guitar by Antonio de Torres (Almeria, 1884)" positioned elegantly against the backdrop of Palais Lascaris' rich history. This vantage point allows you to appreciate the details that made Torres' work revolutionary.

Antonio de Torres, often referred to as the father of the modern guitar, revolutionized guitar design in unprecedented ways. In 1884, during his time in Almeria, Torres crafted this particular guitar, leaving an indelible mark on the world of music. His work set new standards for sound quality, much like Stradivari's influence on violin making in the classical tradition. Torres began his career as a carpenter, which profoundly influenced his approach as a luthier, integrating precision and craftsmanship in his instruments. His transition from carpentry to lutherie brought a unique understanding of woodwork that would later elevate the guitar's construction to new heights. The Palais Lascaris, a seventeenth-century aristocratic building in nice, housing an extensive collection of over 500 instruments, including this significant piece, underscores the museum's dedication to preserving the history of music. A curious connection within this collection is the bass violin by Paolo Antonio Testore from Milan, 1696. Both Testore and Torres exemplified transformative impacts in their respective fields, leading to significant changes in musical instrument craftsmanship.

Orientation: "Guitare baroque by Giovanni Tesler (Ancona is 1618)" at Palais Lascaris and note its proximity to other baroque guitars, offering a glimpse into the evolution of this instrument during a transformative era.

The "Guitare baroque by Giovanni Tesler (Ancona, 1618)" is a rare gem within the Palais Lascaris, a seventeenth-century aristocratic building in nice, reflecting the intricate craftsmanship typical of the baroque period. Tesler, an esteemed Italian luthier active in Ancona during the early 17th century, crafted this guitar with precision, possibly featuring a longer neck originally. Gautier's passion for preserving historic instruments led him to bequeath his collection, including this guitar, to the Palais Lascaris. His donation, made in 1904, significantly enriched the museum's holdings. The collection now ranks as the second most important set of historical instruments in France, surpassed only by the Musée de la Musique in Paris, part of the cité de la musique. This particular guitar offers insights into the evolution of string instruments, with its design reflecting the innovative trends of its time. Tesler’s work emphasizes not only the aesthetic appeal but also functional adaptability, marking a stage in the guitar's development that would eventually lead to more modern forms. The presence of Tesler’s guitar alongside others in the collection illustrates the historical journey of the guitar, making it a crucial piece in understanding the cultural identity that these instruments have shaped over centuries.
