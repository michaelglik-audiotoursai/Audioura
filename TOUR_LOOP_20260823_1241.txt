Stop 2: Au Soleil du Plafond by Juan Gris

Address: 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Orientation: Look for Au Soleil du Plafond in the galleries.

In 1927, the Spanish artist Juan Gris began an ambitious project with French poet Pierre Reverdy. The pair intended to produce a livre d'artiste, a collaborative work where art and poetry unite seamlessly. However, Gris's untimely death at age 40 of kidney failure left this endeavor incomplete. Despite such a loss, Éditions Verve in Paris published the book in 1955, crafting it with 11 lithographs derived from Gris's unfinished pieces, ensuring the project came to fruition long after his passing. Gris and Reverdy's venture was meticulously printed by Mourlot Frères, a renowned lithographic workshop in Paris known for their precision and fidelity to artist intentions. Here, Gris's cubist vision mingles with Reverdy’s evocative poetry, merging visual and literary art, as they intended. By exhibiting "Au Soleil du Plafond", the Museum of Fine Arts Boston furthers the exhibition's argument that artists like Gris revolutionized the book as an art form. The gallery, named for the Linde Family Torf, showcases how this integration of image, text, and typography creates an immersive experience. The rare public display of this work allows one to observe firsthand the echoes of an artistic dialogue nearly a century old, inviting a reflection on the belief systems that shape such transformative art. In 1916–1917, poet Pierre Reverdy, art dealer Léonce Rosenberg, and artist Juan Gris planned *Au Soleil du Plafond*, intending to pair twenty poems by Reverdy with twenty illustrations by Gris [newyorker.com, art-books.com]. The project stalled, and when Gris died of kidney failure in 1927 at age 40, he had completed only eleven illustrations [araderbooks.com, newyorker.com]. His son preserved the original works until publisher Tériade revived the project nearly three decades later [newyorker.com, araderbooks.com]. The book was finally released in 1955, featuring Reverdy's handwritten text published alongside Gris's lithographs as a posthumous tribute to the artist [newyorker.com, araderbooks.com, art-books.com].

In 1974, Salvador Dalí took on the task of interpreting Sigmund Freud's 1939 book, "Moses and Monotheism," through a series of illustrations. Dalí, renowned for his surrealist art, transformed Freud’s complex theories into vivid visual narratives, allowing a broader audience to engage with the psychoanalytic exploration of monotheism. Freud’s original text examined the psychological underpinnings of monotheistic belief systems, which Dalí visually expanded, bringing a unique artistic perspective to the discussion. The exhibition at the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, demonstrates how this collaboration transcends traditional book illustration, showcasing the book as an art form where images and words coexist in a unified, seamless dialogue. The collaboration between Dalí and Freud, although indirect, highlights how two intellectual titans—each in their field—merged their talents to innovate how foundational texts could be presented. By interpreting Freud’s psychoanalytic insights visually, Dalí not only enriched the textual content but also contributed to a deeper understanding of the cultural and historical implications of monotheism. Through Dalí's artistic vision, the narrative within "Moses and Monotheism" emerged not only as a scholarly essay but as an immersive artistic experience, urging visitors at the exhibition to reconsider the roles of art and text in the conveyance of complex ideas.
