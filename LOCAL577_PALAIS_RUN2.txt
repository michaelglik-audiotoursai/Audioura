Step-by-Step Audio Guided Tour: Palais Lascaris, Nice, France - Museum Tour
Tour-Category: museum

Stop 1: Violes gambe by William Turner (Londres, 1652)

Address: 15 Rue Droite, 06300 Nice, France

Coordinates: 43.6984, 7.276

Orientation: Welcome to Palais Lascaris, a captivating museum nestled in the heart of Nice, France, dedicated to the evolution of musical instruments and their profound influence on European music culture. As you explore this remarkable collection, you will encounter four exquisite works: the Violes gambe by William Turner, the Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore, the Harpe by Naderman, and the Sacqueboute ténor by Anton Schnitzer. Each instrument reveals a unique chapter in the history of music-making, uniting craftsmanship with cultural resonance. The Palais Lascaris, bequeathed to the city of Nice in a testament dated 1901, houses this extraordinary collection, including one of the earliest surviving sackbuts in original condition by Anton Schnitzer, and Turner's Violes gambe, often played in concert within the palace's salon. This exhibition, opened to the public in 2011, offers a rare glimpse into the historical musical instrument collections of Nice, transferred from the Musee Massena to this splendid venue. Your first stop is Violes gambe by William Turner (Londres, 1652). Stand in the intimate salon of the Palais Lascaris, where the light dapples through antique windows, casting a warm glow on the six-string bass viola da gamba crafted by William Turner in 1652. This positioning allows you to appreciate the intricate heart-shaped ornament carved on the back of the pegbox scroll, a signature of Turner's craftsmanship.

In 1652, the English luthier William Turner created this six-string bass viola da gamba in London. Its distinctive feature—a heart-shaped ornament carved into the pegbox scroll—exemplifies Turner's attention to detail and artistic vision. This generous act ensured its preservation and public accessibility at the Palais Lascaris, a former aristocratic residence now home to France's second most significant collection of musical instruments, only surpassed by the Musée de la Musique in Paris. Its presence in the Palais Lascaris is a poignant reminder of the rich musical dialogues that transcended national borders. This viola da gamba, once played in private salons, now resides in a public space, bridging centuries of musical history. This particular instrument, with its rich timbre, was often played in concerts held in the palace's salon, resonating through the halls and bringing to life the sounds of a bygone era. The intricate heart-shaped design on the pegbox scroll is a signature element found in many of Turner's creations, underscoring the artistic flair and innovation that marked his contributions to lutherie. This decorative choice is more than an aesthetic embellishment; it symbolizes the heart and soul poured into crafting each instrument, reflecting both the luthier's personal touch and the broader artistic currents of the time.

Directions: Continue through Palais Lascaris — next is Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore (Milan, 1696).

Stop 2: Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore (Milan, 1696)

Address: 15 Rue Droite, 06300 Nice, France

Coordinates: 43.6984, 7.276

Orientation: Stand to the left of the display case to view the internal label, which offers a rare glimpse into the instrument's complex history.

In 1904, the Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore became part of the City of Nice's collection through the bequest of Antoine Gautier, an amateur musician and collector. Gautier's decision to donate his collection to the city ensured that this instrument, along with others, would be accessible to the public and preserved for future generations. As a result, the Basse de violon is now housed at Palais Lascaris, maintained as a museum exhibit rather than a playable instrument, allowing visitors to appreciate its historical significance and craftsmanship. Complicating its history, an internal label reads 'Carlo Giuseppe Testore in contrada larga di Milano al Segno dell' Aquila 169(6ms)', which historically led to its attribution to Paolo Antonio Testore. This label suggests a familial connection to Carlo Giuseppe Testore, Paolo Antonio's father, who founded the Testore workshop in Milan. This connection highlights the multi-generational craftsmanship of the Testore family, whose reputation in instrument-making remains influential in music history. This bass viol, measuring 1,445 mm in total length, showcases the intricate design that made Milanese instruments a staple in European orchestras.

Directions: Next: Harpe by Naderman (Paris, 1780).

Stop 3: Harpe by Naderman (Paris, 1780)

Address: 15 Rue Droite, 06300 Nice, France

Coordinates: 43.6984, 7.276

Orientation: As you stand here, admire the fine details of the Harpe by Naderman, crafted in Paris around 1780. Position yourself to observe its ornate carvings and gilded embellishments, capturing the exquisite finesse associated with Jean-Henri Naderman's work.

Jean-Henri Naderman, renowned for his craftsmanship, built this harp to serve the opulent tastes of the French aristocracy. He was originally appointed harp maker in ordinary to Queen Marie-Antoinette in 1778 by crafting unparalleled instruments that elevated the harp's status in French courts. The harp itself, standing at an impressive 161.5 centimeters, is crafted from fine wood and adorned with various materials, echoing the aesthetic sensibilities of 18th-century France. This dedication to detail is not just ornamental; it signifies the era's intersection of music and art, where instruments were both tools for sound and reflections of social status. At Palais Lascaris, the harp connects to a broader narrative of musical evolution, complementing pieces like the basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore from 1696. These instruments collectively illuminate the changing roles and technical transformations within the world of classical music, offering glimpses into historical shifts that shaped today’s symphonic compositions. As you continue your journey, consider how the craftsmanship and cultural significance of this harp compare to other instruments in the collection.

Directions: Your final stop in Palais Lascaris: Sacqueboute ténor by Anton Schnitzer (Nuremberg, 1581).Stop 4: Sacqueboute ténor by Anton Schnitzer (Nuremberg, 1581)

Address: 15 Rue Droite, 06300 Nice, France

Coordinates: 43.6984, 7.276

Orientation: Stand before the sacqueboute ténor by Anton Schnitzer, crafted in 1581, and note the meticulously engraved bell garland bearing Schnitzer's name and the year, a testament to its enduring history.

 Schnitzer, a renowned craftsman of the Nuremberg tradition, specialized in creating ceremonial brass instruments, and this sacqueboute exemplifies his skill. The refined engravings and structural integrity speak to the Schnitzer family's legacy in brass instrument craftsmanship. It serves as the oldest surviving sackbut in its original condition, linking the medieval sound world to the classical music era. Just as the harp by Naderman you saw earlier reveals the evolution of string instruments, this piece offers insight into the progression of brass instruments, setting the stage for the development of the modern trombone.
