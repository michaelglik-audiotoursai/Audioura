Stop 1: Violes gambe by William Turner (Londres, 1652)

Address: 15 Rue Droite, 06300 Nice, France

Coordinates: 43.6985, 7.276

Orientation: You are about to explore the Palais Lascaris in Nice, a historic gem transformed into a vibrant music museum, thanks to the city's acquisition in 1901. Within its majestic walls, you will encounter an exquisite collection of four musical masterpieces: the Violes gambe by William Turner, the Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore, the Harpe by Naderman, and the Sacqueboute ténor by Anton Schnitzer. Each of these works reflects the evolution of musical craftsmanship, inviting you on a journey through time without ever leaving the room. In 2001, the historical musical instrument collections of the city of Nice were transferred from the Musée Masséna to the Palais Lascaris with the project of transforming it into a music museum In the stops ahead, you will discover William Turner's viola da gamba created during the English Civil War at Violes gambe by William Turner (Londres, 1652) and the harp found in the attic of the Château de Busset at Harpe by Naderman (Paris, 1780). Your first stop is Violes gambe by William Turner (Londres, 1652). Stand before the elegant "Violes gambe by William Turner (Londres, 1652)" in the intimate salon of the Palais Lascaris. From this vantage point, the instrument's intricate craftsmanship and the unique heart-shaped decorative cutout on the scroll come into full view, inviting you to explore its historical resonance.

 Among his treasures was the "Violes gambe" crafted by William Turner in 1652. This viol found a new home in 2001 when it was transferred to the Palais Lascaris as part of the museum's opening, ensuring that it could be appreciated in a setting dedicated to musical history. William Turner, primarily known for his paintings, created this viola da gamba during a time of great political upheaval in England, the English Civil War. Crafted in London, the viol embodies the transitional period between the Renaissance and Baroque music eras. The distinctive heart-shaped cutout on the scroll is a hallmark of Turner's workshop, reflecting both his artistic flair and meticulous attention to detail. This decorative element not only serves an aesthetic purpose but also symbolizes the luthier's unique signature, setting it apart from other instruments of its time. The viol was often played in concerts held in the palace's salon, filling the space with the resonant and haunting tones characteristic of the viola da gamba. As you contemplate this viol, consider the innovations that would follow in the realm of string instruments, such as the development of the modern violin family, which owe much to the foundational craftsmanship seen here.

Stop 2: Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore (Milan, 1696)

Address: 15 Rue Droite, 06300 Nice, France

Coordinates: 43.6985, 7.276

Orientation: ** Stand close to the bass violin by Paolo Antonio Testore, crafted in 1696, and notice the intricacies of its composition that reveal a rich lineage of craftsmanship and a tale of legacy.

In 1696, within the bustling city of Milan, Carlo Giuseppe Testore crafted this remarkable bass viol, affixing his internal label to the finished piece. The Testore family, known for their dedication to exceptional craftsmanship, often inscribed their decorative borders using a marking gauge, or trusquin, rather than traditional inlaid purfling, showcasing their innovative approach. Over two centuries later, in 1904, the instrument entered the collection of the Palais Lascaris through the bequest of Antoine Gautier, a Niçois collector and lawyer who sought to enrich the museum's holdings. Gautier’s generous donation ensured that this piece, attributed to Paolo Antonio Testore, his son, would be preserved and appreciated by future generations. Today, the Palais Lascaris houses this viol as part of France's second most significant collection of musical instruments, highlighting both the historical trajectory and the importance of preserving such culturally invaluable artifacts.

Directions: Next: Harpe by Naderman (Paris, 1780).

Stop 3: Harpe by Naderman (Paris, 1780)

Address: 15 Rue Droite, 06300 Nice, France

Coordinates: 43.6985, 7.276

Orientation: As you stand before the Harpe by Naderman, crafted in Paris in 1780, take a moment to look at its intricate craftsmanship. This position allows you to appreciate its elegant height of 161.5 cm and the exquisite detailing that captures the opulence of its era.

The story of this harp begins with Jean-Henri Naderman, who crafted it in 1780 in Paris during a period when he was making instruments for the French court. Appointed as harp maker to Queen Marie-Antoinette in 1778, Naderman's work was characterized by its adherence to the standards of elegance and refinement the court demanded. This particular harp was later discovered in the attic of the Château de Busset, adding a layer of intrigue to its history. This harp not only reflects the artistic and cultural aspirations of its time but also embodies a journey through history, from its creation in the Enlightenment period to its current status as a cherished exhibit.

Directions: Your final stop in Palais Lascaris: Sacqueboute ténor by Anton Schnitzer (Nuremberg, 1581).

Stop 4: Sacqueboute ténor by Anton Schnitzer (Nuremberg, 1581)

Address: 15 Rue Droite, 06300 Nice, France

Coordinates: 43.6985, 7.276

Orientation: Stand to the right of the sacqueboute’s display case, allowing the ambient light to catch the hammered brass surface. From this vantage point, you can appreciate the craftsmanship and precision that went into its creation, highlighted by the subtle gleam of the engraved details.

In 1581, Anton Schnitzer the Elder crafted this tenor sackbut in Nuremberg, using hammered sheet brass. The intricate inner bore entrance, with its diameter of about 10 millimeters, exemplifies Schnitzer’s dedication to precision and acoustic quality. This instrument became indispensable for understanding Renaissance musical craftsmanship, thanks to its unique design and durability. Gautier's decision not only enriched the museum's collection, making it the second most significant in France, but also ensured that this instrument would become a prototype for modern acoustic reconstructions of Renaissance tenor sackbuts. The sackbut's survival in its original condition is a rare triumph. It allows contemporary musicians and historians to explore the soundscapes of the Renaissance era authentically. The nearby harp by Naderman, also in this grand collection, shows how the evolution of musical instruments has been integral to our cultural heritage.
