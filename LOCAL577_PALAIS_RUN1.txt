Stop 1: Violes gambe by William Turner (Londres, 1652)

Address: 15 Rue Droite, 06300 Nice, France

Orientation: Welcome to the Palais Lascaris in Nice, a museum dedicated to showcasing an exquisite collection of historical musical instruments. As you explore, you will encounter the Violes gambe by William Turner, a Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore, a Harpe by Naderman, and a Sacqueboute ténor by Anton Schnitzer. Each piece represents a facet of European innovation and transformation in music. This collection was bequeathed to the city of Nice in the early 20th century, reflecting a legacy of cultural appreciation. Notably, the Palais Lascaris houses one of the earliest surviving sackbuts, crafted by Anton Schnitzer in 1581, preserving a piece of brass instrument history in its original condition. Meanwhile, the Violes gambe by William Turner, often played in the palace's salon, highlights how musical instruments served as mirrors of societal changes across centuries. Your first stop is Violes gambe by William Turner (Londres, 1652). As you step into the ornate salon of Palais Lascaris, turn your gaze to the bass viola da gamba crafted by William Turner in 1652. From this vantage point, the elegance of its craftsmanship becomes apparent, offering a glimpse into the refined musical heritage of 17th-century England.

In 1652, English luthier William Turner crafted this bass viola da gamba in his workshop near Aldgate, London. Turner, renowned for his precision and artistry, produced instruments that resonated with the distinct, rich harmonies favored by the English court of the time. This blend of technical skill and aesthetic sensibility made him one of the leading luthiers of his era. Several centuries later, the viola found its way into the hands of the Musée des Beaux-Arts, where it became part of an illustrious collection of historical instruments. This was made possible through the efforts of Antoine Gautier, a 19th-century collector based in Nice, who recognized the value of such musical artifacts for cultural preservation. Eventually, the instrument was integrated into the Nice Conservatory's collection, adding layers of history and significance to its journey. The final leg of its voyage brought the viola to the Palais Lascaris. This 17th-century aristocratic residence in Nice, transformed into a museum, now houses over 500 instruments, making it one of the most significant collections in France after the Musée de la Musique. When you ponder the journey of this instrument, consider the courts of continental Europe and their own efforts to rival England's advancements in music, highlighting a fascinating period of cultural exchange and artistic competition. The viola da gamba not only serves as an object of beauty but also as a symbol of an era when music was a bridge between nations.

Address: 15 Rue Droite, 06300 Nice, France

Coordinates: 43.6995, 7.276

Orientation: Stand directly in front of the Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore, Milan, 1696. From this vantage point, you can appreciate its remarkable craftsmanship and trace the lineage of Italian luthiers that shaped its creation.

In 1904, the Basse de violon entered the municipal collections of Nice due to the posthumous bequest of Antoine Gautier, an amateur musician and collector. Gautier had amassed a collection of musical instruments over his lifetime, and his decision to bequeath these instruments to the city ensured their preservation and eventual display at the Palais Lascaris. As a result, the Basse de violon, attributed to Paolo Antonio Testore and cataloged under inventory number C007, became accessible to the public, allowing visitors to appreciate its historical and musical significance. Its arched two-piece back is made of maple, while the belly is crafted from spruce, revealing the meticulous attention to detail that Paolo Antonio Testore inherited from his father, Carlo Giuseppe Testore. The ebony fingerboard adds a touch of elegance and durability, showcasing the craftsmanship that made Testore violins sought after in their time. The legacy of the Testore family is further highlighted by the auction record for a cello by Paolo Antonio Testore, which reached $177,000 in May 2017. This underscores the enduring value and admiration for Testore's work.

Stop 3: Harpe by Naderman (Paris, 1780)

Address: 15 Rue Droite, 06300 Nice, France

Coordinates: 43.6995, 7.276

Orientation: From this vantage point, you can admire the harp by Naderman (Paris, 1780), slightly to your left. The best view of this piece comes from this angle, where you can take in not only its intricate design but also glimpse the broader narrative of its journey through time and history.

In 1995, this exquisite harp, once belonging to the Viscountess of Beaumont, was discovered in the dusty attic of -Busset. Jean-Henri Naderman, renowned for his craftsmanship, built this harp in Paris around 1780, and it stood witness to an era when harps symbolized harmony and progress. Standing at 161.5 centimeters tall, this wooden instrument is more than just a relic; it’s a piece of history. The woodwork and the intricate carvings reflect the craftsmanship of the time, exhibiting both artistry and functionality that positioned the harp as a musical and a cultural symbol. Naderman, a pivotal figure in Parisian musical circles, crafted the harp during a transformative period when the ideals of the French Revolution began to influence all facets of life, including music. At a time when France was embracing modernity and change, the harp's elaborate design subtly echoed the revolutionary ideals of progress and enlightenment. The harp's presence here, amidst the other 17th and 18th-century instruments, weaves a narrative that connects various eras and musical innovations. With an impressive lineage and a story that intertwines music, history, and personal ownership, this harp remains an enduring testament to the cultural and artistic strides made during its time.

Directions: Your final stop in Palais Lascaris: Sacqueboute ténor by Anton Schnitzer (Nuremberg, 1581).

Stop 4: Sacqueboute ténor by Anton Schnitzer (Nuremberg, 1581)

Address: 15 Rue Droite, 06300 Nice, France

Coordinates: 43.6995, 7.276

Orientation: Stand close to the displayed sacqueboute ténor by Anton Schnitzer, crafted in Nuremberg in 1581. From this vantage, the exquisite engravings on the bell garland and the maker's mark become visible, highlighting Schnitzer's attention to both aesthetic and functional detail.

In 1581, Anton Schnitzer the Elder meticulously crafted the sacqueboute ténor in Nuremberg, marking it with his maker's mark and inscriptions. What sets this instrument apart is its original, signed mouthpiece—the earliest known trombone mouthpiece to have survived alongside its instrument. Schnitzer, renowned for his brass craftsmanship, created this piece as part of a larger tradition in Nuremberg, known for its brass instrument-making guilds. The sacqueboute, made from brass, features an entrance bore diameter of approximately 10 to 10.5 mm and a bell garland diameter of roughly 100 mm. Its traditional slide mechanism contributed significantly to bridging the Renaissance and Baroque musical styles, impacting how modern trombones are played today. This piece remains central to understanding the transition in musical instrument design, much like the harp from the first room demonstrated the evolution of string instruments. Reflecting upon this sacqueboute’s design and craft, one can appreciate how these early brass instruments paved the way for future musical innovations.
