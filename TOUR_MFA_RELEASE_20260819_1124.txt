In 1971, Joan Miró created "Le Lézard aux plumes d’or" as a series of forty color lithographs, meticulously illustrating an entire book. As a result, "Le Lézard aux plumes d’or" became one of Miró's celebrated illustrated portfolios, capturing the imagination of art enthusiasts and preserving his artistic legacy. This piece exemplifies the exhibition's argument that the book itself can be a work of art, merging image, text, and material into a cohesive whole. The lithographs were printed by the renowned Atelier Mourlot, a studio known for its expertise in translating an artist's vision onto printed media. The material choice of vellum brings a tactile richness to the book, enhancing the vibrant colors and lending a luminous quality to the pages. The work was gifted to the museum by Boris Fridman, the collector who gave this work to the museum, adding to the MFA's comprehensive collection of Surrealist-era art. Stop 2: Au Soleil du Plafond by Juan Gris

Address: Avenue of the Arts, 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3397, -71.0886

Orientation: The ceiling here transforms with every angle, unveiling hidden depths in the fusion of words and images in "Au Soleil du Plafond."

In the early 1916-1917 period, L. Rosenberg initiated a project that would later become known as "Au Soleil du Plafond," with Juan Gris as the designer and Pierre Reverdy as the author. Despite the project's promising start, Gris's untimely death in 1927 meant he only completed half of the intended work. Reverdy, undeterred by the incomplete state, continued to envision a publication that would do justice to their collaboration. It wasn't until 1955 that their vision came to fruition, with 11 lithographs brought to life on Arches paper. Éditions Verve, recognizing the significance of this endeavor, stepped in to publish the final version. This release marked it as Gris’s last body of work and a poignant reminder of his artistic contributions. In the tapestry of the exhibition, the intersection of Gris’s images and Reverdy’s words exemplifies the show’s argument that artists revolutionized the book as a collective art form. These lithographs echo the transformative power of artistic dialogue, inviting reflection on how such themes relate to our deepest beliefs.

Orientation: As you stand before "Moses and Monotheism," position yourself so you can fully appreciate how the light interacts with the rich texture of the sheepskin. This angle will reveal the intricate details of Salvador Dalí's illustrations, where shadows and highlights play on the surface, drawing you into the depths of this unusual medium.

In 1974-75, Salvador Dalí created a set of illustrations and lithographs on sheepskin for Sigmund Freud's "Moses and Monotheism." This work was significant because it represented Dalí's engagement with Freud's controversial final book, which explored the origins of monotheism and posited that Moses was an Egyptian nobleman. By producing these illustrations, Dalí brought a visual dimension to Freud's provocative ideas, integrating his surrealist style with the themes of psychoanalysis and religion. In a striking decision, Dalí chose to render these on sheepskin, enhancing the physical and historical connection to ancient scrolls. Freud, as the author of the text, delivered a narrative that wove deep psychoanalytic insights with religious history, setting a challenging stage for Dalí's artistic interpretation. This fusion of ideas between Dalí and Freud resulted in a unique artistic interpretation that remains a notable part of Dalí's oeuvre.
