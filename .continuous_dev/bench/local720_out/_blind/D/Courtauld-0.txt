Welcome to the Courtauld Gallery in London. You are about to stand before Édouard Manet's A Bar at the Folies-Bergère. Stand directly in front of the canvas, a little to the left of centre, so that the barmaid's face sits level with your own. From there you will see the mirror behind her, and the reflection in it, which is the key to the painting's famous puzzle.

The painting is an oil on canvas, roughly four feet long, and it shows a crowded evening at the Folies-Bergère music hall in Paris. The barmaid stands behind a marble counter loaded with bottles of champagne and beer, and a dish of oranges sits at the lower edge of the picture. Behind her runs a large mirror in a gold frame, and in that mirror we see the rest of the room: the glittering crowd, a gentleman in a top hat leaning toward her, and, at the upper left, a pair of green feet belonging to a trapeze artist performing above the audience. The barmaid herself is a real person, known as Suzon, who worked at the Folies-Bergère in the early 1880s. Manet posed her in his studio. Look closely at her expression. Her face is blank and withdrawn, while the reflection shows her apparently in conversation with a customer. The gap between the two is the subject of the work.

Manet painted this in 1882, and it was shown at the Paris Salon that same year. He was by then seriously ill, suffering from the effects of syphilis and the circulation problems that would lead to the amputation of his leg. It is considered his last major work, and he died the following year. He had spent his career painting modern Parisian life, the cafés, the streets and the theatres, and here he returned to a place of public pleasure at the moment his own strength was failing. The painting was probably begun as a study of a bar scene and grew into a far more ambitious composition, one that asks the viewer to question what they are actually seeing.

The mirror has troubled critics since the first reviews. The reflection seems to place the viewer in the wrong position, and early writers accused Manet of getting his perspective wrong. In 2000, the art historian Malcolm Park built a physical reconstruction of the scene and showed that the perspective is in fact possible. The solution is that the man in the top hat stands outside your field of view, to the left, and looks away from the barmaid. The conversation many visitors assume is taking place does not exist. The reflection invents it.

The oranges, meanwhile, carry a second message. The art historian Larry Ligo has argued that Manet habitually used oranges to signal prostitution in his paintings, and the historian T. J. Clark reads the barmaid as one of the women the Folies-Bergère was known for, offered to customers as both a server and a commodity. The painting is quietly about how she is looked at.

This work belonged to the composer Emmanuel Chabrier, a close friend of Manet's, who hung it above his piano. It now forms part of the Courtauld's famous Impressionist and Post-Impressionist holdings, which also include Van Gogh's Self-Portrait with Bandaged Ear
