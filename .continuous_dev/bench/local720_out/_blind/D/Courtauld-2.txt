Stand in front of the wall at the centre of the room, about two metres back from the canvas, so that the whole painting fits your field of view. From this distance the surface looks almost smooth, and the wall behind the young woman seems to glow. Step closer only after you have taken this in. Then you will see that the glow is built from thousands of tiny dots of pure colour, which is the technique Georges Seurat is best known for.

This is Young Woman Powdering Herself, a painting in oil on canvas that Seurat made between roughly 1888 and 1890. A young woman sits in a dim interior, dabbing powder onto her face while she looks into a small mirror. Her body is turned away from us, her shoulders are bare, and a pale dress lies in soft folds around her. The light comes from one side, and the artist models the shadows across her skin and the wall with the same small touches of colour.

The method is called divisionism, or pointillism, a term many critics later applied to it. Seurat did not blend his paints on the palette. He placed separate dabs of unmixed colour next to one another on the canvas, so that the eye mixes them at a distance. A shadow that looks grey from across the room turns out, up close, to contain blues, greens and violets set side by side. He was working from colour theory of the time, including ideas about how complementary colours intensify each other when placed close together. The result is a surface that feels calm and luminous rather than loosely brushed.

Seurat painted the piece to study how light and shadow behave under this system, and the intimate subject lets him focus on that problem. Its meaning is quieter than the public scenes he is famous for. Here the question is one of looking: a woman caught in a private moment, observed by an artist who treats her body as a set of colour relationships. Scholars have noted how the composition, with its angled mirror and cropped figure, reflects a broader interest in modern women's everyday routines that many painters of the period explored.

The model was Madeleine Knobloch, who was Seurat's partner and bore him two sons, so the painting carries a personal weight that the formal rigour can obscure. Seurat was living in Paris in these years, working on a demanding technical programme while his private life was closely bound up with his art. This canvas comes from the late phase of his career, shortly before his death in 1891 at the age of thirty-one, and it shows how far he had pushed the dot technique into domestic subjects.

Scientific examination has added another layer. A scanner built by the Italian Institute of Optics revealed that the vase of flowers in the upper left corner was originally a reflection of Seurat himself at work, which would have made it the only self-portrait he ever produced. He painted over it, so the figure of the artist is now hidden beneath the flowers.

The Courtauld is home to a major group of Impressionist and Post-Impressionist paintings, and this Seurat hangs among works by Manet, Cézanne and Van Gogh. Notice how differently the brushwork of those neighbours reads beside this surface of small, deliberate points.
