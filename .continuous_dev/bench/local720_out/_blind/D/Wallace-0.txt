Welcome to the Wallace Collection. Stand in front of the portrait on the left-hand wall of the main gallery, at a distance of about two metres, and look at the man's face first. From this distance the upturned moustache and the slight lift at the corners of his mouth read as one clear expression, while the painting's loose, quick brushwork stays hidden until you move closer.

This is The Laughing Cavalier, painted in 1624 by the Dutch artist Frans Hals. It is a half-length portrait of a man in a richly embroidered silk costume, shown turning slightly toward us, with one hand resting at his waist. The canvas measures about 83 by 67 centimetres. Its inscription in the upper right corner, in Latin, gives the sitter's age as twenty-six and the year as 1624, which tells us he was born around 1597 or 1598.

The sitter's identity is unknown. Nineteenth-century titles in Dutch, English and French mostly describe a military man, an officer in one of the part-time civic militia companies that Hals and Rembrandt painted in group portraits. But he may just as easily have been a wealthy civilian. The art historian Pieter Biesboer has suggested he could be Tieleman Roosterman, a cloth merchant who also sat for Hals.

Look at the sleeve and the lace cuff. The embroidery is dense and expensive, and it is carried out in silk thread. Now step closer, to about a metre. Hals painted it with long, fast strokes that you can see only up close. He worked quickly and loosely, and that speed gives the costume a sense of movement, as if the sitter has just turned toward the artist. The low viewpoint and the turning pose also push the embroidered sleeve and cuff toward the viewer, so that the richest part of the costume dominates the picture.

The embroidery is also symbolic. Bees, arrows, flaming cornucopias, lovers' knots and tongues of fire were emblems of the pleasures and pains of love, a common theme in Dutch art of the period. An obelisk stood for strength, and Mercury's cap and staff, the caduceus, stood for good fortune.

The name The Laughing Cavalier is a later invention. Victorian audiences gave it the title when the painting was shown in London in 1872 to 1875, and reproductions made it one of the best-known old master images in Britain. The man is not laughing. His expression is enigmatic, and the upturned moustache makes his smile seem larger than it is.

Hals was unusual for his time in showing sitters smiling in formal portraits. Commissioned portraits rarely did so before the late eighteenth century. Here, the smile and the sense of motion are what make the painting feel alive, and they are why art historian Seymour Slive called it "one of the most brilliant of all Baroque portraits."

The painting's eyes seem to follow you as you move across the room. That effect comes from the sitter looking straight out toward the artist's position, while the flat canvas stays fixed. Wherever you stand, he appears to be looking at you.
