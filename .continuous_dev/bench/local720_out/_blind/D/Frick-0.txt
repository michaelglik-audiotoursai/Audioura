Stand directly in front of Officer and Laughing Girl, about two or three paces back from the canvas, where the painting reads as a whole. From this distance the window light on the left falls across the woman's face and the map on the back wall comes into view as a single pale rectangle. Step closer and the details dissolve into brushwork; stay here and the room's composition becomes legible.

This is an oil painting on canvas, roughly 50 by 46 centimeters, made around 1657 by Johannes Vermeer. It is small enough to hold at arm's length, and it hangs in the Frick Collection alongside two other Vermeers, Girl Interrupted at Her Music and Mistress and Maid. The scene is domestic. A woman in a yellow bodice sits at a table near an open window, and a soldier in a red coat leans toward her with a wine glass raised. Light enters from the left and sculpts her face, the one area of the picture that seems fully lit.

The yellow dress is a schort, a bodice with braiding that Vermeer painted often, usually on women in ordinary dress. Over it she wears a blue apron, most of which sinks into the shadow under the table. Blue aprons were everyday wear then because they hid stains, so the apron tells you she was working, perhaps in the middle of her morning chores, when the soldier arrived. The wine glass tells you something else. Wine cost more than beer in that period, so holding one signals a household with some money.

The soldier's presence is a deliberate device. He fills the lower foreground, his red coat and black officer's sash pressed close to the viewer. Painters call this repoussoir, a dark or strong object placed in front to push the scene back and create depth. Caravaggio used it constantly, and Vermeer likely learned it from Caravaggio's followers. His hat is made of beaver pelt, a material chosen for resistance to rain and snow, and such pelts came from the New World, probably New Netherland, the Dutch colony in what is now the eastern United States, then under the control of the Dutch West India Company.

The map on the wall is identifiable as a Willem Blaeu and Balthasar Florisz. van Berckenrode map of Holland and West Friesland. Vermeer owned it and used it in three of his paintings, and the linen-backed, rod-mounted sheet is rendered with enough accuracy that scholars can match it to Blaeu's 1621 edition.

The meaning of the encounter is open. Many historians read it as an innocent courtship, a soldier calling on a woman during her morning routine. Others point to her open hand and half-smile as signs of a discreet willingness, and the painting supports both readings without settling on either.

For Vermeer, around 1657, this was a work about the way a painted space can hold a relationship, a wall map, and a shaft of light at once. Across the room, the Frick's Cabinet Gallery has in recent years hosted new paintings by the contemporary Canadian artist Kent Monkman, who took this very picture as a starting point to examine how seventeenth-century Dutch art recorded global trade and colonization
