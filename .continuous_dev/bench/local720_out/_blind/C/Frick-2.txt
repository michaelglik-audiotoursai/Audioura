The Portrait of Comtesse d'Haussonville, completed in 1845, is an oil painting on canvas that captures Louise de Broglie, a member of the prestigious House of Broglie and an accomplished writer. Painted in pale blues, greys, browns, golds, and whites, the cool grey-blue satin of her dress mirrors the hue of her eyes, creating a harmonious color scheme that emphasizes her refined elegance. Ingres uses a meticulous technique to render the fabric’s folds with a smoothness that almost simulates the texture of satin, highlighting his mastery of oil paint to convey tactile qualities. The composition centers on her raised left-hand index finger, placed coyly near her mouth, and her right arm, which is unnaturally elongated in the manner characteristic of Ingres’s style—this elongation lends a sinuous elegance and a subtle tension to her posture. Behind her, a mirror reflects her image, a motif Ingres first explored in his 1814 Portrait of Madame de Senonnes, adding layers of introspection and complexity to the work. The foreground dresser holds writing implements, pots, flowers, and an ornate oriental vase, suggesting the intellectual and cultured life of the sitter. Critics such as the French poet Charles Baudelaire praised this portrait for its “voluptuousness,” and he described Ingres as the quintessential painter of women, emphasizing the artist’s ability to combine idealized beauty with psychological presence.

Jean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres painted this portrait during a pivotal moment in his career when he was widely acclaimed but somewhat reluctant to focus on portraiture, which, though lucrative, distracted him from his preferred subject of History painting. At this time, he only accepted two portrait commissions, including this one. Ingres began an earlier oil version around 1842 but abandoned it when Louise de Broglie became pregnant and could no longer endure the long, slow sitting sessions required. Despite his personal dissatisfaction with the final result, feeling he did not fully capture her charms, the portrait was well received by her family and contemporaries. This work marks an important stage in Ingres’s evolving approach to portraiture, where he balanced precise draftsmanship with a poetic and sometimes idealized representation of the sitter.

Emotionally, the portrait conveys a subtle blend of demureness and intellectual presence, inviting the viewer to sense the sitter’s composed dignity alongside a quietly confident inner life, reflecting both Ingres’s artistic intentions and Louise de Broglie’s own character as a learned and creative woman.

The Frick Collection acquired this portrait as part of its holdings of European masterworks, where it complements the museum’s rich array of 18th- and 19th-century paintings, providing insight into French Neoclassical portraiture alongside its broader focus on refined craftsmanship and historical significance.
