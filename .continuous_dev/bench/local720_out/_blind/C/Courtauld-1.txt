You are standing directly in front of Van Gogh’s iconic Self-Portrait with Bandaged Ear, displayed prominently at The Courtauld Gallery in London. From this close vantage point, you can clearly observe the thick, textured brushstrokes where Van Gogh applied paint in heavy layers, a technique called impasto that gives the surface a vibrant energy and reveals the motion of his hand. This detail would be lost from farther away, where the portrait might appear smoother and less dynamic.

The painting shows Vincent van Gogh himself wearing a blue cap trimmed with black fur and a green overcoat. A white bandage wraps around his head, covering the ear on the right side of the canvas, though this is actually his left ear as he painted his reflection in a mirror. Behind him is an open window, where you can make out a canvas on an easel marked with indistinct strokes alongside a Japanese woodblock print depicting geishas in a landscape. This inclusion reflects Van Gogh’s deep admiration for Japanese art, particularly woodblock prints, which influenced his use of bold colors and spatial composition. The thick paint and vibrant palette express an intense passion, while the fur cap, recently purchased to hold the heavy bandage in place, speaks to the harsh winter and his fragile condition at the time. Van Gogh created this self-portrait in January 1889 shortly after a dramatic personal crisis: in Arles, after a confrontation with fellow artist Paul Gauguin, Van Gogh severed his own left ear and delivered it to a local prostitute named Rachel, who then alerted the authorities. This work captures not only his physical injury but also his psychological turmoil. Art historians note how the impasto technique, inspired by the French painter Adolphe Monticelli, energizes the composition and conveys Van Gogh’s emotional intensity.

Vincent van Gogh painted this portrait during a critical moment in his life. Having moved from Paris to the south of France, he hoped to establish a supportive artists' community called the Studio of the South. His relationship with Gauguin was pivotal yet strained, culminating in the ear-cutting incident that marked a turning point in his mental health and artistic journey. This portrait stands as a testimony to both his vulnerability and his determination to continue working amidst personal adversity.

When viewing the painting, one senses a powerful mix of resilience and fragility. The direct gaze, the vivid colors, and the visible brushwork combine to reveal a man confronting pain and isolation while still committed to his art.

The Courtauld Gallery acquired this painting in 1928 through Samuel Courtauld’s purchase. It remains one of the gallery’s most celebrated works, exemplifying its outstanding collection of Impressionist and Post-Impressionist masterpieces, alongside pieces by Manet and Cézanne.
