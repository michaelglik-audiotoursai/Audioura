Stand a few paces back from Sappho and Alcaeus, centered in front of it. The painting is nearly four feet wide, a long horizontal, and from here you can take in the whole sweep of white marble seating at once. Up close you see paint. From this distance you see the theater, and the curve of the tiers pulling your eye toward the two figures at the center.

Alma-Tadema painted this in oil on a wooden panel, 66 by 122 centimeters. The panel gives the surface a hard, smooth finish, which suits the subject. The marble steps are rendered so crisply that the stone seems to hold sunlight. Look at the long bench running through the middle of the picture. Carved into its front edge are names. In the real Theatre of Dionysus in Athens, which this setting is based on, seats were inscribed with the names of Athenian officials. Alma-Tadema replaced those names with the names of Sappho's friends. It is a private joke for anyone who bothers to read the stone.

Now the scene itself. On the left, Alcaeus of Mytilene plays the kithara, a large wooden concert lyre with a sound box and two upright arms, plucked with a pick. Alcaeus was a poet from the island of Lesbos in the late seventh century BC. Facing him sits Sappho, the other great poet of Lesbos, leaning forward with her arm on a cushion. Resting on that cushion is a laurel wreath, the traditional prize for a performer. She holds the reward in reserve while she listens. Around her are several of her female friends, some attentive, some drifting. Behind them, through a screen of trees, is the blue line of the Aegean Sea.

The picture illustrates a passage by the ancient poet Hermesianax, preserved in a text by Athenaeus called the Deipnosophistae, meaning The Philosophers' Banquet. Alma-Tadema was a scholar's painter. He built his scenes from texts and archaeology, then staged them with a precision that made antiquity look like a place you could walk into.

The painting was exhibited at the Royal Academy in London in 1881 and was well received. Punch, the satirical magazine, called it "marbellous," a pun on all that marble. The painter William Powell Frith included it in his picture of that same exhibition, A Private View at the Royal Academy, 1881, where the artist John Everett Millais can be seen studying it at the far right.

Lawrence Alma-Tadema was Dutch by birth and settled in Britain, becoming one of the most successful painters of the Victorian era. His specialty was the ancient Greek and Roman world, recreated with obsessive archaeological detail. Sappho and Alcaeus shows him at the height of that method, taking a few lines of an ancient poem and building a whole afternoon around them.

Notice how the composition keeps the two poets apart. Alcaeus plays to Sappho, and Sappho listens only to him, while the marble and the sea stretch away behind. The moment is quiet, held between the last note and the wreath.
