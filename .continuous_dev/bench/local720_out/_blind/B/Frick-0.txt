Stand a few feet back from Officer and Laughing Girl and center yourself on the woman's face. From there you can take in the whole small canvas at once: the soldier's dark bulk on the left, the pale window light, and the map behind them. Then step closer to the officer's hat, where the paint becomes more interesting.

This is an oil painting on canvas, about 50 centimeters by 46, made around 1657. The soldier is huge by comparison with the woman. He sits in the immediate foreground, in a red coat with a black sash that marks him as an officer, and his wide-brimmed hat is a dark mass that blocks much of the left side. Painters call this device repoussoir, a French term for placing a large object up front so that everything behind it seems to recede. It makes the room feel deep and makes the girl look small and distant, bathed in light. Vermeer probably learned it from followers of Caravaggio, the Italian master of dramatic shadow.

Look at that hat. It is beaver pelt, which was weather-resistant, and the pelts were imported from the New World, probably from New Netherland, the Dutch colony in what is now the eastern United States. The painting is carrying the fur trade into a small room in Delft.

The map on the wall is the other piece of global trade. It is identifiable as Willem Blaeu's 1621 map of Holland and West Friesland, mounted on linen and wooden rods. Vermeer evidently owned a copy, because he used it in three paintings.

Now the woman. She wears a yellow bodice with braiding, called a schort, which was everyday dress, and a blue apron mostly lost in the shadow of the table. Blue aprons were common because they hid stains. That detail has led historians to suggest the officer has dropped in on her during her morning chores. She holds a wine glass, and since wine cost more than beer, it hints at some wealth.

X-ray photographs reveal that Vermeer changed his mind. He first planned to give her a large white collar that would have covered much of the yellow dress. He painted it out. He also later extended her cap to cover all her hair, which pulls the eye toward her face and her smile.

Scholars disagree about what is happening. Many see an innocent, honorable courtship. Others read her open hand and smile as a discreet willingness to engage in sex. Historians believe a painting by Gerard van Honthorst inspired the composition, and that Vermeer may have used a camera obscura, an early optical device that projects a room's image, to set the perspective.

The woman resembles Vermeer's wife, Catharina Bolnes, who is believed to have posed for many of his paintings. The yellow dress, the window on the left, and the map all return in other works. What is new here is the man at the table.

The picture hangs here as one of the Frick's three Vermeers, alongside Girl Interrupted at Her Music and Mistress and Maid.

The light touches her face, yet the large, dark shape of the officer is what stays with you, a presence that fills the room and leaves her smile the brightest thing in it.
