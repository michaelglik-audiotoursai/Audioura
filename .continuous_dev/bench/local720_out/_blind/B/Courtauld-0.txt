Stand in the LVMH Great Room, about two metres back from Manet's A Bar at the Folies-Bergère, and let your eyes settle on the barmaid's face first. From here, her expression and the long gold edge of the mirror behind her both fit into your view. Then slide your gaze to the right edge of the canvas, where a small reflected figure appears. That man in the mirror is the key to the picture, and you only catch him once you step back.

This is oil on canvas, a little over four feet wide, and the whole width is taken up by a mirror. The barmaid, a real woman named Suzon who worked at the Folies-Bergère in the early 1880s, stands facing you. Manet posed her in his studio, not in the music hall itself. Behind her, the mirror reflects the crowded room: the glow of lights, the balconies, and, in the upper left corner, a pair of small green feet, the legs of a trapeze artist performing above the audience.

The puzzle is the reflection. Her back appears at the right, bent toward a top-hatted man, yet she stands upright and faces you. Critics accused Manet of not understanding perspective. In 2000, the art historian Malcolm Park staged a physical reconstruction of the scene and showed the geometry works. The man is not standing in front of her at all. He stands to the left, outside the painter's view, looking away from her. The viewer is meant to stand to the right, closer to the bar than he is, which departs from the single central viewpoint most perspective pictures assume.

Look at the foreground. A dish of oranges sits on the marble. According to the art historian Larry Ligo, Manet habitually used oranges to signal prostitution. T. J. Clark reads the barmaid as both a saleswoman and a commodity, something to be purchased along with a drink. The beer bottles, the champagne, and the flowers at her bodice are all painted with the same careful attention as her face.

That face is the center of the picture. Manet's preliminary study placed her off to the right. In the finished canvas he moved her to the middle but left her reflection at the right. In the mirror she seems engaged with a customer. Head-on, she is remote and self-protective.

The painting also echoes Las Meninas, the masterpiece by Diego Velázquez, an artist Manet admired. Both use a mirror to complicate where the viewer stands.

This was Manet's last major work. He painted it in 1882 and showed it at the Paris Salon that year, and he died the following year. He was ill, and the scene, full of glitter and noise, is painted with a stillness that sets the barmaid apart from the crowd she serves.

The painting came to the Courtauld through the collection of Samuel Courtauld, who also owned a version of Manet's Déjeuner sur l'Herbe, Renoir's La Loge, and eight major works by Cézanne.

Look again at the small figure at the far right. The tilt of her reflected body, set against the stillness of the woman facing you, is the last thing Manet wanted you to notice.
