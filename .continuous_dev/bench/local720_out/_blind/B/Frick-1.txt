The museum is currently showing Gainsborough: The Fashion of Portraiture, its first special exhibition devoted to the artist and the first on his portraiture ever held in New York. It argues that a painted portrait was subject to fashion just as the sitter's clothing was. The shape, size, and handling of a portrait could be avant-garde or out of date, the same way the cut of a jacket could.

That idea is worth carrying to this painting. A portrait is a record of a person, and it is also a choice about how to present them, made within the conventions of its moment.

Alongside the Gainsborough show, a Cabinet installation displays twenty-four hand-colored engravings of French fashion from the late eighteenth century. They come from Gallerie des modes et costumes français, published between 1778 and 1787 and the largest and most influential series of its kind. It ran to over four hundred images, billed as drawn from life, showing the headwear, jewelry, and dress of French people from many walks of life. No complete set survives, because many sheets were thrown away when styles changed and others may have been lost in the French Revolution.

The Frick Art Research Library holds three hundred and seventy original prints from the series, and this is the first time a selection has been shown at the museum.
