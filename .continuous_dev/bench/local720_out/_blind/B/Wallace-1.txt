Stand a few steps back from *A Dance to the Music of Time*, near the centre of the canvas, so that the ring of four dancers sits at eye level. From here the circle reads as a closed loop, and you can see how the whole picture turns on it. Then let your eye travel up and to the left, into the sky, where a second, smaller procession is easy to miss from close range.

The painting shows four figures holding hands and dancing in a ring, one seen mostly from behind. At the right sits Time, a winged, naked greybeard, playing a lyre. Behind them, in the early-morning sky, Aurora, goddess of the dawn, goes ahead of the chariot of Apollo, the sun god. The Hours accompany him, and he holds a ring that stands for the Zodiac. The dancers and the sky are two views of the same clock: one turning on the ground, one turning overhead.

Who the dancers are has never been settled. According to the biographer Gian Pietro Bellori, the patron, Giulio Rospigliosi, dictated the subject. On that account the four are Poverty, Labour, Riches, and Pleasure or Luxury, a cycle in which Pleasure leads back round to Poverty. Map those onto the Four Seasons and Poverty is Autumn, Labour is Winter, and so on. The art historian Anthony Blunt argued that the figure at the back, unusually for a Season, is male, and the museum now describes him as Bacchus, the god of wine. That fits a story in which Jupiter, answering complaints from the Seasons and Time about the misery of mortal life, gave the world Bacchus and his wine as compensation.

Not everyone accepts this as the original idea. Malcolm Bull traces the imagery to the Greek poet Nonnus, as filtered through the French poet Ronsard. In that text Spring wears roses in her hair, Autumn's brow is wreathed with olive, Winter has bound hair and a shadowed face, and Summer is dressed in white with ears of corn in her braids. Bull suggests Rospigliosi, a writer fond of allegory, invented the Poverty-and-Riches reading during or after the painting's completion. The scholar Ingamells goes further and doubts that Poussin cared much about pinning down each identity.

Look closely at the surface and you can see the painter changing his mind. Pentimenti, the ghostly traces of earlier decisions, show that Poussin removed a second, larger tree on the right, between Winter and Time, clearing space around the old musician. The painting is in generally good condition, though it has been retouched. A large L-shaped tear once ran straight through the central group of dancers, and the repair is part of what you are looking at.

Poussin was a French painter working in Rome, and this canvas, made between about 1634 and 1636, comes from the period when he was turning toward the ordered, classical manner that would define him. It was a commission, and he was working to a patron's intricate programme rather than inventing freely.
