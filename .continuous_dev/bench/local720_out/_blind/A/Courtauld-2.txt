# Georges Seurat: *Young Woman Powdering Herself*

## Orientation

Seurat's *Young Woman Powdering Herself* is best seen from two distances. From a few paces back, the wall behind the woman looks like a smooth field of shifting light and shade. Step closer and that field breaks apart into thousands of small, separate dots of colour, which is how Seurat built the whole image. Then look to the top left-hand corner, at the vase of flowers. That patch hides the most personal secret in the painting.

## The Work

A young woman sits at a small dressing table, powder puff raised, absorbed in her own reflection. The painting dates from between 1888 and 1890, and it is one of the major works Seurat completed before his early death.

The technique matters most here. Seurat did not mix his colours on the palette. He placed small dots of pure, unmixed colour side by side and varied how densely and intensely he clustered them. The modulation of light and shadow on the wall comes entirely from those changing concentrations. Where the dots crowd together or deepen in tone, the wall darkens. Where they thin out or brighten, it seems to glow. Your eye does the blending, so the colour stays vivid rather than muddy.

This method is called pointillism, after the French word for a point or dot. Seurat developed it together with the painter Paul Signac. It became the signature of Neo-Impressionism, the movement that took the Impressionists' interest in light and tried to make it systematic and almost scientific.

The subject is intimate. The model was Madeleine Knobloch, Seurat's mistress, who bore him two sons. Seurat kept this relationship largely private, so the painting is unusually personal: a portrait of the woman he shared his life with, caught in a private daily ritual.
