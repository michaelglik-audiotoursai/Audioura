**The Death of Caesar — Audio Stop**

Stand back far enough from *The Death of Caesar* to take in the whole scene at once. This is a painting about a crowd closing in on one man, and its meaning depends on the relationship between the figures, not on any single face. Up close, you see individuals. From a distance, you see the trap.

The painting shows the assassination of Julius Caesar, the Roman general and dictator stabbed by senators in 44 BC. For artists around 1800, this was more than ancient history. It was a story about power, conspiracy, and whether killing a ruler could be justified. Those questions felt urgent in a Europe living through revolution.

The painter is Vincenzo Camuccini. The painting was commissioned in 1793 by Frederick Hervey, the 4th Earl of Bristol, an English aristocrat and collector. Camuccini had already made him a copy of Raphael's *Deposition*, a Renaissance painting of Christ's body being carried to the tomb. The new commission was Camuccini's chance to move from copying a master to inventing his own grand composition.

He began with a cartoon, a full-scale preparatory drawing used to plan a large painting. Critics in Rome received it favourably. But when he finished a first painted version in 1796, it was less well received. So Camuccini destroyed it and started again from nothing. The version that survives took another ten years and was completed in 1806. Thirteen years separate the first drawing from the final brushstroke.

Even then, the work nearly went unpaid. The Earl had died in 1803, and his heirs refused to pay for it. Camuccini sold it instead in 1807
