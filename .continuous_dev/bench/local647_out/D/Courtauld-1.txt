Stand directly in front of Van Gogh’s iconic Self-Portrait with Bandaged Ear, an 1889 oil painting by the Dutch Post-Impressionist artist. From this exact vantage point, look closely at the upper right background where a framed Japanese woodblock print hangs on the wall. That print is *Geishas in a Landscape*, made by Satō Torakiyo in the 1870s, which Van Gogh included to reflect his deep fascination with Japanese woodblock printing and its bold colors.

In the portrait, Vincent van Gogh portrays himself wearing a blue cap with black fur and a green overcoat, with a thick white bandage covering his ear and extending under his chin. The fur cap was a recent purchase, serving as a memory of the difficult working circumstances he encountered in January 1889 to keep his heavy bandage in place and protect him from the winter cold. Behind him, alongside the Japanese print, are an open window and a canvas on an easel with a few indistinguishable marks. Van Gogh applied thick impasto painting strokes, a technique where paint is laid thickly on a surface to show marks of the painting knife, giving the composition intense energy and passion. He was inspired in this method by Adolphe Monticelli, a French painter known for his heavily textured surfaces. 

This work marks a profound turning point in the artist's life, created shortly after a severe psychological break. Van Gogh had moved from Paris to Arles to establish a community of supportive artists called the Studio of the South, renting four rooms in The Yellow House and inviting Paul Gauguin to join him. On the evening of 23 December 1888, Gauguin threatened to leave, prompting Van Gogh to approach him with a razor before slicing off his own left ear later that night and bringing it to a local prostitute named Rachel, who became distressed and called the police to arrest him for lunacy. Because Van Gogh used a mirror to paint this self-portrait, the reflection reversed the image, making it appear as though his right ear is bandaged instead of his left. 

The painting captures a startling mix of vulnerability and resilient endurance, showing an artist confronting his own trauma while actively continuing to paint in the bitter winter cold. Samuel Courtauld purchased the painting in 1928, bringing it into the permanent collection of The Courtauld Gallery in London.
