*Sappho and Alcaeus* is an oil-on-panel painting measuring 66 by 122 centimetres, executed by the Dutch-British artist Lawrence Alma-Tadema in 1881. The work depicts a musical concert set in the late seventh century BC, showing the male poet Alcaeus of Mytilene playing the kithara, a large ancient Greek stringed instrument related to the lyre. Seated in the audience is the fellow poet Sappho from the island of Lesbos, accompanied by her female companions. Sappho leans forward to listen, resting her arm on a cushion that holds a laurel wreath, an ancient symbol of victory and poetic honor intended for the musician. The scene illustrates a literary passage written by the elegiac poet Hermesianax and recorded by the second-century author Athenaeus in his surviving work *Deipnosophistae*, which translates to *The Philosophers' Banquet*. In the background behind the marble tiers, the blue expanse of the Aegean Sea appears through a screen of trees. 

This painting marks a period when Lawrence Alma-Tadema, an artist celebrated in Victorian London for his meticulously researched depictions of classical antiquity, was at the height of his career, exploring how ancient poetry and archaeology could be fused into a single pictorial space. 

When first exhibited at London's Royal Academy in 1881, the painting was famously depicted in William Powell Frith's canvas *A Private View at the Royal Academy, 1881* on the far right, where it was being inspected by the Pre-Raphaelite painter John Everett Millais. Contemporary critics praised the technical skill of the piece, with the humor magazine *Punch* publishing a punning review that described the meticulous painting of the stone surfaces as "marbellous." 

The painting was acquired directly by William Thompson Walters of Baltimore, and upon his death in 1894, it passed to his son Henry Walters, who bequeathed the work to the Walters Art Museum upon his own death in 1931.
