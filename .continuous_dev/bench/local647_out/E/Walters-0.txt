Step directly in front of *Springtime (1872)*, roughly three to four feet back. From this vantage point, your eye catches the interplay of light and shadow across the fabric of the dress—a dappled, shifting effect that flattens into mere streaks of white and gray if you view it from an angle or from too far away.

The painting depicts Camille Doncieux, the artist’s first wife, sitting quietly in a garden under an arched canopy of lilacs. Claude Monet executed this work in oil on canvas, using a technique where sunlight peeking through branches is applied in broken patches of light directly onto the ground and across Camille’s muslin dress. This muslin—a lightweight, plain-woven cotton fabric—catches the fractured brightness, grounding the figure within the shifting atmosphere of their backyard. Unlike his later depictions that portrayed Camille as older and far less attractive, this canvas brings her face and form into close focus as an enchanting scene of domestic life. Monet chose to show this work at the second Impressionist group exhibition in 1876 at Paul Durand-Ruel’s gallery in Paris, where he showed eighteen paintings, six of which featured Camille; there, it was listed under the more generic title *Woman Reading*. 

Monet painted this piece in the spring of 1872, shortly after settling with his family late in 1871 in Argenteuil, a village northwest of Paris that served as a popular resort for city pleasure-seekers. Monet’s colleagues, such as Auguste Renoir and Édouard Manet—both key painters of modern life who also used Camille as a model—frequently joined him there, transforming the town into a primary hub of Impressionism. Camille possessed an unusual talent as a muse, having served as his mistress and model throughout the 1860s before they married in 1870. What is at stake in this image is Camille’s very visual legacy: Monet’s second wife, Alice Hoschedé, later ordered the complete destruction of all photographs and personal mementos from Camille’s life, meaning that her likeness survives almost entirely through Monet’s painted canvases.

The work conveys a stillness rooted in quiet domestic intimacy, contrasting the bright, fleeting outdoor light against Camille’s absorbed, sheltered concentration. 

The original painting is preserved today as part of the permanent collection of the Walters Art Museum. In 2012, weather-resistant vinyl reproductions of this piece were displayed outdoors across Baltimore, including at the Cylburn Arboretum, as part of the museum's "Off the Wall" initiative.
