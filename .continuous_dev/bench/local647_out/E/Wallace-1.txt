Beneath the paint layer, Poussin made notable pentimenti—alterations visible upon close inspection, including his decision to paint out a second, larger tree originally standing on the right between the dancers and Time. He executed this work between roughly 1634 and 1636 on commission for Giulio Rospigliosi, an intellectual, author, and later Pope Clement IX, who, according to the seventeenth-century biographer Gian Pietro Bellori, devised its intricate iconography. 

The composition shows four figures holding hands in a dance while Time, seated on the right as a winged elder, plays a lyre. They move in the early morning beneath Aurora, the goddess of dawn, who flies before Apollo's sun chariot accompanied by the Hours, with Apollo holding a ring representing the Zodiac. Bellori explained the four dancers as a continuous cycle of human fate: starting at the back, Poverty leads to Labour, Labour brings Riches, and Riches produces Pleasure or Luxury, which inevitably falls back into Poverty. Art historian Anthony Blunt identified the figure at the rear as male, whom the museum describes as Bacchus, following an account by Boitet de Frauville where Jupiter gifted wine to humanity to soften the misery of mortal life. 

Yet this meaning was contested. Scholar Malcolm Bull argues Poussin originally followed the late Greek poet Nonnus and the Renaissance writer Pierre de Ronsard to paint the Four Seasons directly: Spring wreathed with roses on the left, wind-cropped Autumn at the back, shadowed Winter with bound hair, and Summer in white with ears of braided corn at the front. In this reading, Bacchus appears instead on the far left as a stone herm showing both his young and old faces, leaving Rospigliosi to apply the moral cycle of wealth and ruin only during or after the painting's completion. 

Nicolas Poussin spent these middle years establishing his classical style in Rome, where he balanced intellectual commissions with strict allegories. What was at stake here was the artist’s own control over his imagery, navigating the competing demands of classical poetry and a patron whose complex philosophical readings risked overwriting the painter's original design. 

The painting stands out in the Wallace Collection’s historic galleries, giving its title to the twentieth-century novel cycle by Anthony Powell, who drew on this exact canvas to open his narrative.
