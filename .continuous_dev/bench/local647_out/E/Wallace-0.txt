Stand roughly four feet away and slightly to the left of *The Laughing Cavalier*. From this specific angle, you can see how the sitter’s gaze follows you, created because the painter depicted him looking directly out along the artist's original line of sight on a flat plane. You will also see how the upturned tips of his moustache visually exaggerate what is actually an enigmatic, subtle smile.

Painted in oil on a canvas measuring 83 by 67.3 centimetres, the portrait depicts an unidentified twenty-six-year-old man, a fact recorded in the Latin inscription at the upper right reading *Æ'TA SVÆ 26/Aº1624*, meaning he was aged twenty-six in the year 1624. Art historian Pieter Biesboer suggests the sitter might be the Dutch cloth merchant Tieleman Roosterman, though nineteenth-century titles assumed he was a military officer from one of the civic militias that guarded Dutch towns. Frans Hals chose a low viewpoint and a turning pose specifically to thrust the man's left arm forward, turning his lavish silk doublet into the main event. What looks like painstaking, solid needlework from a distance breaks down under close inspection into long, rapid brushstrokes. The embroidery carries specific coded motifs: bees and flaming cornucopiae—the classical horn of plenty—represent the pleasures and pains of love, an obelisk symbolizes strength, and the winged cap and caduceus of Mercury, the Roman god of commerce, signify fortune. Art historian Seymour Slive described the work as one of the most brilliant of all Baroque portraits. 

Hals painted this in 1624, during the Dutch Golden Age when a newly prosperous mercantile class was commissioning private likenesses. At that time, commissioned formal portraits almost never showed adults smiling, reserving grins for low-life tavern scenes or *tronies*—character studies of exaggerated facial expressions. Hals broke that convention across his career, capturing fleeting human expressions and informal postures to inject spontaneous life into what was usually a stiff, formal market. 

What a viewer confronts is not the hearty laughter invented by Victorian journalists, but a calculating, sideways smirk backed by extravagant wealth, leaving the impression of an encounter with a man wholly confident in his own social standing.

The 4th Marquess of Hertford secured the canvas for the Wallace Collection at a Paris auction in 1865, outbidding Baron James de Rothschild at more than six times the pre-sale estimate. Alongside Fragonard's *The Swing*, it stands today as one of the defining masterworks of the museum.
