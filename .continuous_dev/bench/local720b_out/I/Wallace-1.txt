Stand a few steps back from the canvas, near the centre of the gallery, so that the whole picture sits in your field of view. From here you can take in the ring of dancers and the sky above them in a single glance. Then let your eye travel upward from the dancers to the band of sunrise behind them. That vertical reading is the key to this painting.

A Dance to the Music of Time shows four figures holding hands in a circle, turning in rhythm. At the right sits Time, a winged, naked, grey-bearded old man, playing a lyre. Above and behind, the sky holds a second procession. Aurora, goddess of the dawn, goes ahead of the chariot of Apollo, the sun god, with the Hours moving alongside him. Apollo holds a ring that stands for the Zodiac, the band of constellations the sun appears to cross over a year. The earthly dance and the heavenly procession are one clock in two registers, the dancers turning below as the sun climbs above.

Who the dancers are has never been settled. According to the seventeenth-century biographer Gian Pietro Bellori, the patron, Giulio Rospigliosi, dictated the subject. He was a cultivated writer with a taste for allegory, and he later became Pope Clement IX. In Bellori's account the four are Poverty, Labour, Riches, and Pleasure or Luxury, a cycle in which Pleasure leads back to Poverty again. Poussin's friend and biographer André Félibien told it the same way, except that he named the last figure Pleasure where Bellori said Luxury. Read as the Four Seasons, Poverty is Autumn and Labour is Winter. The figure at the back, seen mostly from behind, is now generally accepted as male, which is unusual for a group of Seasons, and the museum describes him as Bacchus, god of wine. The story behind that reading is that mortals and the Seasons complained of life's miseries, and Jupiter answered by giving the world Bacchus and his wine as compensation.

Not everyone accepts that this was the original plan. The scholar Malcolm Bull traces the imagery to the late Greek poet Nonnus, as echoed in a French hymn to autumn by Pierre de Ronsard. In that source, Spring wears roses in her hair, Autumn has olive branches on her brow, Winter has bound hair and a shadowed face, and Summer wears white with ears of corn braided into her hair. Bull suggests Rospigliosi invented the Poverty-and-Riches interpretation during or after the painting's completion. Another scholar, Ingamells, thinks Poussin was not unduly concerned with identifying each figure precisely. Notice the stone herma, a pillar topped with a head, at the left. Its two faces, one young and one old, show Bacchus in his double aspect.

Look closely at the surface and you will find the traces of Poussin changing his mind. These are called pentimenti, the visible remnants of earlier decisions. Between Winter and Time on the right, he painted out a second, larger tree. The painting is in generally good condition, but a large L-shaped tear runs right through the central group of dancers. It was repaired and retouched, so the wound is mended but part of that area is no longer purely Poussin's hand.

Nicolas Poussin was a French painter who made his career in Rome. This canvas dates from around 1634 to 1636, when he was working there on commissions from learned patrons who wanted paintings that rewarded close reading. This was a commission from an intellectual, built on a program supplied by the patron, and Poussin turned it into a picture where the argument is carried by gesture and rhythm. The circle has no leader and no end. Each dancer is joined to the next, and the chain pulls toward the viewer while the musician at the edge keeps time.

The title is a later arrival. It first appears in a Wallace Collection catalogue of 1913. Earlier, the picture went by names referring to the Four Seasons, and an 1845 sale called it The Dance of the Seasons, or the Image of Human Life. The novelist Anthony Powell borrowed the phrase for his twelve-novel cycle, and in its opening pages his narrator, Nicholas Jenkins, sees the picture in a fire and thinks of mortality, of people moving hand in hand in intricate measure.

The dancers do not look at each other or at Time. They face outward, hands locked, stepping to a tune they cannot stop, while the sun behind them is already rising.
