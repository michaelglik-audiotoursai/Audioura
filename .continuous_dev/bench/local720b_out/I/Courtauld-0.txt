Stand directly in front of Manet's A Bar at the Folies-Bergère, about two metres back, with the barmaid at eye level. From here you can see her face, the long gold frame of the mirror behind her, and the reflected crowd all at once. Then take a few steps to the right and look at the man on the far right edge. Moving shifts how the reflection reads, and that is the key to the whole picture.

The painting is oil on canvas, about four and a quarter feet wide, and its central trick is a mirror running the full length of the picture. In front of that mirror stands a young woman named Suzon, a real barmaid at the Folies-Bergère, the Paris music hall and nightclub where this scene is set. Manet did not paint her on location. He posed her in his studio, which is why her face has the fixed, almost sculpted calm of someone holding still for a long sitting.

Look at the reflection. Her back appears at the far right, bent toward a man in a top hat as if deep in conversation. Yet the woman facing you stands upright at the centre, withdrawn and distant. The two views do not match, and critics accused Manet of botching his perspective from the first reviews. In 2000, the art historian Malcolm Park built a physical reconstruction of the scene and showed the geometry holds. The man is not standing in front of her at all. He stands to the left, outside the painter's field of vision, and looks away. The conversation we assume is an optical trick. A preliminary study placed her off to the right, and Manet moved her to the centre of the canvas while leaving her reflection where it was. That choice costs the viewer certainty. You cannot say where you are standing.

The still life on the marble counter carries meaning too. The bowl of oranges, according to the art historian Larry Ligo, is a code Manet habitually used to mark a woman as a prostitute. The scholar T. J. Clark argues she is shown as both a salesperson and a commodity, something to be bought along with a drink. Now look to the upper left corner, where a pair of small green feet dangle. They belong to a trapeze artist performing above the crowd, and Manet gives them no more space than a footnote. The mirror also recalls Las Meninas, the court painting by Diego Velázquez, an artist Manet admired. Velázquez used a mirror to complicate who is watching whom, and the philosopher Michel Foucault's discussion of that painting in The Order of Things in 1966 reopened the comparison for modern readers.

This was Manet's last major work. He painted it in 1882 and showed it at the Paris Salon that year, the official annual exhibition. He died in 1883, so the picture belongs to the final months of a life spent pushing realism, the commitment to showing contemporary Paris as it really looked. It originally belonged to his close friend, the composer Emmanuel Chabrier, and hung over his piano.

At the Courtauld, the painting came with Samuel Courtauld's collection, which also included a version of the Déjeuner sur l'Herbe and a group of eight major works by Cézanne. It now hangs in the Great Room alongside Impressionist and Post-Impressionist masterpieces.
