Welcome to the Gallery at Somerset House. You are about to stand before Van Gogh's iconic Self-Portrait with Bandaged Ear. Stand directly in front of the canvas, about two metres back, and look first at the left side of the picture, where the blue fur cap meets the window frame behind the artist's head. From this distance the thick, ridged paint surface resolves into shapes, and you can see how the painting is built.

The work shows Van Gogh in a blue cap with black fur and a green overcoat, his head turned toward us. A white bandage wraps over his head and runs under his chin, covering where his left ear should be. Because he painted the picture looking into a mirror, the bandaged ear appears on the right side of the image, which is why it seems the wrong ear is injured. Behind him hangs an open window, and on the wall is a Japanese woodblock print, Geishas in a Landscape, made by Satō Torakiyo in the 1870s. The print's bold outlines and flat areas of colour show how closely Van Gogh was studying Japanese art when he made this work.

Look at the paint itself. Van Gogh used impasto, a technique of laying paint on so thickly that the brushstrokes and the marks of the knife stand up off the canvas. He admired this method in the work of Adolphe Monticelli, the French painter whose thick surfaces Van Gogh had written about to his brother Theo in 1888. In the background, the strokes are short and directional, which gives the wall and window a restless energy. The cap and coat are built in a similar way, so the surface seems to vibrate under your eyes.

The fur cap has a practical story behind it. Van Gogh had recently bought it to hold his heavy bandage in place and to keep out the January cold. He painted it as a record of the hard conditions in which he was working.

This portrait was made in January 1889, only weeks after the events of 23 December 1888. Van Gogh had moved from Paris to Arles in the south of France, hoping to found a community of artists he called the Studio of the South. He invited Paul Gauguin to join him in the Yellow House he rented there. After a bitter quarrel, Gauguin threatened to leave, and Van Gogh cut off part of his own left ear. He survived, and in the weeks that followed he turned to self-portraits, painting himself again and again while he recovered. This picture, which he described to Theo in a letter of 17 January 1889, is one of the most direct records of that period.

The painting is now in the Courtauld collection. It was in the possession of the colourman Julien Tanguy at the time of Van Gogh's death, and Samuel Courtauld bought it in 1928. Its presence here places it among the Gallery's celebrated Impressionist and Post-Impressionist paintings, alongside Manet's A Bar at the Folies-Bergère.

The painting reveals a man looking steadily at himself while everything around him is unstable. The calm, direct gaze contrasts with the thick, agitated paint that surrounds his face, and that contrast is what holds the viewer.
