*The Laughing Cavalier* is a portrait painted in 1624. The sitter wears a richly embroidered silk costume, and close inspection reveals long, quick brushstrokes working across it. Hals built the image through speed rather than polish. The turning pose and low viewpoint push the embroidered sleeve and lace cuff toward you. The embroidery itself carries emblems. Bees, arrows, flaming cornucopiae, lovers' knots and tongues of fire signify the pleasures and pains of love. An obelisk or pyramid stands for strength, and Mercury's cap and caduceus for fortune. Art historian Seymour Slive called it "one of the most brilliant of all Baroque portraits."

The title is not Hals's. Victorian viewers and the press gave it the name after the painting was shown in 1872 at Bethnal Green, a loan exhibition deliberately sited away from the West End to reach working-class audiences. The sitter is not laughing. He has an enigmatic smile, and the upturned moustache amplifies it. Commissioned portraits rarely showed adults smiling before the late eighteenth century, though Hals often broke that rule, which is part of why his sitters seem to move.

Frans Hals was working in Haarlem in the Dutch Golden Age, painting militia companies, civic groups and individual sitters for a prosperous market. He painted this man at twenty-six, the age inscribed in Latin across the top right corner: *aetatis suae 26, anno 1624*. The sitter's identity is unknown. Nineteenth-century titles mostly called him a military officer, but he may well have been a wealthy civilian. Art historian Pieter Biesboer has suggested the cloth merchant Tieleman Roosterman, who was also painted by Hals.

The painting's own story is a long one. It was sold at The Hague in 1770, bought in 1822 by the Franco-Swiss banker Comte de Pourtalès-Gorgier, and then bid for in Paris in 1865 by the 4th Marquess of Hertford, who paid more than six times the sale estimate.
