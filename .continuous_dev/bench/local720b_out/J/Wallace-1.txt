# A Dance to the Music of Time: Audio Description

## Orientation

Stand a few paces back from *A Dance to the Music of Time*, square to the centre, so the ring of dancers and the sky above them fit in a single view. From there the painting reads as two linked bands of movement: four figures turning on the ground, and a sun-god's chariot sweeping across the dawn sky. Then step closer to the left edge. A small stone pillar topped with a carved head stands there, easily lost from a distance, and it holds one of the keys to what the picture means.

## The Work

Four figures hold hands and dance in a ring, facing outward. On the right, Time, a winged, naked old man, plays a lyre to set their rhythm. Above them it is early morning. Aurora, goddess of the dawn, flies ahead of the chariot of Apollo, the sun-god. Apollo is accompanied by the Hours, the goddesses of time's divisions, and he holds a ring representing the Zodiac, the circle of constellations that marks the passing year. The whole picture is built from circles: the dancers' ring, the Zodiac ring, and the endless turn of time.

Nicolas Poussin painted it between about 1634 and 1636 for Giulio Rospigliosi, a churchman who later became Pope Clement IX. According to Gian Pietro Bellori, an early writer on Poussin, Rospigliosi dictated the painting's detailed symbolism himself. In that reading, the four dancers form a cycle of human fortune. The figure at the back, seen mostly from behind, is Poverty. Poverty leads to Labour, Labour to Riches, and Riches to Pleasure or Luxury, which leads back to Poverty again.

The dancers can also be read as the Four Seasons. The art historian Anthony Blunt proposed that Autumn, the figure at the back, is unusually male. That view is now widely accepted, and the Wallace Collection identifies him as Bacchus, the god of wine. The idea follows a story in which Jupiter, king of the gods, gave mortals Bacchus and his wine to compensate them for their hard lives. André Félibien, Poussin's friend and biographer, explained the picture in much the same terms.

The scholar Malcolm Bull disagrees, at least about what was originally intended. He traces the imagery to Nonnus, a Late Greek poet, whose seasons Poussin seems to follow closely. In that reading, Spring is on the left with roses in her hair. Autumn is at the back, his hair cropped by the winds but wreathed with olive. Winter has bound hair and a shadowed face, and Summer is in front, dressed in white with ears of corn braided into her hair. In Bull's account, Bacchus appears instead in that small pillar at the left. It shows him in his double aspect, as both a young figure and an old one. Bull suggests Rospigliosi, an author with a taste for allegory, added the moral interpretation during or after the painting's completion. Another scholar, Ingamells, concluded that Poussin was not unduly concerned with naming each figure precisely.

The canvas shows signs of second thoughts. Poussin made several changes as he worked, known as pentimenti, which are traces of earlier painting later covered over. The largest was a second, bigger tree on the right, between Winter and Time, which he painted out. Removing it opened the gap through which the old man's music reaches the dancers.

The painting has also been damaged. A large L-shaped tear once ran right through the central group of dancers. It has been repaired and retouched, and the picture is otherwise in generally good condition. The ring held together, though the canvas beneath it once split.

The painting's famous title is relatively recent. It first appears in a Wallace Collection catalogue of 1913. Earlier titles referred to the Four Seasons, and at an 1845 sale it was called *The Dance of the Seasons, or the Image of Human Life*. The 1913 title later gave its name to Anthony Powell's novel cycle. In the opening pages of the first book, the narrator recalls Poussin's Seasons, "hand in hand and facing outward," and is led to thoughts of mortality.

## The Artist

Poussin made this work as a commission from a powerful patron who wanted to shape its meaning himself. The painting sits between the two men's intentions: Rospigliosi's moral allegory and Poussin's own response to ancient poetry. Félibien, who knew Poussin personally, accepted the patron's reading. Modern scholars are still arguing over how much of it the painter shared.

## What You Notice

Because the dancers face outward, none of them looks at the others, and none can see who leads. Time keeps playing while the sun rises overhead, and the dance continues regardless of who is rich or poor at any given turn.
