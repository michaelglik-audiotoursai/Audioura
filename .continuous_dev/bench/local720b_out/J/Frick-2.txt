# Portrait of Comtesse d'Haussonville
### Jean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres, 1845

**Orientation**

Begin a few steps back from *Portrait of Comtesse d'Haussonville*, where the woman, the dresser and the mirror read as one cool field of pale blue, grey and gold. Then move close and look past her shoulder into the mirror. There you'll find what the front view hides: the back of her head, with a crimson ribbon tied into her parted hair. It is the only strong warm note in the painting, and Ingres placed it where you can see it only in reflection.

**The Work**

Louise de Broglie, the Comtesse d'Haussonville, was twenty-seven when Ingres painted her in oil on canvas. She faces you fully, with a demure expression. Her left index finger is raised coyly to her mouth. Her right arm curls across her body, sinuous and longer than any real arm could be.

That arm is deliberate. Ingres stretched the anatomy to make the line of the body flow, choosing grace of contour over accuracy. The finger and the arm are the core of the composition. They appear in every version he attempted.

The mirror is a device he had used once before, in his 1814 *Portrait of Madame de Senonnes*, where a sitter is also doubled by a glass behind her. Here it shows you two views of the same woman at once.

On the dresser in front of the mirror are her writing materials, along with pots, flowers and a lavishly decorated oriental vase. The writing materials suit the sitter. Highly educated, she later became an essayist and biographer, and wrote historical romances based on the lives of figures including Lord Byron, the English Romantic poet.

Look at the satin of her dress. It is heavily folded, cold and grey-blue, and Ingres painted it in the same hue as her eyes. The dress and the gaze are tied together by a single colour.

This canvas cost years of effort. Two or three years earlier, Ingres made a black chalk drawing of her and began a first oil version. In that version the pose was reversed and there was no mirror. He worked slowly and demanded long sittings, and she found them tedious. At one point she complained that "for the last nine days Ingres has been painting on one of the hands." Then she became pregnant with her third child and could no longer pose. That 1842 painting was abandoned and remains unfinished. The version in front of you is the second attempt, signed and dated by Ingres at the lower left.

Her family was delighted with it. A visitor named Monsieur Thiers came to see it with the sitter and teased her more than once: "M. Ingres must be in love with you to have painted you that way."

In 1846 the painting was shown in Paris alongside two of Ingres's images of women, the *Grande Odalisque* and *Odalisque with Slave*. The poet Charles Baudelaire praised all three for their "voluptuousness," meaning their sensual richness. Afterwards he called Ingres the quintessential painter of women. Critics have often compared the intensity of her gaze to Ingres's later *Portrait of Madame Moitessier*.

**The Artist**

By 1845 Ingres was at the height of his fame and much in demand as a portraitist. He resented it. His ambition was history painting: large scenes from myth and the past, which he considered the highest form of art. To his frustration, those scenes paid far less than portraits. That year he agreed to only two portrait commissions, this one and the *Portrait of Baronne de Rothschild*.

Even after the family's praise, he insisted he had failed. "I do not feel that I have conveyed all the graces of that charming model," he wrote. The irony is that his portraits are what he is best known for today, and this is one of them.

**The Emotional Reading**

The painting holds two things in tension. The surface is cool, with icy satin and a pale, steady gaze. Yet the gesture is intimate, a finger hovering at her lips. Behind her, the small red ribbon in the mirror is the one detail Ingres seems to have kept for those who look closely.
