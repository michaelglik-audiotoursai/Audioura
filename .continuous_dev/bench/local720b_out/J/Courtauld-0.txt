# Manet's A Bar at the Folies-Bergère

**Where to stand**

Stand slightly to the right of the centre of Manet's A Bar at the Folies-Bergère, and step a little closer than feels natural. This is not an arbitrary spot. In 2000, the art historian Malcolm Park staged a physical reconstruction of the scene and showed that its puzzling geometry works only from this position: to the right, and nearer the bar than the gentleman whose reflection appears at the painting's right edge. From dead centre, the mirror seems to lie. From here, the picture starts to make sense.

**The work**

A young woman stands behind a marble bar at the Folies-Bergère, a Parisian nightclub and music hall. She faces you directly, hands resting on the counter. In front of her are bottles of champagne, liqueur and beer, a glass holding two flowers, and a dish of oranges. Behind her stretches the gold frame of an enormous mirror. It runs the full length of this oil on canvas, which is about four and a quarter feet wide. Almost everything else you see, the glittering chandeliers, the crowded balcony and the blur of hats and faces, is reflection.

That mirror is the painting's central compositional choice, and the source of a long argument. From the earliest reviews, critics accused Manet of not understanding perspective. In the mirror, the barmaid's reflection appears far off to the right, leaning towards a top-hatted man. Yet the woman herself stands centred, upright and alone. A preliminary study shows that Manet first placed her off to the right. In the finished canvas he moved her to the centre but deliberately left her reflection where it was. The art historian Jeffrey Meyers sums up the effect: in the mirror she seems engaged with a customer, while face on she is "self-protectively withdrawn and remote."

Park's reconstruction showed that the apparent conversation is an optical trick. The man stands outside the painter's field of vision, to the left, and he is looking away from her. Manet was not making mistakes. He was quietly moving the viewer off-centre, which breaks with the fixed central viewpoint that perspective painting normally assumes.

The woman was a real person, known as Suzon, who worked at the Folies-Bergère in the early 1880s. Manet posed her in his studio. The details around her point to class and commerce. The art historian Larry Ligo argues that Manet habitually used oranges to signal prostitution. T. J. Clark, a leading historian of modern Parisian painting, reads her as both "a salesperson and a commodity," something to be bought along with a drink. In the upper left corner, a pair of small green-slippered feet dangle into the picture. They belong to a trapeze artist performing above the audience, and nobody in the crowd appears to be watching.

Scholars have also linked the mirror to *Las Meninas*, the painting by the seventeenth-century Spanish court painter Diego Velázquez, whom Manet admired, in which a mirror reveals figures standing where the viewer stands. The philosopher Michel Foucault wrote about that kind of mirror play in his 1966 book *The Order of Things*, and it opened a long line of interpretation. Another philosopher, Maurice Merleau-Ponty, described the mirror as an instrument that changes "me into others, and others into me." In this painting, you occupy the customer's place.

**The artist**

Édouard Manet painted this in 1882 and showed it at the Paris Salon, the official state exhibition, that same year. It is considered his last major work. He died the following year, in 1883. At the end of his career he was still doing what he had always done, which was to paint modern Paris as it actually looked. The first owner was the composer Emmanuel Chabrier, a close friend of Manet, who hung it above his piano.

**In this collection**

The painting came to The Courtauld through the collection of Samuel Courtauld, which also included a version of Manet's *Déjeuner sur l'Herbe*. It hangs in the LVMH Great Room alongside works by Degas, Monet and Renoir.

**What stays with you**

The crowd is glittering and loud, yet the woman at its centre is utterly still, and her eyes do not meet yours. Manet's final great picture places the viewer at the bar as the customer, and he keeps the barmaid's attention just out of reach.
