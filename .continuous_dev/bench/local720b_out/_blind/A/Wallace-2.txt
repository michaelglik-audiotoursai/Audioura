Stand a few steps back from Madame de Pompadour, near the centre of the canvas, so you take in the whole figure at once. From there the painting reads as a single sweep of colour and fabric, and you can see how the skirt, the sitter, and the setting are arranged to move your eye from the hem upward to her face.

This is an oil painting on canvas, made in 1759 by the French Rococo painter François Boucher. Rococo is the playful, ornamental style that flourished in eighteenth-century France, favouring soft colour, curving lines, and scenes of leisure over the weight of earlier court art. It was the last of seven portraits Boucher painted of Madame de Pompadour. That number matters. He returned to the same sitter again and again, which means this canvas is the end of a long visual conversation between painter and subject, not a single commission.

The sitter was one of the most powerful women in France, the official mistress of King Louis XV, a role that gave her real influence at court as well as social standing. A portrait of her was never only a likeness. It was a statement of status, taste, and presence, meant to be seen by the court.

Boucher's handling of paint is the clue to how he achieves that. The Rococo habit of rendering fabric as a landscape of its own is on full display here: surfaces of silk and ribbon are built from small, light touches that catch the eye before the face does. The effect is deliberate. The viewer is invited to read wealth and refinement in the materials first, and the woman second.

The painting was first shown at the Château de Versailles, the royal palace outside Paris where the court lived, so its earliest audience was the very society that Pompadour moved within. It later passed to her brother, a fact that follows the portrait out of the palace and into private family hands.

Boucher was among the leading painters of the French court at this point in his career. By 1759 he had spent years painting this one woman, and this canvas closes that series. I have no documented account of his state of mind or the circumstances of this final sitting, so what the painting tells us is limited to what is on the canvas itself.

Look at how much of the picture is given over to dress and surface, and how the sitter's face sits within it rather than dominating it. That balance suggests a woman presented as inseparable from the world of luxury around her, composed and fully aware of being looked at.

The Wallace Collection holds a great deal of eighteenth-century French art, and this portrait belongs among it as a record of the court style that Boucher helped define.
