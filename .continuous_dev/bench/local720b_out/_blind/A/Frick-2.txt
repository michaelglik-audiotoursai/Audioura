Stand directly in front of Portrait of Comtesse d'Haussonville, about six feet back and level with the sitter's face. From here the painting works as Ingres intended. Her gaze meets yours head-on, and the mirror behind her, which shows the back of her head and the line of her neck, falls into view. Step off to one side and you lose that confrontation.

Louise de Broglie was twenty-seven when Ingres finished this canvas in 1845. She stands fully frontal in a heavily folded satin dress, a cold grey-blue painted in the same hue as her eyes. Her hair is parted, with a crimson ribbon at the back. The palette is restrained: pale blue, grey, brown, gold and white. Look at her left hand. The index finger rests lightly against her mouth, a coy, half-thoughtful gesture, and it is the motif Ingres kept through every version of this portrait. Now follow her right arm. It is unnaturally long and sinuous, bending in a way no real arm would. Ingres chose elegance of line over anatomical accuracy, a habit critics had noted in his work since his Grande Odalisque of 1814.

Behind her is the mirror, a device Ingres first used in his 1814 portrait of Madame de Senonnes. The mirror lets him show the sitter from two angles at once. Beside it, a dresser holds writing materials, small pots, flowers, and a lavishly decorated oriental vase. The writing materials suit her. Louise was highly educated and later became an essayist and biographer, and she published historical romance novels based on the lives of Lord Byron, the English poet, Robert Emmett, the Irish rebel leader, and Margaret of Valois, a sixteenth-century French queen.

Ingres had tried this portrait once before. Two or three years earlier he made a black chalk sketch and started an oil version, but that canvas excluded the mirror and reversed the pose. The sittings were long and slow, and Louise found them wearisome. She complained that for nine days he had been painting on a single hand. Then she became pregnant with her third child and could no longer stand for him. The 1842 painting was abandoned, unfinished. So the work in front of you is a second attempt, begun after the first had stalled on the sitter's own exhaustion.

Ingres took this commission reluctantly. By 1845 his fame was at its height and portraits were in demand, but he considered them a distraction from history painting, the grand mythological and classical scenes he believed were the true calling of an artist. History painting paid far worse. He committed to only two portraits at the time, this one and the Baronne de Rothschild. Today it is for portraits like these that he is best known.

He was not satisfied. He wrote that Louise's family, her friends, and above all her father, the duc de Broglie, were delighted. The statesman Adolphe Thiers visited and told her, "M. Ingres must be in love with you to have painted you that way." Ingres replied that none of this made him proud, and that he had not conveyed all the graces of his charming model. The painting is signed and dated at the lower left.

In 1846 it was shown at the Bonne-Nouvelle exhibition beside the Grande Odalisque and the Odalisque with Slave. The poet Charles Baudelaire praised all three for their voluptuousness and called Ingres the quintessential painter of women. The intensity of the sitter's look has often been compared to that of his later portrait of Madame Moitessier.

Notice how little the satin moves. It falls in stiff, sculptural folds, and her gaze is demure yet direct. The cool blue of the dress and the cool blue of her eyes are the same colour, and that match is what holds her so still.

The painting hangs at the Frick, a museum known for its portraiture, which this spring is giving a whole exhibition to Thomas Gainsborough, the English painter whose portraits were shaped by fashion. Ingres paints a different world, but he too treats clothing as part of the sitter's presence. Here the dress is the same blue as her eyes.
