**Audio description: The Death of Caesar, Vincenzo Camuccini, 1806**

**Orientation**

Stand a few paces back from The Death of Caesar and let your eye settle on the center of the canvas, where Caesar is going down. From here you can take in the whole ring of senators at once. Close up, you read individual faces. From a distance, you see what the painter built: a crowd arranged like a stage, every figure turned toward one falling man.

**The Work**

The Death of Caesar shows the assassination of Julius Caesar, the Roman general and dictator, stabbed by a group of senators in 44 BC. Camuccini painted it in 1806, and it is an oil painting in the Neoclassical manner. That means he composed it the way an ancient relief sculpture would be composed, with the figures arranged across the front of the picture in clear, balanced groups rather than in swirling depth.

The key fact about how it was made is that it was made twice. Camuccini completed a cartoon, a full-size preparatory drawing, in 1793. Roman art critics received it favorably. But when he painted a first version in 1796, it was less well received. So he destroyed it and started again from scratch. The painting you see is the second attempt, finished in 1806. Ten years separate the cartoon from the final canvas, and one entire painting was sacrificed along the way. That severity is part of the picture's character: nothing here is casual.

Its fate was also uncertain. It was commissioned in 1793 by Frederick Hervey, the 4th Earl of Bristol, an English aristocrat and collector. But the Earl died in 1803, and his heirs refused to pay. Camuccini was left with a finished masterpiece and no buyer. In 1807 he sold it to Joachim Murat, the French marshal who ruled Naples under Napoleon. After Murat's fall it passed to Ferdinand II of the Two Sicilies, who hung it in the royal palace in Naples, and in 1864 it moved to its present home in the National Museum of Capodimonte.

**The Artist**

Vincenzo Camuccini was working in Rome, the center of Neoclassicism, the movement that turned to ancient Greece and Rome for its subjects and its discipline. By 1793 he was already trusted by the Earl of Bristol, for whom he had made a copy of Raphael's Deposition, a famous Renaissance painting of Christ being lowered from the cross. Copying Raphael was training in order and clarity, and you can feel it here. The Caesar commission was his chance to prove he could do more than copy a master. He could build a great history painting of his own, and he was willing to destroy a canvas that fell short of that.

**The Emotional Reading**

The controlled arrangement is what makes the scene unsettling. The violence is staged with the calm of a classical frieze, so the stillness around Caesar feels colder than chaos would. Knowing the painter burned his own first attempt, you sense a man unwilling to settle for anything less than exactness.
