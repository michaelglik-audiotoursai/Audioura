You are standing before the Portrait of Sir Thomas More, an oak panel painting created in 1527 by Hans Holbein the Younger, here at the Frick Collection in New York. Position yourself close enough to see the delicate brushwork that captures the textures of More’s clothing and the subtle details of his face; from this vantage point, you can also notice the cord tied in a loose Franciscan knot near the upper right, a detail easily missed from afar that reveals More’s spiritual devotion.

This portrait depicts Sir Thomas More, the renowned English statesman and humanist, shown in a three-quarter right half-profile. He holds a book in his hands, symbolizing his learned nature and his commitment to scholarship. More wears a fur-lined coat made of sumptuous fabrics: black satin and rich red velvet, materials that signify his high social status and wealth. Around his neck hangs the Tudor Collar of Esses, a livery chain adorned with Tudor roses, which marks his loyalty and service to the Tudor monarchy. On his left index finger, a wedding ring set with a stone is visible, indicating his marital status. Holbein’s precise use of oil paint on the oak panel allows for an extraordinary rendering of textures—from the softness of the fur to the sheen of satin—showing his mastery of capturing both material richness and individual character. The painting was made during Holbein’s stay in London starting in 1526, at a time when he was establishing his reputation as a portraitist for England’s elite. Scholars have noted the work’s blend of naturalism and symbolism, highlighting More’s intellectual and spiritual identity through both his expression and the carefully chosen objects and garments.

Hans Holbein the Younger was a German artist and printmaker whose career reached a turning point with his move to London. Recommended to Thomas More by the Dutch humanist Desiderius Erasmus, Holbein sought to align himself with humanist circles and powerful patrons. This portrait captures Holbein’s skill in combining Northern European detail with the Renaissance ideal of portraying prominent individuals not only as they appear but as they embody certain virtues and roles in society. At this moment, Holbein was building connections that would soon lead to his position as court painter to Henry VIII.

The emotional impact of the portrait lies in its quiet dignity and intellectual presence. More’s calm, thoughtful gaze and the subtle spiritual symbols invite the viewer to recognize him as a man deeply committed to faith, learning, and public service—qualities that defined his life and would later lead to his martyrdom.

The Frick Collection acquired this portrait as part of its distinguished holdings of Renaissance art, enhancing its focus on European masterpieces that illuminate the intersection of art, history, and culture.
