You are standing in front of Édouard Manet’s A Bar at the Folies-Bergère, positioned to the right side of the painting to fully appreciate its complex use of perspective and reflection—details that would be lost from a direct, centered view. From this vantage, the unusual optical arrangement becomes legible: the barmaid faces you, yet her reflection in the mirror behind her shifts to the right, revealing a scene that contradicts ordinary expectations of mirror perspective.

This work, painted in oil on canvas in 1882, is Manet’s last major painting and captures a bustling moment in the Folies Bergère nightclub in Paris. The central figure, a barmaid named Suzon, stands before a large mirror framed in gold, which stretches across the back of the bar. The mirror shows her reflection engaging with a customer who actually stands outside the painter’s field of vision, to the left, and looks away from her, an arrangement confirmed by later reconstructions of the scene. This deliberate play on perspective challenges traditional compositional rules and invites viewers to question what is real versus reflected. Manet includes subtle details that hint at the barmaid’s social role, such as a dish of oranges in the foreground—a symbol he often associated with prostitution—and green feet above, belonging to a trapeze artist performing overhead. The painting is celebrated for its realistic portrayal of contemporary Parisian nightlife, while also echoing the complexity of Velázquez’s Las Meninas in its use of mirrors to explore perception and reality.

Édouard Manet created this painting at a pivotal moment near the end of his life, in 1882, when he was deeply engaged with modern life and experimenting with new ways to depict it. Having already established himself as a key figure in the transition from Realism to Impressionism, Manet used this work to push the boundaries of visual representation and social commentary. His friendship with composer Emmanuel Chabrier, who originally owned the painting, reflects Manet’s connection to the vibrant cultural circles of Paris at the time.

The emotional atmosphere of the painting is one of subtle detachment and complexity. The barmaid’s expression and posture convey a sense of isolation despite the lively environment, embodying the dual role of being both a service provider and an object of desire. This tension between appearance and reality, presence and reflection, creates a quietly unsettling mood that invites close observation.

The Courtauld Gallery in London, founded and enriched by the collector Samuel Courtauld, holds this painting within its renowned collection of Impressionist and Post-Impressionist masterpieces. Courtauld’s collection also includes a version of Manet’s Déjeuner sur l'Herbe, Renoir’s La Loge, landscapes by Monet and Pissarro, a ballet scene by Degas, and eight major works by Cézanne, making it one of the most significant holdings of modern French art in the UK. A Bar at the Folies-Bergère stands as a highlight of this collection, displayed alongside these other defining works, each contributing to a rich narrative of late 19th-century art.
