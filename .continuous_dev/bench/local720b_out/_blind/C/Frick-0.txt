Stand at the far right of the Vermeer room, at the edge of the wall where the light from the windows falls across the floor, and look at the woman's face first, then at the officer's hat. From this angle the painting's diagonal opens up: the table, the map, and the figures lock into a single line of depth. Stand closer and the composition flattens into a row of objects; here you can read how the officer's bulk pushes the eye back toward her.

Officer and Laughing Girl, also known as Officer With a Laughing Girl, or in Dutch De Soldaat en het Lachende Meisje, is an oil painting on canvas by Johannes Vermeer, made around 1657. It measures 50.5 by 46 centimeters, a modest size that asks you to come close. The main subject is a woman in a yellow bodice, her face lit by soft light from an open window on the left. Beside her stands a man in a red coat and wide-brimmed hat, his black sash marking him as an officer. Behind them hangs a large wall map, a Willem Blaeu and Balthasar Florisz. van Berckenrode map of Holland and West Friesland, which Vermeer owned and used in three of his paintings.

The painting's most important device is the officer himself. He sits in the immediate foreground, large and dark against the lighter room, a technique called repoussoir, a foreground figure that pushes the rest of the scene into depth. Caravaggio used it often, and Vermeer probably learned it from the followers of Caravaggio. The red of the coat adds a note of power and passion, and the hat is made of beaver pelt, a material prized for keeping out rain and snow. The pelts were imported from the Dutch colony of New Netherland, in what is now the eastern United States, which the Dutch West India Company controlled at the time. So a single hat carries the reach of Dutch trade across the Atlantic.

X-ray photographs reveal that Vermeer first gave the woman a large white collar that would have hidden much of her yellow dress. He later extended her cap to cover all her hair. Both changes pull the eye to her face and expression. The blue apron she wears, mostly lost in shadow under the table, was a common working garment because it hid stains. Art historians read the scene as a soldier's surprise visit during the woman's morning chores. The wine glass in her hand suggests some prosperity, since wine cost more than beer at the time.

Art historians have offered conflicting readings of the encounter. Many see a woman being courted honorably by a soldier. Others point to her open hand and smile as a hint of discreet willingness. The painting does not settle the question, and that ambiguity is part of its pull.

Vermeer was then a young painter in Delft, working in a city that had grown wealthy on trade, and his paintings of this period often turn domestic scenes into quiet questions about status and desire. In 2024 the Frick invited the Indigenous Canadian artist Kent Monkman to respond to this painting in its Cabinet Gallery, with new works that examine how seventeenth-century European art represented global trade and colonization. The beaver hat, the map, and the wine all belong to that same economy.
