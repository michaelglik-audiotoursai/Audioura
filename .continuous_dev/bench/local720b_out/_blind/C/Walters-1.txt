Stand at the far end of the gallery, where you can see the canvas whole, with the nearest figures in the foreground and the marble-columned architecture receding behind them. From here the composition reads as a single stage, and you can follow the chain of gestures that leads toward Caesar's body.

The Walters Art Museum, however, does not hold this painting. The source material I have describes Vincenzo Camuccini's *The Death of Julius Caesar*, an 1806 canvas depicting the assassination of Caesar, and places it in the National Museum of Capodimonte in Naples, where it has been since 1864. So the work you are looking for is in Italy, not Baltimore. I cannot give you a description of the physical object here at the Walters, because I do not have source material confirming that it is in the collection.

What I can tell you about the painting itself is this. Camuccini painted the scene of the senators closing in on Caesar in the Roman Senate, the moment of the stabbing that the Roman historians of the late republic described. The composition was built around the fallen dictator, with the conspirators' raised daggers forming a tight arc of motion around him. Camuccini was working in Rome, where the neoclassical revival had made scenes from Roman history a serious subject for history painting, and he meant the picture to be read as a lesson in civic virtue and the cost of tyranny.

The painting's history is itself a story of cost. Frederick Hervey, the 4th Earl of Bristol, commissioned it in 1793 and was pleased with the cartoon, a full-scale preparatory drawing, which critics in Rome praised. The first finished version, completed in 1796, was less well received, so Hervey had Camuccini destroy it and begin again. The final version was not completed until 1806. The Earl died in 1803, and his heirs refused to pay for the work, so Camuccini sold it in 1807 to Joachim Murat, the Napoleonic king of Naples. After Murat's fall, Ferdinand II of the Two Sicilies acquired it and moved it to the Palazzo Reale in Naples.

For a visitor to the Walters, the connection is the subject, not the object. Both the Walters and Capodimonte collect European art, and the Caesar story gave history painters a stage for the same themes of power, betrayal and republican ideals that run through the Baltimore collection.
