Stand facing the portrait from a little to the left of centre, so the light from the gallery windows falls across the canvas rather than glancing off it. From here you can see the way the figure's pale silks and the blue-grey background hold each other up, and the soft, almost powdery finish of the skin. Step closer afterward and you will notice the brushwork dissolving into fine strokes at the edges of the lace.

This is Madame de Pompadour, painted in 1759 by François Boucher, the leading painter of the French Rococo, a style of light colour, curving forms and intimate subject matter that flourished in the courts of eighteenth-century France. Boucher worked in oil on canvas, and in this picture he builds the sitter's luminous complexion from thin, layered glazes and touches of warm pink, so that the face seems lit from within rather than modelled with hard shadows. The composition is deliberately elegant: the figure leans into a sweep of fabric, and the lace and silk are rendered with a shimmering lightness that suits a court portrait meant to project grace and taste.

Pompadour was the mistress of Louis XV and one of the most powerful figures at Versailles, a commoner who shaped royal patronage, politics and the arts. The painting was the last of a series of seven portraits Boucher made of her, which means it closes a long working relationship between painter and sitter. It was first shown at the Château de Versailles, the royal palace where the image was meant to be seen by courtiers, before passing to her brother after her death in 1764.

Boucher was by 1759 at the height of his fame, a favourite of the court and a painter who had shaped the look of the French royal circle for decades. Pompadour had been among his most important patrons since the 1750s, and the series of portraits records how he refined his image of her over time. Painting her for the last time in this polished, luminous manner was a way of setting the final version of her public face.

The portrait leaves a viewer with a sense of composure, of a woman who knew exactly how she wished to be seen. Its surfaces are so smooth and so carefully managed that the painting itself becomes part of the performance of power it was made to carry.
