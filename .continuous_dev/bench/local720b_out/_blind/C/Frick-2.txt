Stand at the center of the room, about six feet back from the canvas, and look for the mirror. Ingres painted the sitter fully frontal, but behind her, in the dresser's glass, a second image of her appears, and from this distance you can see how the reflection repeats the pose and the composition without quite matching it. Up close, the reflection is hard to read. From here, the reversed space becomes legible, and the painting's central trick reveals itself.

Portrait of Comtesse d'Haussonville, painted in 1845 by the French Neoclassical painter Jean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres, shows Louise de Broglie, a twenty-seven-year-old countess from one of France's wealthiest families. She stands fully facing us in a heavily folded satin dress the cool grey-blue of her own eyes. Her hair is parted, with a crimson ribbon tied at the back. Her raised left index finger rests near her mouth, and her right arm curves in a long, sinuous line that Ingres deliberately stretched beyond natural proportion. The palette stays within pale blue, grey, brown, gold and white, so the crimson ribbon becomes the one sharp accent in the room. On the dresser before the mirror sit writing materials, pots, flowers, and a lavishly decorated oriental vase.

The composition repeats a device Ingres first used in his 1814 Portrait of Madame de Senonnes: the sitter's image caught in a mirror behind her. Here it lets him show her from two angles at once, which is a claim about how she wants to be seen as much as a formal experiment. The finger at the lips, the parted hair, and the direct gaze have been compared to his later portrait of Madame Moitessier, and critics have long read the whole work as a study in poised, guarded intimacy.

Ingres was then the most sought-after portraitist in France, but he regarded portraiture as a lesser calling than history painting, the grand Neoclassical subjects that earned prestige but far less money. He accepted only two portrait commissions at this point, this one and the Portrait of Baronne de Rothschild. Earlier, he had sketched the countess in black chalk and begun an oil version around 1842, then abandoned it. The sessions were long, and she found them tedious. One complaint recorded that for nine days Ingres had been working on a single hand. She became pregnant with her third child and could no longer sit, so the first attempt stayed unfinished, and this 1845 canvas was the result of starting over.

Ingres later grumbled that he had not captured her charms, but her family was delighted. Her father, the duc de Broglie, approved, and the politician Adolphe Thiers teased Ingres that he must have been in love with her to paint her that way. Ingres replied that the compliment did not satisfy him.

The painting was shown in 1846 alongside the Grande Odalisque and the Odalisque with Slave. Charles Baudelaire praised all three for their voluptuousness and called Ingres the quintessential painter of women. Nearby, the Frick's small Cabinet display of hand-colored fashion plates from Gallerie des modes et costumes français, produced in Paris between 1778 and 1787, offers a useful counterpoint: the same silhouettes and satin that the countess's dress deploys in pale grey-blue, rendered for a public eager to see what people were wearing.
