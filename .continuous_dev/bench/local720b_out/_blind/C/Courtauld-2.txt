Stand in front of the wall to the right of the central window, about two metres back, where the light falls across the canvas. From here the painting's surface begins to break into its component parts: the pale wall reads as a field of thousands of small coloured touches rather than a flat tone. Step closer and the dots dissolve into solid colour again. This is the distance at which Seurat intended the picture to be seen.

This is Georges Seurat's Young Woman Powdering Herself, painted between 1888 and 1890. It is an oil painting, and the whole image is built from small dots of pure colour placed side by side. Seurat did not mix his paints on the palette. He laid down unmixed pigments in tiny strokes and let the viewer's eye blend them. Look at the wall behind the figure. The light and shadow there are modulated by dots of varying density and intensity, so the surface seems to glow without a single area being painted smooth. This method, which Seurat and Paul Signac developed and called divisionism, is the technique usually known as pointillism. The aim was a light that felt scientifically grounded, based on contemporary theories of colour, rather than the loose brushwork of the Impressionists.

The model is Madeleine Knobloch, Seurat's mistress, who bore him two sons. The painting is among the most intimate things he made, and it is a figure study rather than a public scene. A woman at her dressing table, attending to her face, is a subject that appears often in late nineteenth-century Paris, but Seurat treats it with the same rigour he gave to his grand compositions. The stillness is deliberate. Nothing in the room moves, and the figure's concentration keeps the whole composition quiet.

A technical examination added a further layer. A scan carried out by the Italian Institute of Optics showed that the vase of flowers in the upper left corner was originally a reflection of Seurat himself at work. He painted himself into the mirror, then covered that image with the flowers. If the scan is correct, the painting would be the only self-portrait Seurat ever made, and he hid it.

Seurat was twenty-eight or twenty-nine when he worked on this canvas and had only a few years left. He died in March 1891, aged thirty-one, leaving a small body of finished paintings. By 1888 he had already completed A Sunday Afternoon on the Island of La Grande Jatte, the monumental work that made his reputation, and this figure study belongs to the period in which he refined the dot method and turned it toward smaller, domestic subjects. The Courtauld holds it within its wider collection of Impressionist and Post-Impressionist painting, a group that includes Manet's A Bar at the Folies-Bergère and several works by Cézanne.

What stays with the viewer is the contrast between the method and the moment. The woman is absorbed in an ordinary task, while the surface beneath her is made of thousands of deliberate, measured points of colour, and the mirror in the corner holds a painter who is hidden in the flowers.
