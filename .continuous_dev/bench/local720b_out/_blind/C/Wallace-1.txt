Stand in front of the large canvas and look at the group of four dancers in the centre, then move slightly to your left so you can see the bearded figure in the left background, half hidden by a stone herm. From the central viewing point the four figures read as a single ring. From a step to the side, the figures separate, and you can see that the dancer at the back is turned away from us, his face hidden, while the others face forward. That shift in angle is how the painting's sequence of life stages becomes legible.

This is Nicolas Poussin's A Dance to the Music of Time, painted in Rome between about 1634 and 1636. Four figures hold hands and move in a circle. Time, the winged, grey-bearded figure on the right, plays a lyre to keep them in step. Above them, the goddess Aurora runs ahead of the chariot of Apollo, the sun-god, who carries a ring marking the zodiac. The scene is set in early morning, so the dancers are dancing into the day.

The four dancers represent a cycle of human life, and they are also the four seasons. The figure at the back, seen mostly from behind, is Poverty, which in the seasonal reading is Autumn. Next to him is Labour, or Winter, who is wrapped and bent. On the right is Riches, or Spring, and at the front is Pleasure, or Summer, who leads the others. The sequence runs from poverty through labour and wealth to pleasure, and then pleasure leads back to poverty. The painting is a loop, not a ladder.

The work was a commission from Giulio Rospigliosi, a Roman patron who later became Pope Clement IX. The writer Gian Pietro Bellori reports that Rospigliosi dictated the detailed meaning of the scene. Poussin painted it in oil on canvas, using a clear, even light and smooth surfaces, so that the figures seem carved from the same stone as the herm and the classical relief behind them. Scholars still argue about the exact identities. Malcolm Bull traces the imagery to the Greek poet Nonnus, whose description of the seasons Poussin follows closely, and he argues that Rospigliosi may have added the other interpretation after the painting was finished. The bearded figure at the back has been identified as Bacchus, the god of wine, after the scholar Anthony Blunt suggested that the autumn figure was male, a view now generally accepted.

Poussin painted this in Rome, where he had settled to work away from the French court, and he was known for dense, learned compositions drawn from classical sources. This picture belongs to that body of work. Its title did not come from Poussin. A Wallace Collection catalogue of 1913 first gave it the name by which it is now known, and that name later lent itself to Anthony Powell's twelve-novel sequence.

The painting is a meditation on how each life moves through the same stages, with wealth and poverty passing from one to the other. Its calm, measured figures suggest that the cycle is inevitable, and that Time keeps the rhythm for everyone.
