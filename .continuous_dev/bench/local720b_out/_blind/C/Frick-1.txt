================================================================================
Stand in front of Portrait of Sir Thomas More, and position yourself slightly to the right of center, so you see More in three-quarter right half-profile. From this angle the book he holds and the cord knotted in the upper right come into view together, and the fur lining of his coat catches the light against the black satin and red velvet. Look for these details from here, because a straight-on view flattens them.

This is an oak panel painting made in 1527 by Hans Holbein the Younger, the German artist and printmaker who worked in England. The sitter, Thomas More, was an English statesman and humanist, a scholar who championed classical learning and served as a powerful knighted speaker in Parliament. Holbein paints him in a fur-lined coat of rich fabrics, and around his shoulders hangs a livery chain of Tudor Collar of Esses, with Tudor roses linked along it. Such a chain signaled loyalty to the crown and high office. Notice, too, the cord in the upper right, tied in a loose Franciscan knot. It points to More's private spiritual convictions, a quieter counterpart to the public honors on his chest. On his left index finger he wears a wedding ring set with a stone. Holbein renders each of these things with a precision that lets the surfaces of the velvet, satin, and fur read as distinct materials.

Holbein made this portrait in London in 1527, during his first stretch in England, which began around 1526. He had arrived with a letter of introduction, and through the Dutch humanist Desiderius Erasmus he was steered toward More, the man who would become his most important patron there. The painting is a record of that relationship, and of Holbein's ability to make a sitter look both intellectually formidable and personally reserved. The Frick's version is one of several related works. A closely related drawing with bodycolour survives in the Royal Collection, and a later copy hangs in the National Portrait Gallery, London. Another Holbein portrait of More, part of a large family group, is now lost, though drawings and copies of it still exist.

The painting's meaning sits in that tension between worldly rank and inner faith. More's chain announces his public standing, while the knotted cord and the ring speak to the private man. Holbein holds those two registers together in one face, which is why the image still reads as a portrait of a person rather than only of an office.
