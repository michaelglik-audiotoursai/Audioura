# Portrait of Sir Thomas More

Stand close to Holbein's **Portrait of Sir Thomas More**, near enough to see the sitter's hands, and look first at the upper right corner of the panel. From a distance, that corner reads as background. Up close, a thin cord comes into focus, tied in a loose Franciscan knot. Lower down, on More's left index finger, a wedding ring set with a stone appears. These details are small and easy to miss, yet they carry much of the portrait's meaning.

**The painting**

Hans Holbein the Younger painted this portrait in 1527, in oil on an oak panel. The hard, close-grained wood gave him a smooth surface for the fine detail he is known for. That surface lets you separate the textures of More's clothing: the soft fur lining of his coat, the deep red velvet of his sleeves, and the cooler sheen of black satin. More sits in three-quarter view, turned toward your right, holding a book. The book marks him as a humanist, one of the scholars of the period who revived the study of classical learning.

Across his chest hangs a heavy gold chain called the Collar of Esses, a livery chain made of linked S-shaped forms and hung with a Tudor rose, the emblem of the English royal house. It declares loyalty to the king and high office. More was then a knight and a powerful speaker in the English Parliament.

The portrait sets two kinds of commitment side by side. The chain stands for public duty to the crown. The cord in the corner, knotted in the manner of the Franciscans, the religious order devoted to poverty, points to More's private spiritual convictions. The ring adds a third commitment, to his marriage. Holbein puts worldly power at the centre and quiet signs of faith at the edges.

A related drawing in chalk with bodycolour, meaning opaque watercolour, survives in the Royal Collection in Britain, though scholars think it was probably not a direct study for this panel. A copy in London's National Portrait Gallery was probably painted in Italy or Austria in the early seventeenth century, which shows how widely this image of More was wanted long after his lifetime.

**The artist**

Holbein was a German painter and printmaker who arrived in London in 1526. He came with a recommendation from Desiderius Erasmus, the Dutch humanist scholar and the most famous intellectual in Europe, who urged him to seek out More. This portrait is part of the result of that introduction. Holbein also painted a large group portrait of More's family, but that painting is now lost. Only drawings, most of them also in the Royal Collection, and later copies record it, which makes this panel one of the few surviving paintings from Holbein's first years in England.

**What you notice**

More's direct, alert face sits above all this splendour, and the effect is of a man fully at ease in office yet signalling, in the margins, loyalties beyond it. In a museum currently exploring Gainsborough's portraits and the language of fashion, this panel shows that two and a half centuries earlier, clothing and jewellery were already a portrait's way of stating who a person was and to whom he answered.

The knot in the corner is loose, and the chain is heavy.
