# Officer and Laughing Girl, by Johannes Vermeer

**Orientation**

Stand directly in front of *Officer and Laughing Girl*, close enough that the canvas fills your view, then take one step to the left, toward the side where the painted window lets in light. From here, the officer's dark shape in the foreground looms between you and the woman, as if you are standing just behind his shoulder. The light that falls on her face reaches you across his silhouette. Up close, you can also make out the map on the back wall, which Vermeer painted precisely enough that scholars can identify its exact edition.

**The Work**

This is an oil painting on canvas made around 1657, and it is small: about fifty by forty-six centimetres. Vermeer packs a whole drama into that space. A young woman in a yellow bodice sits at a table, smiling, a wine glass in her hand. Opposite her, a cavalier in a red coat and a broad black hat sits with his back half-turned to us.

The officer's size is the painting's central trick. He is much larger than the woman, pushed into the immediate foreground. This device is called repoussoir, from the French for "to push back." A large dark form placed up front makes everything behind it seem to recede, deepening the room. Caravaggio, the Italian painter famous for dramatic, theatrical lighting, used it often, and Vermeer probably learned it from Caravaggio's imitators. Art historians believe a painting by Gerard van Honthorst inspired this composition. Some also suggest Vermeer used a camera obscura, a darkened box with a lens that projects an image of a scene, to work out the steep perspective between the two figures.

The clothing tells us about these people. The officer's hat is beaver pelt, weather-resistant and costly. The pelts were imported from the New World, probably from New Netherland, today's eastern United States, then controlled by the Dutch West India Company, a trading company with colonial powers. His black sash marks his rank. The woman's yellow bodice, with its braided trim, was an everyday garment called a schort. A blue apron, mostly lost in the shadow of the table, was worn because blue hid stains. Some historians read this as a sign that he has dropped in unannounced during her morning chores. Her glass is the kind used for white wine, and wine cost more than beer, so the glass signals means.

The map behind them shows Holland and West Friesland, a map by Willem Blaeu and Balthasar Florisz. van Berckenrode. Vermeer must have owned it, because it appears in three of his paintings. The scholar Peter van der Krogt pointed out that Vermeer painted it so faithfully, down to its mounting on linen and wooden rods, that it can be identified as Blaeu's 1621 map.

X-ray photographs reveal what Vermeer gave up along the way. He first planned a large white collar on the woman, which would have covered much of her yellow dress. He painted it out. He also extended her cap to cover all her hair, so nothing competes with her face and her expression.

What that expression means is still argued over. Many art historians see an innocent, honourable courtship. Others read her open hand and her smile as a discreet willingness to go further. The painting does not settle it.

**The Artist**

Johannes Vermeer was a Dutch painter of the seventeenth century, and this canvas already holds the elements that define his work. There is a woman in yellow, daylight entering from an open window on the left, and a map on the wall. Each recurs in other paintings. What sets this one apart is the man seated at the table with her. The woman resembles Vermeer's wife, Catharina Bolnes, who is believed to have posed for many of his paintings. So the face he cleared of collar and hair may have been the one he knew best.

**In the Collection**

This is one of the Frick's three Vermeers, alongside *Girl Interrupted at Her Music* and *Mistress and Maid*. It recently inspired new paintings by Kent Monkman, a contemporary Canadian artist who examines European art from an Indigenous perspective. Monkman's work looks at how pictures like this one quietly recorded global trade and colonization, starting with the officer's beaver hat.

**The Emotional Reading**

Because the officer blocks part of our view, we watch the woman's laughter much as he does, from the shadow and at an angle. The light on her face was earned by removing the collar and covering the hair, and it falls just beyond our reach.
