# The Laughing Cavalier, Frans Hals, 1624

Stand close to The Laughing Cavalier, near enough to see the right-hand sleeve clearly. From a distance, the embroidery looks laboriously finished, stitch by stitch. Up close, it dissolves into long, quick brushstrokes. Then step slowly to one side and back again. The man's eyes stay on you the whole way.

## The work

A man of twenty-six turns toward us, one hand on his hip. We know his age because Hals painted it into the canvas. At the top right is a Latin inscription: *aetatis suae 26, anno 1624*. It means "in his twenty-sixth year, in the year 1624," so he was born in 1597 or 1598.

His name was never recorded. Nineteenth-century titles cast him as a soldier, perhaps an officer in one of the part-time citizen militias. Dutch painters often portrayed these companies in groups, as Rembrandt later did in The Night Watch. But he could just as easily be a wealthy civilian. The art historian Pieter Biesboer has proposed the cloth merchant Tieleman Roosterman, whom Hals painted on another occasion.

The canvas measures about 83 by 67 centimetres, and much of it is given over to clothing. His silk costume was gorgeous and very expensive. Hals chose a low viewpoint and a turning pose, which push the embroidered sleeve and lace cuff toward us.

The embroidery carries a coded message. Bees, arrows, and flaming horns of plenty stand for the pleasures and pains of love. So do lovers' knots and tongues of fire. Beside them is an obelisk or pyramid, a sign of strength. There are also the winged cap and serpent-twined staff of Mercury, the Roman messenger god, signifying fortune. Whoever he was, this man wore his ambitions on his sleeve.

The painting's technique matters because it contradicts its subject. The costume would have taken months of skilled needlework. Hals rendered it in rapid, visible strokes. That speed is what makes the composition feel lively and spontaneous rather than stiff.

The famous title is misleading. Victorian audiences invented it after the painting was shown at the Bethnal Green Museum in East London in 1872. That exhibition was deliberately placed away from the fashionable West End to draw working-class visitors. The man is not laughing. He has an enigmatic smile, exaggerated by his upturned moustache.

Even a smile was unusual. Commissioned portraits rarely showed adults smiling until the late eighteenth century. Smiles appeared instead in "tronies," Dutch studies of expressive heads, and in genre paintings, which showed scenes of everyday life. The art historian Seymour Slive called this work "one of the most brilliant of all Baroque portraits."

The following eyes are no trick of special skill. The sitter looks straight out at the painter's position. Because the image is flat, that direct gaze meets you from whatever angle you stand.

## The artist

Frans Hals was a painter of the Dutch Golden Age, the period of Dutch prosperity and artistic flowering. He broke the rule against smiling sitters again and again, often painting broader grins than this one. He favoured informal poses that suggest movement and the impression of a moment caught. He also painted the militia group portraits that may explain this sitter's soldierly reputation.

This picture later reshaped how Hals was seen. At Bethnal Green it was a particular hit with the public and the critics alike. It played a considerable part in raising Hals's critical standing in England, and as prints spread it became one of the best-known old master paintings in Britain.

## Connections

The Marquess of Hertford bought it at a Paris auction in 1865, outbidding Baron James de Rothschild at more than six times the estimate. Today it hangs in the same collection as Fragonard's The Swing, the French painter's flirtatious garden scene, and both are among the most famous paintings in the world.

## What stays with you

The man is a stranger whose name was lost. Yet the painted grin and steady gaze make him feel like someone who has just recognised you. Four centuries on, he is still known by a laugh he never actually gave.
