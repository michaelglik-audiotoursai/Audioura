Your first stop is Le Lézard aux plumes d’or (The Lizard with Golden Feathers). Stand before "Le Lézard aux plumes d’or (The Lizard with Golden Feathers)" and notice the marriage of color and form that immediately captures the eye. The strategic placement of vivid hues against the Louis Broder’s vellum makes this illustrated book more than a visual experience; it's an invitation into a surreal world crafted by Joan Miró.

In 1967, publisher Louis Broder and Joan Miró began printing a series of lithographs to accompany Miró's poem, Le Lézard aux plumes d'or. Shortly after printing, they discovered a defect in the paper that altered and distorted the colors of the lithographs, forcing them to halt the project. Long-standing catalogue accounts reported that the flawed 1967 run was entirely destroyed, though auction records from Sotheby's later revealed that dozens of surviving impressions had been preserved in private collections. Miró reworked the compositions, delaying the official release until a new edition was successfully printed and published by Broder in 1971. "Le Lézard aux plumes d’or" was published in 1971, featuring 40 vibrant lithographs in an illustrated book. The vivid colors and whimsical forms in Miró's lithographs transform his own poem into an evocative visual journey, presenting an ideal of the livre d'artiste—where poetry, imagery, and craftsmanship unite seamlessly. Boris Fridman, a devoted collector of artist’s books, gifted this exquisite piece to the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, expanding its collection of avant-garde printed works. Fridman's donation not only enriched the museum's holdings but also cemented its reputation as one of America’s most comprehensive art collections. Miró’s playful yet meticulous abstraction invites viewers into a realm where dreams become tangible, inspiring us to look beyond the surface of art and explore hidden meanings.

Stop 2: Au Soleil du Plafond by Juan Gris

Address: Avenue of the Arts, 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3391, -71.0879

Orientation: To fully appreciate "Au Soleil du Plafond," take a moment to stand before its display case, where the alignment of text and lithographs becomes vividly apparent.

In 1915, art dealer Léonce Rosenberg conceived a collaborative book pairing twenty poems by Pierre Reverdy with matching illustrations by Spanish painter Juan Gris. The project stalled due to the dealer's inaction and was completely halted when Gris died in 1927, having completed only eleven of the planned images. Nearly thirty years after the painter's death, publisher Tériade revived the undertaking to honor Gris's memory. "Au Soleil du Plafond" exemplifies the exhibition's thesis that artists revolutionized the book as an art form through collaborative efforts. Such integration invites viewers to explore how the domains of art and poetry can intersect, redefining the book as a complete and unified artwork. As you reflect on this work, consider its place within a larger narrative of artistic experimentation during the early 20th century. In this vibrant dialogue between Gris’s illustrations and Reverdy’s text, we witness the transformative power of artistic collaboration.

Stop 3: Moses and Monotheism by Salvador Dalí

Address: Avenue of the Arts, 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3391, -71.0879

Orientation: As you stand before the "Moses and Monotheism" exhibit, position yourself directly in front of the display case. From this vantage point, the interplay of Salvador Dalí's imaginative illustrations with Sigmund Freud's provocative text becomes fully visible, allowing you to appreciate the synergy between visual art and written word.

In 1974, Salvador Dalí produced a series of illustrations for Sigmund Freud's 1939 text *Moses and Monotheism*. He drew the compositions onto printing plates and produced the prints across etching and lithography. Dalí published the suite directly on leather, with some descriptions documenting the material as sheepskin and others as lambskin. Dalí's illustrations for Freud's text are more than mere images; they are a profound dialogue with Freud’s ideas. The use of bold, almost dreamlike imagery reflects Dalí's signature surrealist style, inviting viewers into a world where the unconscious mind is fully realized. The illustrations, with their intricate details and striking compositions, are printed on soft glove sheepskin, adding a tactile richness that complements the cerebral themes of the book. This work exemplifies the exhibition's thesis: artists like Dalí and authors like Freud collaborated across disciplines, transforming the book into a holistic art form. The intersection of Dalí’s surrealist visuals with Freud’s psychoanalytic text marks a pivotal moment in the evolution of both art and ideas, prompting us to consider what the evolution of art can reveal about the evolution of belief.
