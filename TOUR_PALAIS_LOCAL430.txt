As you continue your tour, you will encounter the Violes gambe by William Turner (Londres, 1652) at Palais Lascaris and the Sacqueboute ténor by Anton Schnitzer (Nuremberg, 1581), showcasing the evolution of musical instruments across centuries. Your first stop is Harpe by Naderman (Paris, 1780). As you stand before the harp by Naderman, built in Paris in 1780, position yourself so you can see the intricate detailing along the curved neck of the instrument. From this angle, the gold leaf restoration glistens under the museum lights, revealing the craftsmanship that Jean-Henri Naderman was known for during his tenure as harpmaker to Queen Marie Antoinette.

The harp you see was crafted by Jean-Henri Naderman, a master harpmaker who was appointed to the French royal court in 1778 and made instruments for the likes of Marie Antoinette, the last queen of France before the Revolution.Although the specific details of this harp’s visual design are not extensively documented, its presence at the Palais Lascaris connects it to a broader narrative of musical history. Jean-Henri Naderman’s work was significant not only for its technical excellence but also for its historical context. During the late 18th century, the harp was a symbol of both musical excellence and aristocratic refinement. Naderman’s harps were not just instruments; they were works of art that graced the royal courts of France. The Palais Lascaris itself, which houses France’s second most important collection of historical musical instruments, offers a rich context for understanding the place of Naderman’s harp within the broader tapestry of music history. This harp, part of that collection, serves as a bridge to the past, offering visitors a glimpse into the musical traditions and innovations of 18th-century France. As the harp stands in this storied venue, it invites curiosity about the future advancements in string instruments, just as Naderman's creations once foreshadowed the evolution of the harp itself.

From this vantage point, you can appreciate the viol's craftsmanship and read accompanying details about its creation and historical context, illuminating its significance within the broader collection of this aristocratic 17th-century building.

The viola da gamba by William Turner, crafted in London in 1652, is a remarkable piece in the Palais Lascaris, a seventeenth-century aristocratic building in Nice,' collection of over 500 historical instruments. This viol, with its resonant six-string design, showcases the transition from Renaissance to Baroque music, reflecting England's cultural changes during a period of significant political upheaval. William Turner, based in Aldgate, London, was renowned for his intricate craftsmanship, often recognized by the heart-shaped openings on his instruments. This viol is among the treasures of the Palais, alongside viols made by Paolo Antonio Testore in Milan and Anton Schnitzer's sacqueboute ténor. The collection owes its existence to Antoine Gautier, a 19th-century collector and amateur musician from Nice, who left many of these instruments. The viol played a role in the evolution of musical expression during the late Baroque era, a time when composers and musicians expanded sonic possibilities. It not only showcases the artistry of its maker but also acts as a bridge to understanding the broader cultural shifts of its time.

Stop 3: Sacqueboute ténor by Anton Schnitzer (Nuremberg, 1581)

Coordinates: 43.6973, 7.2702

Orientation: Stand just a few feet back to allow the full view of the tenor sackbut by Anton Schnitzer (Nuremberg, 1581). From here, its historical presence emerges as it holds the title of one of the earliest surviving sackbuts in original condition. This position also allows you to notice its detailed engravings and craftsmanship that may escape a closer inspection.

The tenor sackbut by Anton Schnitzer is a remarkable piece from 1581, crafted in Nuremberg, a hub for instrument making during the Renaissance. The sackbut itself, an early form of the trombone, is characterized by its comparatively narrow bore and more delicate sound, designed for the intricate music of its time. Schnitzer, part of a distinguished family of brass instrument makers, imbued this tenor sackbut with a subtle grace, evident in the instrument's engraved bell garland. The date 1581 etched there confirms its origin and links it to another Schnitzer creation, showcasing the consistent artistry of this Nuremberg workshop. The Palais Lascaris, a seventeenth-century aristocratic building in Nice, renowned for its impressive collection of historical musical instruments, houses over 500 pieces, with this sackbut being one of its most prized. Its presence in the museum underscores the importance of preserving musical history and the evolution of sound.

Stop 4: Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore (Milan, 1696)

Coordinates: 43.6973, 7.2702

Orientation: Stand to the right of the Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore, crafted in Milan in 1696. From this angle, you can appreciate the instrument's intricate craftsmanship and the way its form complements the surrounding exhibits in this room of Palais Lascaris, part of France's second-largest collection of musical instruments.

The Testore legacy began with Carlo Giuseppe Testore, who learned his craft from his teacher Giovanni Grancino, a pupil of the renowned luthier, and established his workshop in Milan. His sons, Carlo Antonio and Paolo Antonio, continued the family tradition, each contributing uniquely to the world of string instruments. While he may have been considered the least skilled among his family, his work still reflects the resilient and practical approach that characterized Milanese violin making. The presence of this instrument in the Palais Lascaris, the city's historical music collections, underscores the museum's transformation from a historical palace into a vibrant center for musical heritage. By 2011, the permanent exhibition opened to the public, showcasing a rich tapestry of instruments, including this bass viol. This collection, second only to the Musée de la Musique de la Philharmonie in Paris, a collection of over 7,000 instruments, offers visitors a deep dive into the history and artistry of music.
