Your first stop is Le Lézard aux plumes d’or (The Lizard with Golden Feathers). As you approach "Le Lézard aux plumes d’or (The Lizard with Golden Feathers)" at the Museum of Fine Arts in Boston, position yourself near the center of the gallery space. From here, you can fully appreciate the interplay of vibrant colors and whimsical forms that Joan Miró has woven into this illustrated book. This vantage point reveals the intricate details of the lithographs, allowing you to see how the images dance across the Louis Broder’s vellum, inviting a deeper understanding of the work's symbolic language.

In 1971, the renowned publisher Louis Broder took the extraordinary step of collaborating with Joan Miró to produce "Le Lézard aux plumes d’or." Broder, known for his dedication to the livre d'artiste tradition, ensured that this project became a landmark in artistic publishing by integrating artist, printer, and poet in a harmonious collaboration. As a result, Boris Fridman's generous donation of this work to the Museum of Fine Arts in Boston significantly enriched the museum's collection, highlighting the essential role of collaboration in advancing the book as an art form. The illustrated book, with its 40 color lithographs, embodies Miró’s playful yet profound exploration of myth and nature. The images are printed on publisher’s vellum, a choice that lends them a luxurious texture and a sense of permanence.This piece exemplifies the exhibition's argument that artists revolutionized the book form through deeply collaborative ventures, blending images, words, and typography into a single, cohesive work. Looking ahead, the concept of transformation in art continues to inspire new interpretations, echoing Miró’s legacy in unexpected ways.

Address: 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Orientation: Look for Au Soleil du Plafond in the galleries.

In 1955, Tériade commissioned to illustrate Pierre Reverdy's poems, resulting in the creation of "Au Soleil du Plafond," a work that intertwines visual art and poetry in a harmonious dialogue. Gris, a pivotal figure in the Cubist movement, crafted each lithograph with a structure and rhythm that echoes Reverdy's poetic sensibility, manifesting the ethos of the livre d'artiste—a book where text and image coalesce into a singular art form. Displayed in the Torf Gallery, named after a Linde Family of the arts, this piece exemplifies how artists and poets revolutionized the book as an art form, transcending conventional boundaries and enriching the museum's collection with rarely seen works. Here, the viewer is invited to ponder how modern interpretations of transformation might connect to ancient narratives, echoing through time.

Address: 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Orientation: ** As you stand facing the display case featuring "Moses and Monotheism," position yourself to the left to catch the interplay of Dalí's surreal illustrations with Freud's provocative text. This angle reveals how the texture of the sheepskin shifts under the exhibit lighting, amplifying the tactile effect of this bold work.

Salvador Dalí, famed for his surreal imagery, chose to illustrate Sigmund Freud's "Moses and Monotheism" in 1974, bringing the psychoanalyst's controversial theories to life. The medium of drypoints and lithographs on sheepskin was not merely a stylistic choice; it reinforced the ancient and venerable nature of the subject matter itself. In these illustrations, Dalí visually complements Freud's argument that monotheism was a revolutionary shift in human thought, much like the Surrealism movement was for art. The exhibition places this collaboration within a larger context of artists and authors pushing boundaries, echoing the dynamic blend seen in works like "Au Soleil du Plafond." This interplay between image and text invites viewers to consider the enduring legacy of these transformative ideas.
