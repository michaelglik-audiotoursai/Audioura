Stop 1: Harpe by Naderman (Paris, 1780)

Coordinates: 43.6967, 7.2716

Orientation: You are about to explore the Palais Lascaris in Nice, home to a remarkable collection of historical musical instruments. Within this museum, you will encounter four exquisite works, including the Harpe by Naderman from Paris, 1780, the Violes gambe by William Turner from London, 1652, the Sacqueboute ténor by Anton Schnitzer from Nuremberg, 1581, and the Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore from Milan, 1696. This tour delves into the evolution of musical craftsmanship and cultural exchange in Europe from the 16th to 18th centuries, as seen through these unique instruments. The museum's transformation into a music museum in 2011, with instruments like the Testore violin bass, underscores its rich historical significance. Your first stop is Harpe by Naderman (Paris, 1780). As you enter the opulent interiors of the Palais Lascaris in Nice, head towards the section dedicated to string instruments. From this vantage point, the delicate craftsmanship of the Harpe by Naderman (Paris, 1780) becomes apparent, its grandeur set against the ornate backdrop of the former aristocratic residence. This angle allows the viewer to appreciate the intricate details of the harp's design, which might otherwise be overlooked amidst the extensive collection.

Known for his association with the French court, Naderman served as the harpmaker to Queen Marie Antoinette, the last queen of France before the revolution, herself. His harps were esteemed not only for their aesthetic elegance but also for their technical innovation, which played a pivotal role in shaping the music halls. The choice of wood and other materials in Naderman's work was deliberate, aimed at achieving a resonant sound quality that was highly sought after by musicians of the time. This harp connects to the larger story of the Palais Lascaris, the music museum established in 2001, once owned by the Vintimille-Lascaris family until 1802, and later acquired by the city of Nice in 1942 to become a museum. Today, the venue houses over 500 instruments, making it France’s second most significant collection after the Musée de la Musique in Paris, part of the Cité de la Musique. As music transcended borders, innovations in harp design, such as those introduced by Naderman, continued to influence the evolution of this instrument, offering a glimpse into the vibrant musical landscape of 18th-century France.

Stop 2: Violes gambe by William Turner (Londres, 1652)

Orientation: Stand slightly to the left of the viol, allowing the light to catch the intricate craftsmanship of its strings and body. From this angle, the heart-shaped motif—distinctive to Turner's viols—becomes particularly visible, providing a glimpse into the artistry and skill of the mid-17th-century English instrument maker.

The viol features six strings, allowing it to produce rich, resonant tones favored in chamber music of the time. With heart-shaped motifs, the instrument reflects the delicate and precise aesthetic choices of its maker, who worked in Aldgate, London, around 1650. The viol's presence in this collection was made possible through the bequest of Antoine Gautier, a 19th-century collector from Nice. Gautier, an amateur musician himself, was deeply passionate about preserving the legacy of historical musical instruments. His dedication significantly enriched the Palais Lascaris, a seventeenth-century aristocratic building in Nice, enhancing its status as one of France's most important repositories of musical history, second only to the Musée de la Musique in Paris. Both instruments exemplify the evolution of musical design and the shifting tastes in sound production over the centuries. As music evolved, so too did the craftsmanship and design of instruments like Turner's viol, which influenced the stringed instruments that followed.

Directions: Next: Sacqueboute ténor by Anton Schnitzer (Nuremberg, 1581).

Stop 3: Sacqueboute ténor by Anton Schnitzer (Nuremberg, 1581)

Orientation: As you stand in front of the Sacqueboute ténor by Anton Schnitzer, position yourself slightly to the side to catch a glimpse of the intricate details on the bell garland. From this angle, the mark that dates it to 1581 is visible — a rare feature that links it to its historical roots.

The Sacqueboute ténor, a tenor sackbut crafted by Anton Schnitzer in 1581, is one of the earliest surviving examples in its original condition. This instrument, a precursor to the modern trombone, reflects the craftsmanship of the Schnitzer family of Nuremberg, renowned for their influence in brass instrument-making during the Renaissance. The sackbut's design incorporates a slide mechanism, allowing musicians of the era to achieve varied pitches, a revolutionary feature at the time. Its survival in original form offers a tangible connection to the soundscapes of the past, bridging centuries of musical evolution. Anton Schnitzer's craftsmanship is evident in the meticulous construction of the instrument, which includes a conical mouthpiece and finely engineered slide braces. These elements not only highlight his technical expertise but also demonstrate the innovation that characterized the late 16th-century German brass production. The instrument's historical significance is further underscored by its provenance; it shares the same mark as another Schnitzer instrument, establishing a direct link to its maker. The presence of such a significant piece underlines the museum's role in preserving musical history. As part of this extensive collection, the sackbut complements other notable instruments, such as violas d'amore and clarinets by Adolphe Sax, the inventor of the saxophone, showcasing the diversity and depth of the collection.

Directions: Your final stop in Palais Lascaris: Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore (Milan, 1696).

Stop 4: Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore (Milan, 1696)

Orientation: Stand slightly to the side of the Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore, Milan, 1696, to observe the intricate craftsmanship of this bass viol, part of Palais Lascaris's impressive collection of musical instruments.

The Testore family, including Paolo Antonio, was central to the Milanese tradition of crafting resilient and practical string instruments, a legacy initiated by Carlo Giuseppe Testore, who founded the family workshop. In the early 18th century, Paolo Antonio Testore, alongside his brother Carlo Antonio, continued their father's craft, producing instruments known for their robust construction and rich sound. Their work has left a lasting mark on the world of string instruments, influencing the development of the violin family during a time when music was undergoing significant transformation. The presence of Testore’s bass viol in this collection highlights the museum's commitment to preserving the history and evolution of musical craftsmanship. The instrument's enduring impact on music is evident in the way it bridges the baroque traditions with the future innovations of the string family.
