"Le Lézard aux plumes d’or," by Joan Miró, is an illustrated book published in 1971 by Louis Broder. Broder, renowned for his expertise in creating limited edition books that required seamless collaboration between artist and publisher, commissioned Miró to create this fascinating work. This collaboration resulted in an illustrated book consisting of 40 color lithographs, each one a vibrant dance of form and color. These lithographs embody the surrealist curiosity that Miró often explored, blending mythical imagery with the unconscious mind. The Louis Broder's choice to use vellum for the binding adds a tactile richness that complements the visual depth of Miró's work. The consequence of this collaboration is a piece that transcends traditional visual art, merging text and image into a cohesive whole. His gift enriches the museum's collection, allowing visitors to experience the revolutionary intersection of modern art and literary form.

Salvador Dalí's illustrations for Sigmund Freud's "Moses and Monotheism" are more than mere accompaniments to the text. They transform the book into an artistic dialogue, with Dalí’s surreal style inviting viewers to reassess Freud’s controversial thesis about the origins of monotheism. Freud, known for his psychoanalytic theories, authored this groundbreaking text suggesting Moses might have been an Egyptian, challenging traditional narratives of Hebrew history. Dalí’s visual interpretation adds layers of meaning, utilizing his distinct surrealism to mirror Freud's audacious narrative. In 1974, Dalí undertook the task of illustrating Freud's contentious ideas, merging visual art with psychoanalytic theory. The result was a deeply collaborative endeavor, marrying words and images to push the boundaries of what a book could be. This exemplifies the exhibition’s thesis that revolutionized the livre d'artiste form, where artist and author work in tandem to create a unified art piece.
