Orientation: You are about to explore the Picasso, Miro, Dali: Unbound exhibition at the MFA in Boston. This exhibition focuses on the livre d'artiste, a groundbreaking art form that revolutionized the book as an art form through deeply collaborative ventures, rarely seen by the public. Within the Lois B. and Michael K. Torf Gallery, you will encounter Le Lézard aux plumes d’or (The Lizard with Golden Feathers), Au Soleil du Plafond, and Moses and Monotheism. These extraordinary works by Spanish artists delve into the theme of transformation as a catalyst for change and enlightenment in art and belief. Your first stop is Le Lézard aux plumes d’or (The Lizard with Golden Feathers). As you approach the display in the Linde Family Gallery, focus your gaze on the intricate bindings of "Le Lézard aux plumes d’or (The Lizard with Golden Feathers)." From here, the interplay between Miró's vibrant lithographs and the text's typographic elegance becomes strikingly clear, lending insight into the harmonious marriage of art and literature.

In 1971, Louis Broder, an influential publisher known for his passion for fine artist books, commissioned Joan Miró to create "Le Lézard aux plumes d’or." Broder's commitment to this project resulted in a set of 40 color lithographs, printed on exquisite publisher’s vellum, demonstrating the rich, tactile quality of the materials chosen. This work became a hallmark of the livre d'artiste format, which seamlessly integrates image and text, exemplifying the exhibition's thesis that the book itself is a work of art. Boris Fridman, a dedicated collector of such artist's books, graciously donated this piece to the Museum of Fine Arts, enriching the museum's collection of collaborative printed works. Miró's interplay between bold, whimsical forms and text in "Le Lézard aux plumes d’or" not only reflects but also elevates Broder’s vision, making it a crucial piece in understanding the transformation of traditional books into art. Such transformations point toward how enlightenment and belief are often intertwined in the world of art.

Stop 2: Au Soleil du Plafond by Juan Gris

Address: 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3394, -71.0905

Orientation: Stand directly beneath the display of "Au Soleil du Plafond" to fully appreciate how the nuances of color and line converge within this exceptional livre d'artiste. From this vantage point, the intricate interplay between word and image becomes strikingly evident, inviting a contemplation on the artistic collaboration at play.

In 1955, Éditions Verve published "Au Soleil du Plafond," a significant work by Juan Gris and Pierre Reverdy, seeking to encapsulate the essence of the collaborative spirit that defined their era. Gris, known for his mastery in Cubism, crafted eleven lithographs for this edition, merging visual art with Reverdy's poetic texts. This intersection of visual and literary art exemplifies the exhibition's thesis that artists revolutionized the book form through such collaborations. Pierre Reverdy, a pivotal figure in the literary avant-garde, wrote the accompanying text, instilling his distinct poetic sensibility into the work. Their combined efforts produced a harmonious dialogue, setting a precedent for future collaborations in artist books. This creation, on Arches paper, underscores the transformative power of collaboration, reshaping perceptions of what a book can embody within the gallery walls.

Stop 3: Moses and Monotheism by Salvador Dalí

Address: 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3394, -71.0905

Orientation: As you stand before the "Moses and Monotheism" exhibit, position yourself to the left of the center display case. Here, the intricate details of the illustrations on sheepskin come into clearer view, revealing the fusion of Salvador Dalí's surreal imagery with the text of Sigmund Freud. This angle allows you to perceive the texture and sheen that the unusual medium adds to each piece.

In 1974, the iconic surrealist artist Salvador Dalí created a series of illustrations for Sigmund Freud's "Moses and Monotheism." Dalí's choice of sheepskin as his canvas lent an exceptional tactile quality to the work, enhancing the interplay between image and text. This collaboration highlighted a dynamic intersection where visual arts and psychoanalytic theory met in a revolutionary format. Freud, known for his foundational contributions to psychoanalysis, authored the provocative text in 1939, suggesting that Moses was an Egyptian nobleman rather than a Hebrew slave. This particular edition of the work was published by The Hogarth Press, famous for taking bold steps in the literary and artistic world. Together, Dalí and Freud’s combined efforts created a set of ten illustrations that not only challenged conventional religious narratives but also exemplified the exhibition's argument that books could emerge as unified art forms through inter-artist collaborations. The exhibition posits that such ventures revolutionized book form into expressive multi-dimensional artworks. Dalí’s surreal visuals enriching Freud’s controversial ideas resulted in a transformative exploration of theology and history.
