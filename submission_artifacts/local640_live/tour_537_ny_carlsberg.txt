Stop 1: The Absinthe Drinker

Address: Dantes Plads 7, 1556 København, Denmark

Coordinates: 55.6759, 12.5683

Museum Information: 

The museum is open Tuesday to Sunday from 10:00 to 5:00, and closed on Mondays. Admission is free.

Orientation: These diverse works, spanning different styles and periods, unite in their transformative power to reshape perceptions. Your first stop is The Absinthe Drinker. Stand before Édouard Manet's "The Absinthe Drinker" and observe the way the dimly lit room enhances the painting's somber mood. From here, you can appreciate the rough texture of the canvas, which subtly amplifies the stark loneliness of its solitary figure.

Édouard Manet chose an alcoholic ragpicker named Collardet, who frequented the area around the Louvre, to serve as the model for this canvas. When he showed the finished piece to his former teacher, Thomas Couture, the older master dismissed it by declaring that the painter himself was the true absinthe drinker who had lost his moral sense. Manet nevertheless submitted the painting as his debut entry to the 1859 Paris Salon. This piece, a pivotal moment in Manet's career, captures a man enveloped in the ritualistic consumption of absinthe. Manet chose to depict this scene using oil paint on canvas, a medium that allowed him to create a textured surface, emphasizing the blurred line between sobriety and intoxication. The absinthe drinker, cloaked in shadow, becomes both an embodiment of bohemian allure and a commentary on social isolation. In the mid-19th century, absinthe was a symbol of both creative inspiration and moral decline. Manet, aware of the cultural significance, painted this work not just as an aesthetic exploration but as a social statement. By adding Manet's work to his museum's holdings, Jacobsen ensured the painting's preservation and public accessibility. The painting's presence in the Ny Carlsberg Glyptotek is particularly noteworthy because the museum is primarily known for its sculpture collection. Jacobsen's inclusion of Manet's work reflects his vision to broaden the museum's scope, making it a home for both classical sculptures and pioneering paintings. As you reflect on "The Absinthe Drinker," consider how altered perception influenced artistic renditions of more serene landscapes. Manet's choice to tackle the complexities of human experience through this piece resonates deeply, offering a window into the struggles and inspirations of 19th-century Parisian life.

Stop 2: Windmill and Boats near Zaandam

Address: Dantes Plads 7, 1556 København, Denmark

Coordinates: 55.6759, 12.5683

Orientation: Look for Windmill and Boats near Zaandam in the galleries.

In 1871, amidst the unfolding aftermath of Monet found refuge in the tranquil town of Zaandam, Netherlands. Encouraged by fellow artist Charles-François Daubigny, Monet chose this location deliberately, captivated by its windmills and waterways. During his five-month stay, Monet created an impressive 25 works, capturing the essence of the town and its landscape. Monet’s exploration of the natural landscape is evident in his choice of oil on canvas, measuring 48 by 75.5 centimeters. The painting features a cluster of boats gently tethered, seemingly poised to drift along the Zaan River, a river in the Netherlands. Meanwhile, the windmill, with its vividly contrasting sails, serves as the painting’s focal point, symbolizing both movement and stillness. Standing before this piece, viewers might sense a serene harmony, evoking a moment of peace amidst a world in flux. As you ponder this scene, consider the transformation in Monet’s works as he later shifts his gaze back to the vibrant blooms of his garden at Giverny, exploring the delicate beauty of nature’s intricate details.

Stop 3: Pink Roses

Address: Dantes Plads 7, 1556 København, Denmark

Coordinates: 55.6759, 12.5683

Orientation: As you find yourself in front of Vincent van Gogh's "Pink Roses," step close enough to appreciate the brushwork—those subtle, swirling strokes—and notice how the light dances across the canvas, highlighting the ephemeral beauty of the roses. This will give you insight into the artist's delicate use of color and form during those last poignant weeks of his life.

Vincent van Gogh painted this canvas in 1890 as part of a series of flower still lifes celebrating renewed life, reflecting his optimism about the future. The painting is an oil on canvas measuring 32 by 40.5 cm and captures a still life of pink roses that exude a gentle translucence, their soft pinks standing out against the darker green leaves. The pink tones in the blossoms have faded significantly over time, softening the contrast originally struck against the surrounding green shades. The contrasting tones create a depth that seems to momentarily suspend time, reflecting van Gogh's ongoing endeavor to capture the fleeting beauty of life.

While some records date the work to his final weeks at Saint-Rémy in May, other accounts place its creation in June. It was one of his final works before his untimely death later that year. Van Gogh, grappling with deteriorating mental health, used painting as a means to find solace, pouring his hopes and despair onto the canvas.

After his death, the painting was generously donated to the Ny Carlsberg Glyptotek by Helga. Her contribution allowed the piece to be housed in a museum renowned for its sculptures, yet richly enhanced by such profound paintings. Standing here, parallels emerge between van Gogh's "Pink Roses" and Claude Monet's "Windmill and Boats near Zaandam," both capturing the transience of light and nature. These works offer a glimpse into the impressions that defined an era. Van Gogh's gentle bloom serves as a tender reminder of life's impermanence.

This tour highlights the theme of capturing the fleeting beauty of life through artistic expression, as seen in works like van Gogh's "Pink Roses." These pieces remind us of the delicate balance between transience and permanence, encouraging reflection on the complexities of human experience and the natural world. That's 3 stops in all.
