Stop 2: Buddha dated 338

Address: 200 Larkin St, San Francisco, CA 94102

Coordinates: 37.780164, -122.416199

Orientation: Stand slightly to the right of the Buddha dated 338, allowing your gaze to take in the full expanse of the large lotus base beneath the figure.

The inscription on its base dates the work to the year 338, making it the oldest known dated Buddha sculpture produced in China, anchoring it firmly in the early development of Buddhist iconography in East Asia. The statue’s unadorned surface contrasts markedly with the richly bejeweled Buddha you visited earlier, reflecting a focus on spiritual essence rather than regal display. Avery Brundage gave this work to the museum because he sought to establish a collection that demonstrated Buddhism’s deep historical roots across Asia. As a result, this statue now occupies a central place not only in the museum’s collection but also in discussions of cross-cultural transmission of Buddhist iconography throughout Asia. The figure’s calm, meditative poise, carved with quiet precision, invites a measured contemplation of time’s passage—how centuries have layered meaning onto this stone, as the museum itself has grown from these roots into a space that now includes contemporary Asian art.

Continue to Head of a Buddha.

Stop 3: Head of a Buddha

Address: 200 Larkin St, San Francisco, CA 94102

Coordinates: 37.780164, -122.416199

Orientation: As you stand close to the Head of a Buddha, focus your attention on the subtle contours of the face—the soft curve of the eyelids, the gentle tilt of the lips. From this vantage point, the fine stucco surface reveals the quiet craftsmanship that gives this fragment its meditative presence, a detail easily lost from a distance.

This head is made of stucco, a material that allowed artisans to achieve refined detail and a polished finish, reflecting a moment frozen in spiritual calm. The technique is significant because stucco enabled a delicate expression of serenity that stone or bronze might not have conveyed so softly. The face’s serene gaze and subtle folds around the neck indicate the intention behind this piece: to embody the Buddha’s inner peace and enlightenment. The calm surface and measured features invite reflection on the continuity of Buddhist art’s evolving emotional language. The way this head isolates serenity from the body suggests an enduring human pursuit: capturing inner stillness despite external fragmentation. This Buddha head echoes the contemplative silence seen in the earlier Buddha dated 338 you visited, grounding a legacy in both ancient and modern expressions of spiritual introspection. That pursuit continues today in contemporary works held here, as the museum’s recent Heart of Zen exhibition showcased 13th-century Chinese ink paintings.

Stop 5: Seated crowned and bejeweled Buddha

Address: 200 Larkin St, San Francisco, CA 94102

Coordinates: 37.780164, -122.416199

Orientation: From this vantage, the serene expression and the intricate crown become clearer, inviting a closer look at the quiet power held in stillness.

The crown and jewels adorning the Buddha signify his enlightened status and royal dignity; these elements echo the iconography seen in the Amida Buddha the Byodo-in temple, built in the 11th century, linking this work to a wider East Asian devotional tradition. The artist who shaped this figure worked within a flourishing era art, likely responding to the spiritual and political currents of the Ly court, which actively promoted Buddhism as a state religion. Standing before this statue, one senses the convergence of meditative calm and regal presence—a composed satisfaction that arises from the Buddha’s poised gesture and the enduring solidity of the stone. Many years after her death, a collector gifted the Seated crowned and bejeweled Buddha to the Asian Art Museum. The museum relocated to its current location and reopened on March 20, 2003.

This tour highlights the evolution of Asian art in the 21st century, illustrating how works like the Seated crowned and bejeweled Buddha reflect a rich dialogue between historical traditions and contemporary expressions. Understanding this evolution deepens our appreciation for the enduring significance of Buddhist iconography and its impact on cultural identity.
