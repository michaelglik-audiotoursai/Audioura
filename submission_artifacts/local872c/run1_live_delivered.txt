Stop 1: Notre-Dame Cathedral Basilica of Saigon

Coordinates: 10.779722, 106.699167

Type/Specialty: Historical Landmark

Specific Examples: Gothic architecture, stained glass windows, and the statue of the Virgin Mary.

Orientation: This tour will take you from the Notre-Dame Cathedral Basilica of Saigon to Thi Bar Saigon, covering an approximate distance of 5 km through the heart of the city. As you traverse this bustling urban landscape, you’ll encounter a blend of colonial architecture and modern life, revealing the layers of history and transformation that characterize this vibrant metropolis. Your first stop is Notre-Dame Cathedral Basilica of Saigon. From the intersection of Lê Duẩn and Công xã Paris, walk east along Công xã Paris for about 200 meters. You will see the cathedral's towering spires as you approach. The contrast of red brick walls against the bright sky draws your eyes upward along the cathedral’s vertical lines, while the wide square before you hums with the mingled sounds of city life and distant church bells.

The cathedral before you was constructed between 1877 and 1880 under the direction of French administrators, who initially named it the Church of Saigon, or l'Église de Saïgon in French. It replaced an earlier wooden church that had become too small for the growing Catholic community established after the French conquest of Cochinchina. The decision to build a grander stone cathedral reflected the colonial administration’s intent to create a lasting symbol of their presence and to serve both French settlers and local converts.

The building’s twin bell towers rise 58 meters into the sky, a clear statement of the importance attributed to this site in the late 19th century. The red bricks that form the cathedral’s walls remain striking, testifying to the materials and techniques brought from France and physically linking the architecture to European traditions. The sound of bells tolling from the towers punctuates the urban noise, reminding visitors that this is still an active place of worship amid Ho Chi Minh City’s rapid pace.

In 1959, the cathedral’s identity shifted when the statue of Our Lady of Peace was installed in the square before it, and the name Notre-Dame Cathedral began to be used. This addition gave the site a new focal point for devotion and connected local Catholic practice to a broader tradition of Marian reverence. The installation of the statue made the forecourt a gathering place, merging colonial heritage with ongoing religious rituals.

Stop 2: Saigon Central Post Office

Coordinates: 10.780000, 106.700000

Type/Specialty: Architectural Landmark

Specific Examples: Beautiful colonial architecture, large interior with wooden benches, and historical maps.

Orientation: Standing in Paris Commune Square, directly facing the broad facade of the Saigon Central Post Office, your gaze aligns with its grand arches and the green ironwork that frames tall, shuttered windows.

The Saigon Central Post Office was constructed between 1886 and 1891, during the period when Vietnam was under French colonial rule as part of French Indochina. Designed chiefly by Alfred Foulhoux, with the influence of Auguste Henri Vildieu nearby, the building blends Gothic, Renaissance, and French architectural styles into a single, striking composition. Although the post office is often mistakenly credited to Eiffel, it was Foulhoux and Vildieu who shaped the structure you see today. Inside, vast arched windows cast light onto wooden counters and green-painted metal details, while the air carries a faint coolness—a quiet reminder of the building’s ongoing function amid the bustle of the city.

The story of communication here stretches further back. In 1864, Saigon was the origin point for the first letters bearing Vietnam’s inaugural ‘stork’ postage stamp. This emblem, the earliest stamp used in the country, marked the beginning of formalized mail service in the region and transformed how information and connection flowed across distances. The post office’s location and design embody the ambition of that era to link Saigon not only to the wider colonial network but also to the vast, interconnected world beyond.

Stop 4: Independence Palace

Coordinates: 10.777050, 106.695370

Type/Specialty: Historical Site

Specific Examples: The main hall, war rooms, and beautiful gardens.

Orientation: Standing at the wide front steps, the smooth concrete underfoot and the cool shade from the soaring canopy of trees, your gaze meets the broad façade of the Independence Palace. The low hum of the city blends with the birdsong threading through the gardens nearby.

Completed on October 31, 1966, this building was designed by architect Ngô Viết Thụ to replace the older Norodom Palace, which had been damaged in a 1962 bombing by two Republic of Vietnam Air Force pilots. One of their bombs pierced a room where President Diệm was reading but did not detonate. Although the bomb failed to explode, Madame Nhu, the president’s sister-in-law, was injured; three palace staff members died, and thirty were wounded. The palace then served as the home and workplace of the president of the Republic of Vietnam until 1975.

Today, the palace is preserved as a museum and convention hall. The quiet footsteps of visitors have replaced the tension of the past, but scars remain visible in the bullet marks and preserved war rooms. The gardens offer a softer rhythm, contrasting with the dramatic events that once took place inside. The Independence Palace stands as a witness to moments that redefined a nation, its gates both a barrier and a threshold between eras.

Stop 5: War Remnants Museum

Coordinates: 10.779500, 106.692000

Type/Specialty: Museum

Specific Examples: Exhibits on the Vietnam War, military artifacts, and photography displays.

Orientation: Stand before the broad façade of 28 Võ Văn Tần Street, where the War Remnants Museum asserts itself with a sober presence.

The museum holds the tangible traces of the First Indochina War and the Vietnam War—photographs, military equipment, and documents—that tell stories far beyond propaganda. The walls here are thick with photographs that capture faces of loss and survival, each image a frozen instant of anguish or resilience. The clink of metal from vintage aircraft and artillery pieces on display punctuates the otherwise quiet halls, a reminder of violence made material. In 2024, the War Remnants Museum was named Asia’s No. 1 museum by Tripadvisor. This accolade reflects its profound impact on visitors, drawn not by spectacle, but by the raw, unvarnished realities it presents. Here, history is neither sanitized nor distant; it is immediate, palpable, a presence in the air and on the surfaces you touch.

Stop 6: Saigon Opera House

Coordinates: 10.776390, 106.702780

Type/Specialty: Cultural Venue

Specific Examples: Stunning French colonial architecture, ballet performances, and musical shows.

Orientation: Standing just across the busy Lam Sơn Square, the best vantage point for this stop is facing the ornate façade of the building.

This building, designed by Félix Olivier under the supervision of Eugène Ferret and Ernest Guichard, was completed between 1898 and 1900. The façade’s delicate arches and sculpted balconies reveal a city that once embraced European artistic styles while holding onto its own vibrant identity. A story that captures the building’s layered history begins during World War In 1944, when Saigon was under Japanese occupation, the city became a target for Allied bombing raids. The scars of that time are part of the building’s fabric, silently marking the years when this cultural gem was caught in a far larger struggle. Today, the Opera House remains a symbol of Saigon ward’s evolving identity. Beyond its carved stone and gilded ironwork, this building quietly reflects the city’s capacity to embrace tradition and change. Behind its curtains, where once lawmakers shaped the nation, the pulse of Saigon continues to beat.

Stop 7: Ho Chi Minh City Hall (People's Committee Building)

Coordinates: 10.776470, 106.700970

Type/Specialty: Government Building

Specific Examples: French colonial architecture, beautifully lit at night, and the surrounding gardens.

Orientation: From here, the building’s light-toned stone façade rises nearly 30 meters, framed by the low green gardens that soften its formal edges.

The building before you, completed in 1908, was designed by Fernand Gardè in the Beaux-Arts style—a French architectural approach known for its classical lines and decorative flourishes. Originally called Hôtel de ville, it served as the municipal hall for the French colonial government. Its carefully balanced proportions and sculptural details stand as a record of the city’s colonial era, a physical imprint of a time when Saigon’s urban identity was shaped under French influence. The French Beaux-Arts architecture was more than stylistic—it represented the colonial administration’s intent to impose a civic order and identity. This administrative hall became the operational heart for French officials, organizing the city’s affairs in a way that had not existed before. As a result, the city was physically and bureaucratically reshaped, laying the groundwork for the governance challenges that followed.

During the Vietnam War, the building continued to function as an administrative center, serving as a backdrop to the city’s shifting control and political conflict. Its survival and continued use reflect the layers of political change the city has experienced. Today, it serves as the headquarters for the People’s Committee and People’s Council of Ho Chi Minh City, blending the colonial legacy with present-day governance. The building’s enduring presence connects the city’s colonial past to the future of its civic life. Inside the gardens, the scent of tropical flowers mingles with urban air, a subtle reminder of the city’s ongoing daily rhythms outside these walls. Its continued use through these eras makes it one of the few structures that witnessed the city’s transformation from colonial Saigon to the bustling metropolis of Ho Chi Minh City today.
