Stop 1: Notre-Dame Cathedral Basilica of Saigon

Coordinates: 10.779722, 106.699167

Type/Specialty: Historical Landmark

Specific Examples: Beautiful neo-Romanesque architecture, iconic twin towers, and a serene garden.

Orientation: This tour will take you from the iconic Notre-Dame Cathedral Basilica of Saigon to the lively Thi Bar Saigon, covering approximately 5 kilometers through a city that seamlessly merges its colonial past with modern life. Your first stop is Notre-Dame Cathedral Basilica of Saigon. From the main street of Nguyễn Huệ, head east towards Công xã Paris, then turn right. The cathedral will be on your left. You might notice the bustling atmosphere of the surrounding area. Standing at the intersection facing the cathedral’s main entrance, you have a full view of its soaring twin bell towers. These reach 58 meters high, framing the red brick walls that catch the sunlight with a stubborn glow.

The Notre-Dame Cathedral Basilica of Saigon was designed by Jules Bourard and completed in 1880, a landmark representing French colonial ambitions in Vietnam. Its construction began in 1877, replacing an earlier wooden church too small for the growing Catholic community. This new structure marked a deliberate statement by French administrators who originally called it the Church of Saigon (l’Église de Saïgon). The red bricks that clad the cathedral were imported all the way from Marseille, France, a detail that links this building directly to the distant Mediterranean coast and signals the extent of colonial networks at the time. Jules Bourard’s design included two bell towers that soar nearly 60 meters, a bold vertical gesture in the city skyline then. The choice of imported bricks was not only aesthetic but practical: they resisted Saigon’s tropical humidity better than local materials. This decision ensured the cathedral’s walls have preserved their distinctive red hue more than a century later, making it a vivid landmark amid the city’s ever-changing urban fabric. Another moment came in 1959 when the cathedral’s forecourt welcomed the statue of Our Lady of Peace. The name Notre-Dame Cathedral began to be used from this time, reflecting the installation of this statue as a new focal point. This addition shifted how the site was identified and perceived, linking the building’s identity more closely with Marian devotion. The statue’s presence offers a quiet contrast to the city’s bustle, its stone form weathered but serene, inviting a pause in the everyday rhythms of life outside. The cathedral’s presence captures the pulse of Saigon’s layered history—a place where colonial architecture, religious ritual, and local life continue to intersect.

Directions: From Notre-Dame Cathedral, walk south on Công xã Paris for about 200 meters. The post office will be directly across the street. The vibrant street life and colonial buildings create a charming backdrop. Continue to Saigon Central Post Office.

Stop 2: Saigon Central Post Office

Coordinates: 10.780000, 106.700000

Type/Specialty: Historic Post Office

Specific Examples: Impressive colonial architecture, a large mural of Vietnam's map, and vintage postal artifacts.

Orientation: Standing on the bustling edge of Paris Commune Square, your gaze meets the broad façade of the Saigon Central Post Office.

The Saigon Central Post Office, completed in 1891, stands as a monument to the communication needs of the late 19th century. Construction began in 1886 under the direction of architects Auguste Henri Vildieu and Marie-Alfred Foulhoux, whose design melds Gothic, Renaissance, and French colonial styles into a harmonious whole. Eiffel’s original structure laid the groundwork in both function and form, but the growing demands of the colonial administration required a new building. Vildieu and the project expanded this vision into a larger, more ornate space that could handle the surge in postal and telegraph traffic.

Inside, the high vaulted ceiling arches overhead, embellished with intricate ironwork that echoes the precision of the engineering. The inauguration of the building on 14 July 1891 was delayed until the return of the Governor General, the highest-ranking official in the colony, according to an architectural journal of the time. This delay marked the importance placed on the structure within the colonial administration. Its presence next to the cathedral signals how communication and faith were pillars of French influence in the city.

The Central Post Office is not merely a relic; it remains a living piece of Saigon’s urban fabric, bridging past and present through the daily exchange of letters and parcels. As you stand here, the hum of Saigon’s modern life flows past, yet within these walls, the echoes of 19th-century ambitions remain tangible—etched in iron, stone, and the steady rhythm of the postal service.

Directions: From Saigon Central Post Office, head east on Công xã Paris, then turn left onto Nguyễn Văn Bình. Walk straight for about 100 meters. You’ll see book stalls lining the street, creating a literary atmosphere. Continue to Nguyễn Văn Bình Book Street.

Stop 4: Independence Palace

Coordinates: 10.777050, 106.695370

Type/Specialty: Historic Site

Specific Examples: Beautiful gardens, historical rooms, and tanks from the Vietnam War.

Orientation: As you arrive at the wide entrance of the Independence Palace, the low, horizontal silhouette of the building stretches across a spacious courtyard. Here, the weight of history is visible in the sturdiness of the gates and the clean lines of the Vietnamese modernist structure, designed by Ngô Viết Thụ. The hum of city life mingles with the rustle of leaves in the gardens that frame the palace, anchoring this place of monumental decisions in the everyday pulse of Ho Chi Minh City.

This palace was completed on 31 October 1966, after construction began on 1 July 1962. Architect Ngô Viết Thụ designed it to replace an older palace that had been damaged by bombing. In November 1963, Diệm and his brother, Ngô Đình Nhu, were assassinated during a coup led by General Dương Văn Minh. This violent shift in power unfolded within the walls of the old palace and shaped the nation’s fate.

Years later, the palace witnessed its most dramatic moment. General Nguyễn Văn Thiệu, who had inaugurated the completed palace and used it as his office, fled the country just days before this event, leaving the palace to mark the closing chapter of a bitter conflict. Once a seat of power and tension, the palace now serves as a convention hall and museum. Its rooms echo with the decisions that shaped modern Vietnam, offering a vivid counterpoint to the city’s living present.

Directions: From Independence Palace, head east on Nam Kỳ Khởi Nghĩa, then turn left onto Võ Văn Tần. Walk for about 500 meters. The museum’s impactful displays can be seen from a distance. Continue to War Remnants Museum.

Stop 5: War Remnants Museum

Coordinates: 10.779500, 106.692000

Type/Specialty: Museum

Specific Examples: Exhibits on the Vietnam War, poignant photographs, and military equipment.

Orientation: Stand near the entrance at 28 Võ Văn Tần Street, where the broad facade rises with a sober presence. From here, the sounds of busy city life filter through—motorbikes humming, distant calls of street vendors—yet the building’s stoic exterior draws attention inward. The concrete walls bear a quiet weight, a threshold where history’s shadows begin to gather.

It was not the first attempt by the Vietnamese state to document the cruelties of foreign powers—the tradition stretches back to earlier exhibitions exposing French war crimes beginning in 1954—but this museum formalized and centralized that effort after reunification. The shift from an American information center to a museum of war evidence stands as a physical testament to the transformation of Saigon into Ho Chi Minh City. Over its 50-year history, the museum has received more than 25 million visitors, a fact that affirms its role as a site of memory and reckoning. This immense number of visitors reflects not only local interest but also a global curiosity in the stories held within these walls. The weight of history is palpable in the quiet corners and open courtyards, where the echoes of past violence meet the present’s steady pulse. Leaving behind this place, the city’s layers unfold differently—a modern art gallery or a bustling book street now awaits, offering a contrasting form of expression shaped by peace rather than war.

Directions: From War Remnants Museum, head west on Võ Văn Tần, then turn right onto Nam Kỳ Khởi Nghĩa. The opulence of the opera house stands out against the city skyline. Continue to Saigon Opera House — it is roughly 1.2 km (about a 15-minute walk) away.

Stop 6: Saigon Opera House

Coordinates: 10.776390, 106.702780

Type/Specialty: Performance Venue

Specific Examples: Stunning French colonial architecture, various performances, and a lively atmosphere.

Orientation: The best place to take in this building is just across the street, standing near the edge of Lam Sơn Square. From here, your eyes catch the full sweep of the opera house’s façade, its ornate arches and columns framed by the buzz of city life.

The Saigon Opera House opened its doors on January 1, 1900, a creation designed by Félix Olivier under the supervision of Eugène Ferret and Ernest Guichard. Its architecture reflects the flamboyant style favored in late 19th-century French theaters, one that aimed to transplant a slice of Parisian elegance into Saigon’s bustling center, now formally known as Saigon ward after the 2025 nationwide administrative reform. One chapter of this building’s life reveals a wartime transformation few notice today. In 1943, as the world was engulfed in war, French colonial authorities ordered the removal of statues and most of the decorative flourishes from this façade. Their goal was to make the building appear more youthful—perhaps a strategic attempt to project resilience or modernity amid the uncertainties of World War Instead of hosting performances, it became a seat of government, serving as legislative offices during the Republic of Vietnam era. This was a practical repurposing, transforming a cultural symbol into the heart of political life, before it returned again to the arts. The city’s heartbeat pulses through this building—its past, its present, and the layers that connect them.

Directions: From Saigon Opera House, head south on Lam Son Square, then turn left onto Lê Thánh Tôn. Walk for about 200 meters. The impressive architecture of the City Hall is a highlight as you approach. Continue to Ho Chi Minh City Hall (People's Committee Building).

Stop 7: Ho Chi Minh City Hall (People's Committee Building)

Coordinates: 10.776470, 106.700970

Type/Specialty: Government Building

Specific Examples: Magnificent façade, surrounding gardens, and historical significance.

Orientation: Position yourself to best view this location.

It served as the municipal hall during French colonial rule, a role that made it the administrative heart of Saigon at the time. One notable episode in its history is how it served as the government’s administrative hub not just during colonial times but continued its role through the Vietnam War era. This continuity marks the building as a silent witness to the city’s turbulent transformations — from French control to the Republic of Vietnam administration, and finally into the modern governance of Ho Chi Minh City. The gardens around it invite a moment of stillness amid the city’s rush, while the sound of motorbikes and voices occasionally drifts past, grounding this grand structure in the daily flow of a city that is Vietnam’s largest financial center, contributing around a quarter of the nation’s GDP. It anchors the administrative life of Ho Chi Minh City while the nearby Saigon River continues to carry goods and people, linking past and present in the city’s ceaseless movement.

Directions: Head southeast for roughly 400 meters, and you'll find yourself at Café Apartments, 42 Nguyễn Huệ. Enjoy your stroll!
