Stop 1: Notre-Dame Cathedral Basilica of Saigon

Coordinates: 10.779785, 106.699020

Type/Specialty: Historical Cathedral

Specific Examples: Stained glass windows, French colonial architecture, and the iconic statue of the Virgin Mary.

Orientation: This route stretches approximately 6 kilometers from the Notre-Dame Cathedral Basilica of Saigon to Thi Bar Saigon, traversing a cityscape marked by colonial architecture and the bustling energy of urban life. Your first stop is Notre-Dame Cathedral Basilica of Saigon. From the main street, walk towards Lê Duẩn, then turn right onto Công xã Paris. The cathedral will be on your left. Standing on the broad square before the cathedral, your gaze rises along the twin bell towers reaching 58 meters above the street, their red bricks catching the sunlight with a warm, earthy glow. The air carries faint echoes of bells and the murmurs of visitors mingling with the faint scent of incense from nearby street vendors.

The Notre-Dame Cathedral Basilica of Saigon began as a wooden church built in 1863 along the Charner Canal. To create a more impressive structure, the French administrators commissioned a grander stone cathedral designed by the architect Jules Bourard. Construction took place between 1877 and 1880, resulting in the edifice you see now, originally named the Church of Saigon (l’Église de Saïgon). The red bricks that make up its walls were imported from Marseille, France, serving as a tangible sign of colonial ambition and the desire to imprint French Catholic identity on Vietnamese soil.

In 1959, the cathedral was renamed Notre-Dame Cathedral when a statue of Our Lady of Peace was installed in the forecourt. This statue became a new focal point, symbolizing peace and anchoring the cathedral’s identity in the post-colonial cityscape. The two bell towers, standing 58 meters tall, continue to mark the passing hours, their toll blending with the rhythms of city life around the square. The echo of footsteps on stone, the rustle of pilgrims’ prayers, and the steady hum of the city remind us that this cathedral is both a monument to history and a living part of Ho Chi Minh City’s daily pulse.

Stop 2: Saigon Central Post Office

Coordinates: 10.779852, 106.699844

Type/Specialty: Historic Post Office

Specific Examples: Beautiful vaulted ceilings, antique wooden counters, and a large map of Vietnam.

Orientation: Standing near the arched entrance of the Saigon Central Post Office, your gaze is drawn upward to the soaring vaulted ceiling. The rhythmic echoes of footsteps and the faint rustle of letters being sorted mingle with the distinct scent of aged wood and ink.

The Saigon Central Post Office you see before you was constructed between 1886 and 1891, during the period when Vietnam was part of French Indochina. Designed by architect Auguste Henri Vildieu with assistance from Alfred Foulhoux, the building stands atop the site of an earlier structure: the Saigon Steel Wire Department, erected between 1860 and 1863. That earlier building, designed by Gustave Eiffel, marked a pivotal moment in Saigon’s urban development and laid the groundwork for modern communication infrastructure.

When the post office was built on this foundation, it inherited a legacy of connection and transmission. Inside the grand hall, the vaulted ironwork overhead reflects this lineage, while the rivets and beams carry the weight of decades of messages sent across continents. One remarkable episode dates back to 1864, when the first letters bearing the ‘stork’ stamp were dispatched from Saigon to the wider world. This was Vietnam’s inaugural postage stamp, chosen for its symbolism of travel and return, and it marked the beginning of Saigon’s integration into global postal networks by enabling reliable, standardized mail delivery.

The Saigon Central Post Office embodies these layers of history—a structure born from French colonial ambitions, rooted in a site where engineering innovation met the dawn of Vietnam’s modern postal system. The clatter of typewriters, the rustling of envelopes, and the quiet weight of the maps on the walls all bear witness to a story of communication evolving through time.

Stop 4: Independence Palace

Coordinates: 10.776994, 106.695305

Type/Specialty: Historical Site

Specific Examples: The main hall, war rooms, and the lush gardens.

Orientation: The palace’s clean, angular silhouette and its surrounding gardens mark a quiet stage that once bore witness to seismic moments in Vietnam’s mid-20th-century history.

You stand before the Independence Palace, a building whose calm, angular silhouette belies the dramatic history it witnessed. Construction began here in 1962, ordered by President Ngô Đình Diệm, but he never saw it completed—he was assassinated in a coup led by General Dương Văn Minh in November 1963. This palace was the center of power for the Republic of Vietnam until that moment, and it still holds remnants of the war rooms and the presidential helipad inside. The most defining event came on 30 April 1975, when a tank from the Liberation Army of South Vietnam crashed through these very gates. That moment marked the Fall of Saigon and the end of the Vietnam War, sealing the fate of the city and the nation. Now preserved as a museum, the palace stands as a powerful reminder of how political power and the lives of ordinary people intertwined here, shaping Vietnam’s future.

This building’s purpose was forged in the immediate aftermath of 1975, when it opened as the Exhibition House for U.S. and Puppet Crimes. That original name, recorded on September 4, 1975, reveals the museum’s initial mission: to document the impact of foreign intervention in Vietnam through a lens shaped by recent conflict. This was no neutral space but a deliberate recounting of war’s harsh realities, housed where the United States Information Agency once operated—an ironic reclamation of narrative authority.

Stop 6: Saigon Opera House

Coordinates: 10.776613, 106.703170

Type/Specialty: Cultural Venue

Specific Examples: Stunning exterior, grand staircase, and performances of traditional Vietnamese arts.

Orientation: The best place to take in this building’s full presence is from Lam Sơn Square, just across the street. From here, your eyes can travel up the ornate façade, catching the delicate ironwork and the carved details that crown the entrance.

From 1956 to 1967, this very Opera House was not just a place for performances, but the home of the unicameral National Assembly of South Vietnam. After 1967 and until 1975, it housed both the Lower House and the Senate, becoming the heart of the country's legislative power. In 1975, the building took on a new role as it hosted the People's Congress of Deputies of the Provisional Revolutionary Government of the Republic of South Vietnam. Standing here, you can feel the weight of history in the air, where debates and decisions once shaped the nation's future. The ornate façade and French colonial details remind you that this building has witnessed both cultural performances and the powerful drama of politics. This is a place where culture and power have long shared the stage, making the Saigon Opera House a symbol of Vietnam's complex history.

Stop 7: Ho Chi Minh City Hall (People's Committee Building)

Coordinates: 10.776543, 106.700910

Type/Specialty: Government Building

Specific Examples: French colonial style, beautifully landscaped gardens, and illuminated at night.

The story of this building begins with its construction during the French colonial period, from 1898 to 1908, and its official opening in 1909. Designed by architect Fernand Gardè, it originally served as the Hôtel de ville, or municipal hall, for Saigon. Its Beaux-Arts style, with classical lines and elaborate ornamentation, reflects the influence of the colonial era and marks it as a seat of authority. Over the decades, the building has witnessed Saigon’s transformation from a colonial city to a wartime capital, and finally to the bustling metropolis it is today. What was once the City Hall now houses the Ho Chi Minh City People's Committee, the executive branch of the local government, linking modern governance with its layered past. For over a century, visitors could only admire this emblem of power from the outside. That changed on April 30, 2023, when the People's Committee Building opened its doors to the public for the first time, welcoming guests inside halls that had long been closed off.

Bạch Đằng Pier once served as a key boat quay and ferry terminal, linking this side of the Saigon River directly to Thủ Thiêm Pier. The worn concrete and metal fixtures here retain the patina of continual use, while the air carries faint traces of diesel, river water, and tropical greenery from the nearby banks. The physical presence of the pier evokes the networks where traders, travelers, and locals mingled, their footfalls and cargo loading marking rhythms along the river’s edge for decades.

Bạch Đằng Pier forms a tangible crossing between urban life and historical exploration.
