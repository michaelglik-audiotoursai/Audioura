Stop 1: The artist is in his studio.

Address: 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3399, -71.0942

Orientation: You are about to explore the Museum of Fine Arts in Boston, a treasure trove of artistic expression and cultural exchange. Your first stop is The artist is in his studio... The faint gleam of oil paint on panel draws the eye even across the softly lit gallery. Stand just far enough back from Rembrandt’s Artist in his Studio at the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, so the entire studio interior and both figures are visible in a single glance. From this vantage, the scale of the easel, the presence of the artist, and the subtle transitions in color become clear, and the physical relationship between painter and creation emerges in full.

In 1629, Rembrandt was working in his studio, facing the challenge of capturing the atmosphere and detail that would set his paintings apart. Because he made these choices, art historians now confidently recognize The Artist in his Studio as his work, even though he never signed it. This oil painting on panel demonstrates deliberate control over shadow and light. The surface shows early mastery: the artist stands to the left, pausing before a towering easel on the right, his hands empty, contemplating rather than painting. The emptiness of the easel’s canvas and the modest size of the painter’s figure compared to the structure around him reflect a sense of beginnings — of uncertainty and possibility — that characterized Rembrandt’s early career. Rembrandt’s resolve to set himself apart through technique was shared by the Museum of Fine Arts when it began its own journey, first opening on the top floor of the Boston Athenæum, one of the oldest independent libraries. By acquiring The Artist in his Studio, the museum built its collection of Netherlandish art, which also includes paintings Rubens, Frans Hals, Anthony van Dyck, and Rachel Ruysch. The collection continues to grow and change, reflecting the ambition and labor involved in creating something new. Just as Rembrandt’s early experiments sparked a lifetime of transformation in painting, the museum’s beginnings also illustrate this ongoing evolution.

Stop 2: Madame Monet wearing a kimono

Address: 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3399, -71.0942

Orientation: Standing at least three paces back from “Madame Monet wearing a kimono,” align yourself with the center of the canvas. From this vantage, the full height—over two meters—of Camille Doncieux as she stands in the extravagant red kimono comes into perspective, and the scattered Japanese fans behind her resolve into a distinct, rhythmic pattern across the expanse of the oil on canvas.

Camille Doncieux is shown enveloped in a scarlet uchikake, a formal Japanese bridal robe, its surface glimmering with gold embroidery and circular motifs. Behind her, a field of fans spreads across the neutral background, each rendered with a flatness inspired by the ukiyo-e woodblock prints that fascinated Paris in the 1870s. The pose is both playful and staged, her European features framed by the symbolic garments of another world. The brushwork oscillates between careful rendering and evocations of texture, with the surface shimmering where paint piles up to mimic silk.

The painting’s structure recalls the academic figure painting format, a gesture toward tradition that was adopted at the second Impressionist exhibition. “La Japonaise” was exhibited at that second Impressionist exhibition in 1876, with its ambitious scale and academic approach chosen deliberately to capture public attention at a time when Monet's financial circumstances were just beginning to improve. Camille agreed to pose in this elaborate costume, performing a vision of Japonisme that reflected the fashion and cultural curiosity of the era.

The Museum of Fine Arts later acquired the work for its collection. Organizers invited visitors to dress up in a kimono in front of the painting “La Japonaise,” linking the experience of viewing the canvas to the material act of wearing such a garment. The painting stands as a record of cross-cultural exchange, and its presence in this gallery reflects the museum’s own journey—gathering works shaped by encounters and borrowings, never static, always shifting.

Continue to Watson and the Shark.

Stop 3: Watson and the Shark
Address: 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115
Coordinates: 42.3399, -71.0942
Orientation: As you stand here, position yourself just to the left of center before Watson and the Shark—Copley’s 1782 oil painting—so that the energy of the rescue scene draws your eye in a diagonal from water to boat, and the faces of both rescuer and victim become visible. From this angle, the black sailor’s outstretched arm and the boat hook aimed at the shark’s jaws come into full relief, and you can see the way the grouping of figures compresses the tension into the composition’s vertical thrust. In 1749, 14-year-old Brook Watson was attacked by a shark while swimming in Havana Harbor and lost his right leg below the knee. He was not rescued until the third attempt, which is the dramatic moment shown in this painting by John Singleton Copley. About twenty- the attack, Watson, who had become a successful merchant and military man, commissioned Copley to capture the event. Watson later commissioned Copley to create a second version of the painting for himself, a gesture that ensured the story of his rescue remained anchored not only in public memory but also in his own private collection. Copley revised these figures during the painting process, resulting in a more diverse and dramatic rescue scene; this change is still visible today through infrared analysis, tying the painting to the artist’s evolving vision. The painting itself is oil on canvas, standing out for its tight, theatrical arrangement: the boat crowded with sailors, each body twisted in urgent movement. The composition’s vertical format and dense figural grouping set this version apart from Copley’s earlier, more horizontal attempts. There’s a palpable unease to standing before this canvas. The faces in the boat—fear, determination, a flicker of hope—echo other moments you’ve seen today, such as Madame Monet wearing a kimono, where surface beauty veils something precarious beneath. In Watson and the Shark, the stakes are life and death, rendered in oil and motion, and the possibility of rescue is never certain until the last second.
Directions: Proceed to Dance at Bougival.

Stop 4: Dance at Bougival

Address: 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3399, -71.0942

Orientation: Stand several paces back from Dance at Bougival so the full height of the canvas fills your sight. From this distance, the life-size dancers step almost into the gallery, making their movement as immediate as the hush you bring with you from Watson and the Shark just a room ago.

The painting shows two nearly life-size figures caught mid-spin beneath chestnut trees in Bougival, a riverside town outside Paris. The woman’s rose-trimmed straw hat tilts above her flushed face as she looks past her partner, whose blue jacket and deep-brimmed hat suggest both the formality and verve of the Parisian dance hall. Under Renoir's brush, swift strokes of pink and white evoke the swirl of her skirt, while the bustle of the crowd is hinted at by blurred faces and dabs of color behind them. He chose long, loose brushstrokes to capture the movement and brilliance of the moment, achieving impressionist luminosity of color while preserving a strong modeling of flesh and fabric.

Suzanne Valadon, then seventeen and already supporting herself as a model and aspiring artist, posed as the woman at left. Her participation gave the painting its directness and flesh-and-blood immediacy. In May of that year, Renoir showed this painting along with seventy others in his solo exhibition. The warm reception to Dance at Bougival and its companion canvases marked a turning point: Renoir’s decision to balance impressionist color with careful modeling of form made possible new approaches to painting life on this scale, for himself and others.

Directions: Continue to Bocca Baciata.

Stop 5: Bocca Baciata

Address: 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3399, -71.0942

Orientation: Stand to the left of Bocca Baciata, just far enough back that the full height of the panel comes into view. From this angle, the format of the composition becomes clear—one figure, filling the frame, her presence both intimate and central. The fall of light on the panel’s surface, especially in the lower left, reveals the care taken in building up the flesh tones: the result of a painter’s challenge, not merely a model’s pose.

Dante Gabriel Rossetti had long struggled to capture the quality of flesh in his paintings, believing his technique suffered from a fault common among Pre-Raphaelite artists. That same year, George Price Boyce commissioned Rossetti to create Bocca Baciata. The medium is oil paint, layered on panel with a density and warmth distinct from the looser, lighter touch seen in some works you have already encountered, such as Dance at Bougival. Here, the single subject’s form is built from deliberate, patient brushwork, each stroke intended to conjure the fullness of living flesh rather than an idealized type. Rossetti’s focus on the surface of the skin, with its subtle transitions and blushes, creates a sense of nearness. The painting’s scale—neither grand nor miniature—forces a private proximity. Bocca Baciata marked a turning point in Rossetti’s career, and the work now hangs in the Museum of Fine Arts as a record of that change. The painting stands as the artist’s reply to years of self-doubt, a declaration not only of technique but of presence. In this room, the centuries accumulate in flesh and gaze, shaped by the choices and changes of those who stood behind the canvas.

Directions: Your final stop in Museum of Fine Arts: Fray Hortensio Félix Paravicino.

Stop 6: Fray Hortensio Félix Paravicino

Address: 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3399, -71.0942

Orientation: Directly in front of El Greco’s “Portrait of Fray Hortensio Félix Paravicino,” angled just enough to let the light reveal both the texture of the paint and the subtle geometry of the sitter’s habit, the relationship between painter and subject becomes physically present: each brushstroke is visible as a record of their encounter.

Paravicino, a monk of the Trinitarian Order and a major Spanish poet, sits for El Greco as both a man of faith and a celebrated literary figure. The deliberate choice of medium—oil applied in a way that privileges luminosity and expressive form—signals the style of El Greco’s late period, where anatomy and fabric are rendered less for naturalism than for spiritual resonance. Paravicino was not only a monk but also a key voice in the literary movement of Culteranismo, a style defined by complex metaphor and ornate language. In a gesture of friendship and memorial, Paravicino published a volume of poems in 1641 that included four sonnets dedicated to El Greco’s memory. This act gave the painter’s reputation a lasting place in Spanish letters, ensuring El Greco’s name continued to circulate among poets and intellectuals. El Greco, who had built a career in Spain after training in Italy and being born in Crete, painted this portrait during a period when his work increasingly focused on close personal relationships, capturing both likeness and inner life. Another episode unfolds in the sitter’s own story: Paravicino, as a renowned orator, brought the ideals of the Trinitarian Order and the innovations of Culteranismo into the public sphere, shaping the discourse of his era. His dual identity—as the subject of a late El Greco portrait and as an active poet and preacher—marks a convergence of art and literature, religious devotion and intellectual ambition. The painting’s presence in this museum speaks to a lineage of creative affinities and transformations—linking the reflective solitude of Paravicino with the sensual immediacy you encountered at Bocca Baciata. Here, the cost was time: a friendship preserved in paint and poetry, and a moment of contemplation made visible to all who stand before it.

The museum moved to its current Fenway location in 1909.

This tour reveals the interplay between artistic technique and personal narrative, as seen in works like Bocca Baciata. Such connections remind us that art not only reflects individual journeys but also shapes the cultural dialogues that continue to evolve within our shared history.
