Step-by-Step Audio Guided Tour: Musée d'Orsay, Paris, France - Museum Tour

Stop 1: Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe (Luncheon on the Grass)

Address: Musée d'Orsay, 1 Rue de la Légion d'Honneur, 75007 Paris, France

Coordinates: 48.881207, 2.328608

Museum Information:

The museum is open Tuesday to Sunday from 9:30 to 6:00, and closed on Mondays.

Le Déjeuner sur l’herbe, painted by Édouard Manet in 1863, is a large oil on canvas that depicts a picnic scene in the countryside. The figures are rendered with sharply defined outlines and areas of looser brushwork, emphasizing the flatness of the canvas rather than creating an illusion of three-dimensional space. This compositional choice disrupts the expectations of academic painting, which favored detailed modeling and atmospheric perspective to suggest depth. By placing his figures in a contemporary setting while referencing Renaissance compositions, Manet unsettles traditional narratives and the idealization of the nude, presenting a candid and modern tableau.

At the time, Manet was navigating tensions between academic traditions and emerging modernist approaches. Le Déjeuner sur l’herbe marked a turning point in his career, as he rejected historical and mythological subjects in favor of contemporary life. The painting reflects Manet’s desire to provoke and redefine artistic norms during a period when official art institutions mandated strict rules about subject matter and technique. The direct gaze of the nude woman, combined with the casual poses of the men, creates a tension between familiarity and estrangement that lingers in the viewer’s perception. The painting evokes a complex curiosity rooted in its refusal to conform, inviting awareness of the social conventions and artistic expectations Manet confronted, as well as the ambiguity in the relationships among the figures.

Le Déjeuner sur l’herbe was rejected by the Paris Salon in 1863 and was instead displayed at the Salon des Refusés, an alternative exhibition created for works rejected by the official Salon. The painting’s medium of oil on canvas allowed Manet to explore contrasts in texture and light with immediacy and boldness, embodying a moment when painting began to break free from established conventions.

Monet’s interpretation, produced shortly after Manet’s original, is held in the Musée d’Orsay’s permanent collection and demonstrates the painting’s influence on the evolution from Realism toward Impressionism. This connection situates Manet’s work as a pivotal moment in the broader trajectory of nineteenth-century French art. The painting arrived in the Musée d’Orsay as part of the national collection, where it anchors the museum’s narrative of nineteenth-century innovation.

Louis Broder commissioned limited editions of prints inspired by Manet’s work, insisting on close collaboration between artist, poet, and printer to revive the tradition of artist’s books. These editions, produced in Mourlot’s workshop on Rue de Chabrol in Paris, maintained a dialogue with Manet’s legacy. This chain of decisions highlights the continuing resonance of Le Déjeuner sur l’herbe beyond its original canvas, extending its influence into multiple artistic media and collections.

Stop 2: La Nuit étoilée (Starry Night Over the Rhône)

Address: Musée d'Orsay, 1 Rue de la Légion d'Honneur, 75007 Paris, France

Coordinates: 48.8606, 2.3266

Orientation: Look for Starry Night Over the Rhône (La Nuit étoilée) in the galleries.

Vincent van Gogh painted *Starry Night Over the Rhône* in September 1888 while living in Arles, just a short walk from the Yellow House where he was renting a room. He chose a spot on the riverbank to capture the reflections of gas lighting on the water, exploring the contrast between artificial light and the natural stars above. The composition is anchored by the reflections of yellow and blue hues on the river’s surface, capturing the fleeting effects of both artificial and celestial light—a pursuit that prefigured Impressionism’s fascination with transient moments. Near the bottom of the canvas, two lovers stroll quietly on the riverbank, their presence lending a tranquil, intimate atmosphere that contrasts with the vastness of the night sky above. This painting is part of Van Gogh’s sustained effort to portray the nocturnal world, a pursuit he undertook despite the physical difficulty of working outdoors at night. The emotional tenor of the scene balances awe and quiet companionship, rooted in Van Gogh’s attempt to connect human presence with the expansive cosmos. Here, light acts as both a poetic subject and an expressive device, defining the surface of the water and the distant cityscape, and drawing the viewer’s gaze across the canvas’s depth.

Stop 3: L'Église d'Auvers-sur-Oise (The Church at Auvers)

Address: Musée d'Orsay, 1 Rue de la Légion d'Honneur, 75007 Paris, France

Coordinates: 48.8606, 2.3266

Orientation: Stand close enough to take in the full height and width of Vincent van Gogh’s L'Église d'Auvers-sur-Oise, and angle your gaze to catch the church’s rear façade, or chevet, which faces you nearly head-on. From this vantage, you grasp how Van Gogh’s brushwork and color choices pull your eye along the building’s stark lines and into the sprawling sky above, a detail easily lost from other viewpoints.

Vincent van Gogh had spent time in Arles and then in the hospital at Saint-Rémy-de-Provence, longing for the north he remembered from his youth. In May 1890, he moved to Auvers-sur-Oise, just outside Paris, and in June he painted The Church at Auvers, drawing on memories and feelings he described in letters to his sister Wilhelmina and his brother Theo. Because he painted it during these final weeks, the work reflects the nostalgia and the sense of longing that filled his last days before his death on July 29, 1890. After Van Gogh died, the painting eventually came to hang in the Musée d'Orsay in Paris. Because it is shown here, visitors can see for themselves the deep blue and the diverging paths that Van Gogh wrote about, preserved in oil and canvas for generations after his passing. The church’s rear, or chevet, faces us, framed by a vivid cerulean sky streaked with swirling azure and cobalt strokes. The church’s walls, painted in contrasting whites and ochres, tilt slightly, lending the structure a restless energy that resonates with Van Gogh’s turbulent state. His brushstrokes here are thick and rhythmic, a technique called impasto, layering paint in a way that emphasizes texture and movement. This method transcends mere representation, conveying not just the building’s shape but the intensity of the artist’s perception, creating a dialogue between the tangible and the emotional. Vincent Van Gogh was in the final chapter of his life when he painted this church. After a turbulent period in the South of France, including his stay in Arles and hospitalization in Saint-Rémy-de-Provence, he sought proximity to his brother Theo and a fresh start near Paris. While in Auvers-sur-Oise, he produced approximately 70 works in just ten weeks, a prolific burst that included this painting. L'Église d'Auvers-sur-Oise stands as a visual echo of the artist’s personal and artistic struggles, where the solidity of the church contrasts with the restless sky, reflecting Van Gogh’s oscillation between hope and despair. This painting shares a subtle, emotional thread with the Starry Night Over the Rhône you admired earlier: both depict night skies alive with motion and color, but here the church grounds Van Gogh’s cosmic vision in a human place. The diverging paths in front of the church, which Van Gogh mentioned in his letters, symbolize choices and passage, echoing the crossroads of his own life. The work’s tension between architectural solidity and natural turbulence compels a quiet contemplation of impermanence and longing. The painting is a vivid chapter in the museum’s narrative of Post-Impressionism, offering an intimate counterpoint to the nocturnal wonders you viewed in Starry Night Over the Rhône. A lesser-known fact about this piece’s journey is that Van Gogh’s brother Theo, who supported Vincent emotionally and financially, played a crucial role in promoting his work after his death. Theo’s widow preserved these paintings, including L'Église d'Auvers-sur-Oise, eventually enabling them to enter public collections like this one. This stewardship ensured that Van Gogh’s intense final creations would survive beyond the tragedy that shadowed his last days—and it’s these works that now invite close study here, decades later.

Stop 4: L'Origine du monde

Address: Musée d'Orsay, 1 Rue de la Légion d'Honneur, 75007 Paris, France

Coordinates: 48.8606, 2.3266

Type/Specialty: Peinture

Orientation: Stand close in front of L’Origine du monde, at eye level with the canvas, to fully engage with the intimate scale and precise detail of the painting’s surface, which a more distant view would diminish. From this vantage, the texture of the oil paint and the sculptural quality of the forms become visible, revealing Courbet’s deliberate choice to focus tightly on the subject’s body, refusing any distraction.

L’Origine du monde is a relatively small canvas, measuring approximately 46 by 55 centimeters, but its scale intensifies the immediacy of the subject. Courbet’s technique is marked by meticulous rendering of flesh tones and textures, with oily brushstrokes shaping the soft curves of skin and subtle shadows that model the form. The painting’s tight framing excludes any facial features or setting, placing all attention on the natural body itself—unidealized and unapologetic.

At the time he created this work, Courbet was deeply invested in radical realism, a movement that sought to depict the world unfiltered by academic conventions or mythological allegories. Even within his own body of work, this painting stands apart for its explicitness and refusal to cloak the female body in symbolic meaning or narrative. Courbet’s choice to paint this subject so directly marked a turning point in his career toward a confrontational realism that rejected idealization. Such frank depictions were considered scandalous, and as a result, this work remained hidden for decades because of its provocative nature.

Standing before L’Origine du monde, one confronts the shock that Courbet intended—a raw, unmediated presence of the female body that unsettled norms and forced a reckoning with the material reality of flesh. This realism is a rupture from earlier works like Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe, which also challenged social and artistic mores but retained a broader narrative and social context. Here, Courbet’s focus is unyielding and elemental.

After Jacques Lacan died in 1981, his family faced a substantial inheritance tax bill. The French Minister of Economy and Finances agreed to accept L’Origine du monde itself as payment for the taxes, using a legal mechanism called “dation en paiement.” Because of this transfer, finalized in 1995, the painting left the Lacan family’s private collection and entered the public collection at the Musée d'Orsay, where it is now held. The painting’s presence in the Musée d’Orsay enriches the collection’s narrative of the transition from Realism to Impressionism, highlighting how Courbet’s unvarnished depictions of reality opened a path for later artists to explore light, atmosphere, and perception without the constraints of academic idealization. L’Origine du monde is not only a confrontation with the body but also a moment that bridges artistic traditions, marking a bold step toward the modern explorations that define this museum’s holdings.

Stop 5: Bal du moulin de la Galette

Address: Musée d'Orsay, 1 Rue de la Légion d'Honneur, 75007 Paris, France

Coordinates: 48.887398, 2.337129

Orientation: Stand slightly to the right of the painting’s center, where the dappled sunlight filtering through the painted treetops becomes most legible.

The composition teems with life: men and women mingle beneath a canopy of leaves, their figures rendered with loose brushwork that suggests movement rather than stillness. The painting is oil on canvas, its surface lively with textured brushstrokes that capture both the solidity of figures and the flickering of light through leaves. This method allows the eye to blend colors and shapes, evoking the transient quality of light and the informal atmosphere of the gathering.

At this moment in Renoir’s career, he was immersed in Impressionism’s pursuit of capturing modern life through light and color. This painting marked a turning point by emphasizing social interaction and joy within a natural setting, a departure from the more serious or static subjects of his past. The scene conveys a sense of communal pleasure and vitality, the kind of joy found in shared moments away from work and routine. It reflects not only the physical act of dancing and dining but also the social fabric of late 19th-century Paris, where public leisure spaces like the Moulin de la Galette became stages for urban conviviality.

Renoir’s choice of medium and technique was essential to expressing the ephemeral quality of this festive moment. The challenge for him was to render a complex, crowded scene without losing individuality in the figures or coherence in the whole. Bal du moulin de la Galette serves as a luminous record of Parisian social life, the shifting patterns of light echoing Renoir’s exploration of modernity. This approach paved the way for future artists to explore the subjective perception of time and atmosphere in their work.

Stop 6: Une moderne Olympia (A Modern Olympia)

Coordinates: 48.870167, 2.328375

Orientation: Stand a few steps back from Une moderne Olympia to take in the full scale of Cézanne’s brushwork and composition, allowing the figure’s pose and the stark contrasts of light and shadow to register fully. From this vantage, you can observe how Cézanne reinterprets Manet’s original subject with a deliberate restraint that might be lost at closer range.

The woman lies nude, confronting the viewer with a gaze less provocative and more detached, her figure articulated through broad, simplified planes of muted tones. The technique marks a transition from Impressionist spontaneity toward a structural exploration of volume and composition, laying groundwork for modernism’s concern with form and perspective. The painting’s materiality—oil on canvas—carries Cézanne’s signature brushstrokes, emphasizing the solidity of the body rather than its surface allure. At this moment in his career, Cézanne was distancing himself from the fleeting impressions of his contemporaries, including those of Manet, whom he both admired and challenged. Moderne Olympia was part of Cézanne’s ongoing effort to redefine the nude, abstracting the figure into geometric shapes, moving away from the scandalous intimacy of Manet’s original to something more architectural and contemplative. The emotional register here is one of reflection rather than confrontation. The figure’s immobilized stillness and Cézanne’s methodical brushwork invite a sober consideration of the human body as an object of study, not spectacle. The work embodies a quiet resistance to the sensationalism that surrounded Manet’s Olympia, contained within a compositional rigor that anticipates the fragmentation. It resonates with the presence of L’Origine du monde, which you passed earlier, both challenging traditional representations of the nude with their frankness and modernity.
