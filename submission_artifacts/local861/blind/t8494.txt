Step-by-Step Audio Guided Tour: Rijksmuseum, Amsterdam, Netherlands
Tour-Category: museum

Stop 1: Winterlandschap met ijsvermaak (Winter landscape with skaters)

Address: Museumstraat 1, 1071 XX Amsterdam, Netherlands

Coordinates: 52.36, 4.8852

The museum is open daily from 9:00 to 17:00.

Orientation: You are about to explore the Rijksmuseum in Amsterdam, dedicated to Dutch arts and history. Your first stop is Winterlandschap met ijsvermaak (Winter landscape with skaters). Stand slightly to the right of the painting’s center, where the frozen river’s broad expanse unfolds beneath a pale, clouded sky. From this vantage point, the intricate details of the figures’ activities become distinct—their gestures, groupings, and the subtle variations in winter play that might otherwise blur together from a direct head-on view.

Hendrick Avercamp’s Winter landscape with skaters (Winterlandschap met ijsvermaak), painted around 1608, is his largest and most ambitious winter scene. Measuring 77.3 by 131.9 centimeters and rendered in oil on oak panel, the painting reveals Avercamp’s pioneering role as one of the earliest Dutch painters to represent winter and its leisure activities. The composition offers a broad panorama of a frozen river crowded with people engaged in diverse pastimes—not only skating alone or in pairs, but also playing kolf, a game resembling ice hockey, as well as socializing and walking.

At just 23 years old, Avercamp created this work as an early landmark in his career during the Dutch Golden Age of painting. He adapted compositional elements reminiscent of Pieter Bruegel, whose expansive crowd scenes Avercamp reinterpreted for a northern winter setting. His focus was to depict not only the landscape but also the human experience within it—a snapshot of daily life where nature and society intersect. The choice of oil on oak allowed Avercamp to achieve a crispness and permanence in the cool, muted palette, capturing both the chill of the season and the warmth of communal life on ice.

The scene evokes nostalgia through its detailed portrayal of communal winter recreation, a fleeting moment when the frozen landscape became a stage for social interaction and sport. Avercamp’s delicate brushwork and compositional breadth invite reflection on how leisure shaped art’s evolving focus on everyday life. The painting’s presence here at the Rijksmuseum situates it among other works that define the Dutch Golden Age, a period when landscape and genre painting flourished together, reflecting societal values that shaped art’s future directions.

Directions: Continue through Rijksmuseum — next is View of Houses in Delft.

Stop 2: Gezicht op huizen in Delft, bekend als ‘Het straatje’ (The Little Street)

Address: Museumstraat 1, 1071 XX Amsterdam, Netherlands

Coordinates: 52.36, 4.8852

Orientation: Stand directly before the canvas to take in the quiet lane that Vermeer painted from the window of his own home. From this vantage, you can discern the tactility of the brickwork and the subtle variations of natural light that illuminate each surface—effects that would be lost if viewed from a distance or at an angle.

Johannes Vermeer lived and worked in Delft in the seventeenth century. Around 1658, he painted View of Houses in Delft, also known as ‘The Little Street.’ In this work, Vermeer chose an unusual subject for him: a quiet street scene. He painted from a window in his home, which allowed him to study the interplay of shadow and light on the modest facades opposite. Using oil on canvas, he applied thick paint to the walls and brickwork, making them almost palpable and giving the bricks a textured, three-dimensional quality.

This painting stands out as one of only two known cityscapes in Vermeer’s entire body of work. Unlike the bustling cityscapes common in that era, Vermeer focused deliberately on the intimate and unremarkable, elevating the everyday environment. The painting’s name, ‘The Little Street,’ refers to a narrow alley in Delft—a detail Vermeer preserved with quiet reverence. A 1696 auction catalogue records ‘The Little House’ as one of at least three cityscapes by Vermeer, highlighting how rare it was for him to produce urban views. The stillness of the scene holds the weight of centuries, inviting a moment of quiet observation amidst the museum’s broader collection.

Directions: Next: The Threatened Swan (De bedreigde zwaan).

Stop 3: De bedreigde zwaan (The Threatened Swan)

Address: Museumstraat 1, 1071 XX Amsterdam, Netherlands

Coordinates: 52.36, 4.8852

Orientation: Stand slightly to the left and at eye level with The Threatened Swan (De bedreigde zwaan). From this vantage, you can fully absorb the swan’s threatening posture and its commanding presence against the muted landscape behind, details that recede when viewed head-on.

Jan Asselijn’s oil painting from around 1650 draws you into a moment charged with tension. On this large canvas, measuring 144 by 171 centimeters, a mute swan stands in full defense, wings spread wide and neck arched protectively over its nest. Its open beak signals readiness to strike at an unseen dog, the implied aggressor in this standoff. Asselijn uses oil on canvas to render the swan’s feathers with softness, which contrasts sharply against the darker, stormy background and heightens the mood.

This was no ordinary wildlife portrayal; the painting became a political allegory at a turbulent time in the Netherlands’ history. Later, the museum’s director Van de Weyer insisted on displaying the painting prominently, securing its place as a visual emblem of national protection. His effort ensured that visitors would recognize the painting not only as a naturalistic scene but also as a statement on vigilance and the costs of safeguarding sovereignty. The swan’s defiant posture evokes both the fragility and fierce protection of what matters most—nature, home, country—an idea that ripples through the Netherlands’ artistic and political landscape during the mid-17th century.

Directions: Proceed to The Milkmaid.

Stop 4: Het melkmeisje (The Milkmaid)

Address: Museumstraat 1, 1071 XX Amsterdam, Netherlands

Coordinates: 52.375917, 4.883463

Orientation: Stand close enough to see the brushwork yet step back slightly to take in the full figure of The Milkmaid, positioned centrally on the canvas. From this vantage, the subtle gradations of light on her face and the textures of her clothing become legible, revealing Vermeer’s delicate balance between the mundane and the monumental in this kitchen scene.

The Milkmaid is an oil-on-canvas painting, measuring roughly 45.5 by 41 centimeters. It depicts a kitchen maid pouring milk from a jug, caught in a moment of quiet concentration. The composition is tightly focused, with the figure occupying the central space against a plain wall, emphasizing her physical presence and the tactile reality of her work. The texture of the bread on the table, the coarse fabric of the maid’s apron, and the soft fall of light across her face all demonstrate Vermeer’s subtle mastery of light and material.

At this moment in his life, Vermeer was in the final phase of his early career, refining his approach to light and composition. "The Milkmaid" is often considered one of the last works of this formative period before he moved toward more complex narratives and interiors. An art historian remarked on the painting’s ability to combine dignity and tenderness, noting that it transforms a modest domestic servant into a figure of quiet strength—a tribute to ordinary labor. The painting’s emotional resonance lies in this elevation of the everyday: the steady, unassuming work of the maid becomes a subject worthy of attention and admiration. It captures a pause in time that feels both intimate and universal, making the viewer aware of the beauty in focused, humble effort.

"The Milkmaid" connects to the Rijksmuseum’s broader collection by exemplifying the Dutch Golden Age’s interest in domestic scenes and the transformative use of light, a theme echoed in several works you have already encountered, such as the detailed houses in Delft or the frozen winter landscape. This painting also invites observation of how global influences and trade during the 17th century subtly shaped Dutch art through materials and techniques.

Because of a debate, the government decided to buy the work for the Rijksmuseum. As a result, "The Milkmaid" remained in the Netherlands, where it can be seen today.

Continue to De grote golf bij Kanagawa (The Great Wave off Kanagawa).

Stop 5: De grote golf bij Kanagawa (The Great Wave off Kanagawa)

Address: Museumstraat 1, 1071 XX Amsterdam, Netherlands

Coordinates: 52.36, 4.8852

Orientation: Stand close to the print, near eye level, so you can observe how Hokusai’s precision in carving the woodblocks reveals every crest and curl of the wave. From this vantage point, the interplay of line and color emerges fully—the deep blues of the sea contrast sharply with the white foam, and Mount Fuji appears almost fragile beneath the towering wave.

This work uses a multi-block printing technique, carefully layering shades of blue—known as aizuri-e—by carving separate blocks for each color. This process creates the dynamic movement and depth of the ocean, and the technique’s precision is visible in the wave’s rhythmic curves and the fishermen’s taut boats, underscoring the print’s complex craftsmanship. The delicate gradations of Prussian blue pigment, first synthesized in Germany and imported from Europe, highlight the fusion of materials and ideas across continents.

The image illustrates the tension between human vulnerability and nature’s overwhelming power. The fishermen, dwarfed and nearly swallowed by the wave, embody endurance amid chaos, while Mount Fuji’s steadfastness suggests permanence and order. Because this method allowed for mass production, the image became accessible beyond elite circles and deeply embedded in popular culture.

The Rijksmuseum holds this print as part of its extensive collection dedicated to arts and history. Its presence here connects to the museum’s role in preserving and displaying art that illustrates how international artistic currents converged with Dutch art traditions.

Your final stop in Rijksmuseum: A Militiaman Holding a Berkemeyer, Known as the ‘Merry Drinker’.

Stop 6: A Militiaman Holding a Berkemeyer

Address: Museumstraat 1, 1071 XX Amsterdam, Netherlands

Coordinates: 52.36, 4.8852

A Militiaman Holding a Berkemeyer, Known as the ‘Merry Drinker’ is an oil-on-canvas painting by Frans Hals, a Dutch painter of lively portraits, created around 1628 to 1630. Measuring 81 by 66.5 centimeters, the work captures a moment of conviviality: a young Dutch militiaman holds up a Berkemeyer, a drinking glass with a wide, flared bowl common in the Netherlands during the 17th century, as if inviting a toast. The vividness of the militiaman’s flushed face and the gleaming metal buttons of his attire emerge from a dark background, emphasizing the subject’s joyful presence.

This painting embodies the social vibrancy of celebrating life’s simple pleasures—a theme that resonates with the broader collection here, which highlights daily life and civic pride. At the time Hals painted this piece, he was well established in Haarlem and known for his portraits of local militia groups. This work reflects a turning point in his career, where he moved beyond formal group portraits to capture individuals with a more relaxed, animated approach. His ability to suggest movement and personality through economical brushstrokes was becoming a defining feature of his style.

Hals’s treatment of the militiaman is less about strict formality and more about capturing a moment of human warmth and humor, which marked a shift in how portraiture could engage viewers. The painting conveys a sense of joy grounded in real human interaction—the militiaman’s inviting gesture and candid smile reveal a moment of shared camaraderie, an antidote to the rigidity often associated with official portraiture of the period. This warmth reflects Dutch cultural values of sociability and community, where public service in the militia was both duty and occasion for celebration.

The story behind this painting involves the way Hals’s portraits of militiamen served both as personal commemorations and public affirmations of civic identity. His dynamic approach allowed these portraits to break from stiff formal conventions, making the figures appear approachable and human. The Rijksmuseum’s collection, including this work, preserves the legacy of that cultural moment, offering insight into the relationship between art, social identity, and daily life in the early 17th century.

Across these stops, one thread runs through: landscape in the seventeenth century. Seen together, the works show how differently that subject could be imagined.
