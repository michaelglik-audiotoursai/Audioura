Step-by-Step Audio Guided Tour: Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, MA
Tour-Category: museum

Stop 1: The artist is in his studio.

Address: 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3399, -71.0942

Orientation: You are about to explore the Museum of Fine Arts in Boston. Your first stop is The artist is in his studio... Stand before the panel where the painting rests, and shift your gaze from the artist at the left to the easel on the right. This vantage reveals the quiet tension between the creator and his creation—a dialogue of presence and absence—where the artist is not captured in the act of painting but in a moment of contemplation about his work.

The Artist in his Studio is an oil painting on panel by Rembrandt, created in 1629 as he worked to establish his reputation as a young painter in the Dutch Republic. Modest in size yet commanding in presence, the painting uses a restrained palette of earthy browns and muted grays. Rembrandt’s careful layering of oil glazes creates a semi-transparent effect, allowing light to permeate the scene and enhancing the spatial depth of the studio. Dark outlines define the artist’s figure and the easel with precision.

The artist’s gaze, fixed on the easel, suggests a moment of reflection rather than creation—he appears to be measuring the progress of his work and contemplating his next step. This intimate perspective focuses on thought rather than gesture, marking an early moment when Rembrandt chose to portray the artist as a thinker rather than a heroic doer. This approach would echo in his later self-portraits and influence how artists represented themselves.

The quiet tension in the composition invites a human reading of the artist’s solitude and the mental labor behind art. The subdued tones and careful technique emphasize the contemplative atmosphere of the studio, capturing the passage from raw inspiration to deliberate execution and holding the viewer in the space where ideas first take shape.

The painting’s survival owes much to Chevalier Antoine de la Roque, a Parisian collector born in 1672, who preserved the work through his collection in the 18th century and enabled its eventual arrival at the Museum of Fine Arts in Boston. Today, The Artist in his Studio is part of the Museum’s collection of Netherlandish art, which provides important context for understanding Rembrandt’s place among contemporaries such as Gerrit Dou and Anthony van Dyck. The painting also connects viewers to the MFA’s Center for Netherlandish Art, supporting research and engagement with these works and anchoring Rembrandt within a broader narrative of Dutch art history. The Artist in his Studio is among the MFA’s distinguished holdings, which also include works by Rubens and Rachel Ruysch, bridging Rembrandt’s presence to a wider spectrum of Dutch decorative arts and painting.

Stop 2: Madame Monet wearing a kimono

Address: 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3399, -71.0942

Orientation: Stand close to the center of the painting to take in its full height and width—231.8 by 142.3 centimeters—so you can grasp the imposing scale Monet chose. From this vantage, the delicate textures of the oil on canvas emerge more clearly, especially the interplay of light on the red silk of the uchikake kimono and the subtle expression on Camille’s face. The pale background flattens the space, accentuating the garment’s bold patterns and the figure’s poised posture.

Claude Monet feared that revealing the identity of the model for Madame Monet wearing a kimono would invite endless gossip and annoyance, so he asked for complete silence and requested that Dubois not be told. Because of this caution, no one mentioned Camille's name in connection with the painting until Monet himself revealed her role to Georges Bernheim and René Gimpel years later. The painting now hangs in the Museum of Fine Arts, where its history of secrecy and revelation still shapes how we see it. Later, the critic Simon Boubée reviewed the painting and wrote that Monet "has shown a Chinese in a red robe with two heads, one is that of a demimondaine placed on the shoulders, the other that of a monster, placed we dare not say where." Because Boubée publicly drew attention to the suggestive elements of the painting, it became a subject of controversy and discussion in Paris, adding to its notoriety and the layers of meaning that surround it today. The painting itself is a monumental oil on canvas. Monet’s choice of oil allowed him to build rich layers of pigment that capture the silky sheen and dense embroidery of the uchikake, a formal Japanese outer robe often worn at weddings. The figure of Camille Doncieux dominates the composition, her form rendered with crisp outlines and flattened planes of color that echo the influence of Japanese woodblock prints. This stylistic borrowing—part of the Japonisme movement sweeping through European art in the late 19th century—allowed Monet to explore both cross-cultural aesthetics and the boundaries of portraiture. Monet was at a pivotal moment in 1876, balancing the demands of public success with private artistic experiments. The painting reflects his fascination with the exotic and the theatrical, while also marking a shift from traditional French portraiture toward a more stylized and decorative approach. The emotional resonance of the painting lies in this tension between identity and disguise. Camille’s serene, almost inscrutable expression contrasts with the flamboyance of the kimono, suggesting the layered roles she inhabited—as muse, wife, and symbol within Monet’s expanding vision. The museum’s decision to invite visitors to don kimonos in front of this painting, notably during the exhibition La Japonaise, stirred debate about cultural appropriation and admiration, mirroring the painting’s own complex history. This event opened a new chapter in the dialogue between Western art and Japanese culture, echoing the layered meanings Monet embedded in the canvas over a century earlier.

Continue to Watson and the Shark.

Stop 3: Watson and the Shark
Address: 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115
Coordinates: 42.3399, -71.0942
Orientation: As you stand close to Watson and the Shark, position yourself so that the full scene of the struggling figures and the open jaws of the shark are visible without obstruction. From this vantage, you can follow the tense gestures and expressions of each person, noticing how they cluster around the water’s surface, drawing your eyes into the drama that unfolds at the center of the canvas. This painting shows the dramatic rescue of 14-year-old Brook Watson, who was attacked by a shark in Havana Harbor in 1749 and lost his right leg below the knee. Watson was not saved until the third attempt by his crew. About twenty-, after becoming a successful merchant and public figure in London, Watson commissioned John Singleton Copley to paint this scene. The 1778 painting is the large, original version by Copley. The artist chose to soften the horror of the attack by hiding the injury beneath the water, though you can still see a hint of blood in the waves. Watson survived the ordeal and later became an Alderman of the City of London and Deputy Governor of the Bank of England, his life forever shaped by those moments in the water—moments captured in Copley’s painting. Because Copley made these changes, the painting before you holds traces of different stories beneath its surface, revealing the artist’s hand in shaping how Watson’s ordeal would be remembered. The oil painting measures over six feet wide, a scale uncommon for an American artist working abroad at the time, emphasizing the physical struggle and near-fatal stakes of the moment. The composition directs your gaze in a swirl around Watson’s outstretched limbs and the shark’s gaping mouth, creating a palpable tension between life and death. The presence of the black sailor in the rescue party offers a layered reading of race and heroism during the eighteenth century, a detail Copley introduced despite originally sketching only white figures. This choice complicates the story and reflects changes in how the event was visually commemorated. Just as Madame Monet’s kimono from an earlier gallery stop tells of exchanges across cultures, here Copley’s brush reconfigures identities in the midst of crisis, hinting at complexities beneath the surface of history.
Directions: Proceed to Dance at Bougival.

Stop 4: Dance at Bougival

Address: 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3399, -71.0942

Orientation: Stand a little to the left of center before Dance at Bougival to take in the full height and narrow vertical sweep of the canvas, where the embrace and motion of the dancers become clear against the soft but vivid background. From this vantage, the contrast between the warm flesh tones and the carefully brushed fabrics gains definition, revealing the energy concentrated in their clasped hands and poised feet.

The scene in Dance at Bougival portrays a couple locked in dance, their bodies entwined in a moment captured between movement and stillness. The brushwork balances luminosity with solidity of form, maintaining the tangible weight of flesh and fabric while conveying a sense of fleeting light. The woman, modeled by the young artist Suzanne Valadon, wears a softly patterned dress and a wide hat, while the man’s posture and shadowed face suggest both strength and attentiveness. Each stroke conveys the rhythm and celebration of a festive afternoon in the suburb of Bougival. The dancers’ closeness and the warmth of their colors create a sense of communal delight that lingers beyond the canvas. The lasting impression is of movement caught and held—a celebration not only of dance but of life’s fleeting, joyful encounters.

When Félix François Depeaux bought Dance at Bougival from Paul Durand-Ruel in 1894, he promised to eventually give it to the Musée de Rouen. However, he later needed money to finance a costly divorce, so he broke that promise and sold the painting instead. The work never entered the Rouen museum, changing the course of its journey and leading it to Boston.

Directions: Continue to Bocca Baciata.

Stop 5: Bocca Baciata

Orientation: Stand directly before Bocca Baciata, at a distance that lets you take in the entire panel without losing sight of the subtle shifts in brushwork. From here, the delicacy of the oil medium and the signature in the lower left become clear, anchoring the piece in both technique and artist.

Dante Gabriel Rossetti set out to address a fault he saw in his earlier paintings—a stippled appearance of flesh, common among Pre-Raphaelite artists. In 1859, he painted Bocca Baciata, deliberately focusing on rendering flesh more fully and carefully, as he explained in a letter to William Bell Scott. The painting, oil on panel and measuring 32.1 by 27 centimeters, captures a single female figure in three-quarter profile, her gaze turned away, lit with a soft, even light that models the surface of her skin with new fullness. Rossetti’s choice of Fanny Cornforth as the model, the principal inspiration for his sensuous figures, lends a quiet, personal gravity to the work. The emphasis on smooth, lustrous flesh was a conscious response to the stippling effect he had previously found limiting, reflecting his continued search for a new expressive realism.

This conscious shift in technique marked a turning point in Rossetti’s career and stands as a record of his resolve to learn and improve as an artist. His signature—“G C D R 1859”—at the panel’s lower left documents this deliberate moment of development in his craft. The painting holds a stillness—an introspective pause within the museum’s larger narrative of human expression and transformation. Among the museum’s oldest and smallest panel paintings, Bocca Baciata is a concentrated study of surface and expression that quietly marks a turning point in one artist’s career and in the representation of feminine presence.

Directions: Your final stop in Museum of Fine Arts: Fray Hortensio Félix Paravicino.

Stop 6: Fray Hortensio Félix Paravicino

Address: 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3399, -71.0942

Orientation: Stand slightly to the right of the painting so that the subtle interplay of light on the folds of the Trinitarian habit becomes clear, revealing the solemn dignity that El Greco imparted to Fray Hortensio Félix Paravicino’s figure.

Fray Hortensio Félix Paravicino lived at a time when religious authorities debated the place of art in society. He argued for the destruction of all nude paintings, believing their existence threatened moral order. El Greco’s brushwork elongates the figure, a hallmark of his style, emphasizing spiritual elevation over earthly realism. The muted palette of ochres and blacks contrasts with the pale, expressive face, drawing attention to the friar’s contemplative gaze and furrowed brow. His hands, though only partly visible, rest calmly, suggesting restraint and inner resolve. El Greco painted this portrait late in his career, around 1609, a few years before his death. At this moment, he was deeply engaged in portraying figures who embodied religious fervor and intellectual rigor. Paravicino was a prolific poet and renowned orator, known for his eloquence and literary skill. This portrait reveals El Greco’s evolving approach to portraiture—melding spiritual intensity with psychological insight, distinct from the more decorative works you may have seen earlier on this tour, such as the delicate expression in Bocca Baciata. The composition conveys a reverence that echoes the museum’s historical mission to preserve works that root cultural memory in careful observation and respect for the sitter’s identity. The painting's solemnity invites reflection on the tension between faith and artistic expression in early 17th-century Spain.

The museum moved to its current Fenway location in 1909.

Across these stops, one thread runs through: the human figure in the seventeenth to nineteenth centuries. Seen together, the works show how differently that subject could be imagined.
