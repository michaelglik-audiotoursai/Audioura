Step-by-Step Audio Guided Tour: Musée d'Orsay, Paris, France - Museum Tour

Stop 1: Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe (Luncheon on the Grass)

Address: Musée d'Orsay, 1 Rue de la Légion d'Honneur, 75007 Paris, France

Coordinates: 48.881207, 2.328608

Your first stop is Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe (Luncheon on the Grass). There’s a hush just in front of Le Déjeuner sur l’herbe at the Musée d'Orsay—a pause as visitors face the expanse of oil on canvas, nearly life-size, the brushwork close enough to see where Manet’s hand worked the surface. Stand a pace or two back to see the composition as a whole: the bold, angular arrangement of figures becomes clear, and the contrast of flesh, fabric, and dappled greenery strikes with full force.

Édouard Manet completed Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe in 1863, working in oil to realize his vision on canvas. Manet chose to pursue a new freedom from traditional subjects. He originally titled the work Le Bain, but ultimately presented it as Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe, a choice that situated the scene in the contemporary world rather than in myth. By placing his large-scale oil on canvas alongside other avant-garde artists, Manet’s action led to a public reckoning over what subjects and techniques could be called art. This episode not only gave Manet’s vision a platform outside academic sanction, but also set a precedent for other artists to challenge institutional authority. Le Déjeuner sur l’herbe—its very title, “Luncheon on the Grass”—offers a scene at once ordinary and disquieting: two fully dressed men in modern Parisian attire picnic beside a nude woman, her gaze direct and unashamed. Another woman bathes in a stream behind, partly obscured by shadow and foliage. The paint’s texture is evident where sunlight strikes a pale shoulder or glimmers on the tossed fabric, and the scale of the canvas allows viewers to experience the figures almost at their own height. Standing before this work, the sharp contrasts between light and shadow, and between clothed and naked, carry the charge of Manet’s refusal to disguise the present as allegory. The Musée d'Orsay, renowned for its unmatched collection of Impressionist and Post-Impressionist masterpieces, places this painting at the hinge where tradition gave way to the new. The risk Manet took here shaped the freedom that later Impressionists would claim for themselves.

Stop 2: La Nuit étoilée (Starry Night Over the Rhône)

Address: Musée d'Orsay, 1 Rue de la Légion d'Honneur, 75007 Paris, France

Coordinates: 48.8606, 2.3266

Orientation: Stand close enough to "Starry Night Over the Rhône (La Nuit étoilée)" that the lower edge of the canvas is at eye level. From here, the couple of lovers at the bottom of the painting emerges from shadow—a detail that anchors the entire nocturnal scene and is easily missed when viewing at a distance. This vantage also allows the shifting reflections across the Rhône to unfold before you, connecting the lights of Arles to the expanse of night sky overhead. Opening hours weren't published where we could read them.

Vincent van Gogh painted Starry Night Over the Rhône in September 1888 while living in Arles, just a short walk from his rented Yellow House. He chose a spot on the riverbank to capture the night scene, and wrote to his brother Theo describing his efforts to paint the night sky using vibrant colors instead of black. The painting's foreground shows signs of heavy reworking, and Van Gogh was fascinated by the technical challenge of painting outdoors at night. The brushwork is deliberate, short strokes of color layered to give the water a sense of gentle movement—a technical choice that sets off the night’s stillness with subtle vitality. Above, the sky is studded with stars, their pale halos mirrored in the river’s shifting surface. At the bottom, a couple of lovers stands together, rendered in just a handful of brushstrokes. Their presence confers a human quietness upon the scene—a gesture toward serenity amid the vastness of the night. The painting’s physical qualities—oil on canvas, 1888—bear the trace of the artist’s hand as he layered blue, gold, and green, refusing the conventions of darkness to find emotion in the night itself. The presence of the lovers grounds the scene in human experience, a quiet counterpart to the vast, shifting sky above. In this work, the sense of wonder is inseparable from the artist’s willingness to see the night as luminous—an approach that would ripple outward in the development of Post-Impressionism. Emotion here is rendered not as an idea, but as color and light—an innovation that signals the direction Impressionism would take in the hands of those who followed.

Directions: Next: L'Eglise d'Auvers-sur-Oise. Continue to L'Église d'Auvers-sur-Oise (The Church at Auvers).

Stop 3: L'Église d'Auvers-sur-Oise (The Church at Auvers)

Address: Musée d'Orsay, 1 Rue de la Légion d'Honneur, 75007 Paris, France

Coordinates: 48.8606, 2.3266

Orientation: As you stand here, bring your gaze level with the centre of L’Eglise d’Auvers-sur-Oise. Position yourself so the painting fills your vision edge to edge—this lets you see how the warped blues and trembling outlines pull the church away from reality. From this slight angle, the paths in the foreground fan out, breaking the illusion of a single direction and making the church appear almost to hover above the earth.

In June 1890, Vincent van Gogh created L’Eglise d’Auvers-sur-Oise as an oil painting on canvas, just weeks before his death. Van Gogh painted this work during his ten-week stay in Auvers-sur-Oise, a village near Paris where he completed about 70 works in a final, fevered burst of activity. Choosing the church’s apse—the chevet—as his viewpoint, Van Gogh used steep, anxious outlines and a palette of cobalt blues and violet shadows to twist the building’s form. The sky above the church churns with dark clouds, painted in heavy, swirling strokes, while the split paths below lead away from each other, refusing the viewer a simple entrance. Van Gogh himself decided to paint the church because it stood at the centre of Auvers-sur-Oise, a landmark he passed daily as he wandered the village with his canvas and paints. This choice gave the painting a personal stake: Van Gogh used the building’s familiar presence to channel the turmoil and melancholy he felt in those last weeks. The oil on canvas surface allowed him to layer thick, urgent brushwork, creating a sense of agitation and instability in the otherwise still village scene. During these weeks, Van Gogh wrote to his brother Theo in Paris, describing how he was working at a furious pace, producing landscape after landscape—each one an attempt to order his thoughts and stave off despair. This painting stands among the final images he sent to Theo, marking a connection between the artist’s private struggle and his family’s distant support. Standing before L’Eglise d’Auvers-sur-Oise, the building no longer appears as simple stone and glass, but as a vessel for Van Gogh’s agitation—his struggle with the world, and his refusal to paint it as calm or resolved. The painting’s melancholy lingers, a reminder of what was at stake for the artist in his final weeks: not only the image of a church, but the attempt to translate a turbulent mind into paint.

Directions: Proceed to L’Origine du monde. Continue to L'Origine du monde.

Stop 4: L'Origine du monde

Address: Musée d'Orsay, 1 Rue de la Légion d'Honneur, 75007 Paris, France

Coordinates: 48.8606, 2.3266

Type/Specialty: Peinture

Orientation: Stand several paces back from L’Origine du monde and let your gaze rest level with the center of the canvas. From this vantage, the painting’s uncompromising directness is fully apparent, its content impossible to soften or evade. The muted wall and hushed gallery make the flesh tones and contour even more pronounced.

Khalil-Bey, a Turkish-Egyptian diplomat, wanted a painting that would surpass the boundaries of academic art and commissioned Courbet to create L’Origine du monde in 1866. Here is the canvas itself: oil on linen, measuring just over 46 by 55 centimeters. Courbet’s brushwork is dense and deliberate, lending the flesh a palpable immediacy. At the center, a woman’s torso and thighs fill the frame—not an allegorical Venus, but the body itself, cropped to anonymity, with the focus entirely on physical origin. There is no face, no narrative context, only skin, pubic hair, and the gentle shadowed folds of the lower abdomen.

Because Khalil-Bey asked for something that defied the smooth, idealized nudes favored in the salons and instead spoke directly to the eroticism hidden beneath conventions, Courbet painted this work in a way that shocked even his contemporaries. The painting was made at a time when works like Le Déjeuner sur l’herbe were already unsettling the boundaries of what could be shown in art, but Courbet went further: here, the body is not a symbol or an ideal, but a fact. The shock is not only in what is shown, but in how completely the painting refuses to apologize for itself. The power lies in Courbet’s refusal to flatter, to explain, or to retreat.

Because of its scandalous subject, Courbet created the work in secrecy, and it was kept hidden from public view for many years. Khalil-Bey displayed the painting in his dressing room, concealed behind a green curtain, and would reveal it only to select visitors and guests.

Stop 5: Bal du moulin de la Galette

Address: Musée d'Orsay, 1 Rue de la Légion d'Honneur, 75007 Paris, France

Coordinates: 48.887398, 2.337129

Orientation: From the middle of the gallery, stand a few paces back from "Bal du moulin de la Galette." Here, the full sweep of the canvas—nearly life-sized at 131.5 by 176.5 centimeters—unfolds in front of you, the figures and their gestures clear, the shifting Sunday light rendered legible across faces and tables. At this angle, the depth of the crowd and the flicker of sunlight through chestnut leaves become apparent, as if the afternoon has been captured and held here for centuries.

Auguste Renoir struggled to paint Bal du moulin de la Galette on the spot, as the wind constantly threatened to blow his canvas away. Despite these difficulties, he insisted on working outdoors, returning to the site again and again as the season advanced and the light shifted. Each visit brought new adjustments, allowing him to refine the composition. Renoir positioned his friends within the crowd, capturing faces he knew and arranging them in poses that animate the scene with a sense of authentic leisure. The painting shows a throng of working-class Parisians—men in straw boaters and women in striped dresses—gathered on a Sunday afternoon at the original Moulin de la Galette. They drink, dance, and eat the namesake galettes, the thick pancakes that gave the place its name. Renoir used a rapid, broken brushstroke, letting flecks of pale blue and rose intermingle with ochre and white. His commitment to painting outdoors, known as "en plein air," allowed him to achieve a radiance and immediacy that could not be found in a studio.

Critics have speculated that it was the larger d'Orsay painting that Renoir completed in the garden of the abandoned cottage on rue Cortot, rather than the smaller version. In the context of this museum—a place housing the largest collection of Impressionist and post-Impressionist paintings in the world—the canvas stands as a record of a specific social world. It marks a turning point: after its making, scenes of daily life—rendered with the effects of light and movement—became central to Impressionist art, opening new possibilities for what painting could show. This is a painting of joy, its energy drawn from the mingling of bodies, the tilt of hats, and the blur of hands in motion.

Stop 6: Une moderne Olympia (A Modern Olympia)

Coordinates: 48.870167, 2.328375

Orientation: Stand a few paces back from Une moderne Olympia to let the entire canvas come into view—the contrast of light and shadow, and the complex arrangement of figures, become legible only when you see the painting as a whole.

The painting centers on a white woman reclining on a bed, her body boldly outlined and set against a backdrop that feels at once intimate and exposed. Behind her, a black servant stands—her presence a visible marker of the economic means the reclining woman possessed, an acknowledgment of the social patterns of the time. The composition is compressed: the figures are pressed close to the picture plane, lending a sense of immediacy and confrontation that disrupts the distance usually kept between subject and viewer. Cézanne created this painting in direct response to Manet’s Olympia, which had scandalized Paris in 1865 with its frank depiction of a nude courtesan and her servant. Cézanne chose not to simply copy the earlier work but instead reworked its provocations, using a rough, almost unblended application of paint to break down the boundaries between figure and ground. This gesture ensured that Cézanne’s challenging vision would enter the public sphere, allowing the painting to take its place among the museum’s collection. What comes across in standing before the work is both tension and exposure: Cézanne’s Olympia is not content to be a passive object of viewing but asserts herself, uncompromising and unyielding, in a scene that makes visible the economic and racial dynamics of its time. The result is a painting that both draws from and challenges the Impressionist and Realist traditions, marking a turning point in Cézanne’s own approach to representation, and laying groundwork for what Post-Impressionism would become.

Together, these stops reveal a transformative exploration of artistic freedom and social commentary in 19th-century art, as seen in works like Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe.
