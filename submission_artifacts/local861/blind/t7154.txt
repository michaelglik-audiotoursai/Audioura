Step-by-Step Audio Guided Tour: Rijksmuseum, Amsterdam, Netherlands
Tour-Category: museum

Stop 1: Winterlandschap met ijsvermaak (Winter landscape with skaters)

Address: Museumstraat 1, 1071 XX Amsterdam, Netherlands

Coordinates: 52.36, 4.8852

The militiaman’s head tilts joyfully; his mouth is open, his gaze direct, his cheeks flushed—every detail painted with a freedom that suggests the sitter is caught in the height of a celebration.

Orientation: Each piece reflects the evolution of ordinary scenes into extraordinary art, showcasing the essence of Dutch and global culture. Your first stop is Winterlandschap met ijsvermaak (Winter landscape with skaters). Stand several meters back from this painting so your eyes can sweep the full breadth of Avercamp’s panoramic winter scene. From this vantage, the horizon stretches wide and low, revealing not just the skaters but a whole frozen world—villagers, children, and animals—each detail emerging as your gaze travels across the painted ice.

Hendrick Avercamp’s “Winter landscape with skaters,” painted in oil on panel around 1608, presents a Dutch river locked in winter, but teeming with life. The paint’s surface is dense with minute gestures: a mother steadying a child, a dog sniffing the snow, a group gathered in lively conversation. Avercamp’s brushwork, particularly in the clusters of figures, animates every corner with stories of winter recreation and survival. Avercamp was only in his early twenties when he created this work, already intent on capturing the communal rituals of his time. He drew inspiration from southern Netherlandish painters but shifted the focus to the world of the Dutch village in winter, where shared experience outweighs individual heroics. In this painting, Avercamp’s decision to fill the landscape with so many distinct episodes meant that the viewer could read the rhythms of Dutch life—work, play, and rest—happening all at once. A story unfolds in the painting’s making: Avercamp, ambitious and young, looked to the example set by southern Netherlandish artists who had depicted winter’s hardships but rarely its pleasures. He chose to emphasize recreation and community instead. This shift gave Dutch viewers a new way to see themselves reflected in art—not as anonymous figures in a harsh season, but as neighbors bound by shared joys. Avercamp’s work marks a turning point for the Dutch Golden Age of painting, not through grand innovation, but through the quiet, persistent attention to ordinary life. The painting’s surface, built up with careful layers of oil, becomes a record of a society finding warmth in togetherness. For visitors in the Rijksmuseum, this scene offers not just nostalgia but a window onto the enduring rituals and pleasures that shaped, and still shape, Dutch identity. The Rijksmuseum’s collection is dedicated to preserving these snapshots of Dutch experience—here, in Avercamp’s world, the ordinary becomes unforgettable. Other galleries nearby reveal how artists across centuries have immortalized different facets of Dutch life: city streets, domestic interiors, and distant seas, each echoing the communal spirit so present on Avercamp’s frozen river.

Directions: Continue through Rijksmuseum — next is View of Houses in Delft.

Stop 2: Gezicht op huizen in Delft, bekend als ‘Het straatje’ (The Little Street)

Address: Museumstraat 1, 1071 XX Amsterdam, Netherlands

Coordinates: 52.36, 4.8852

Orientation: From this vantage, each stroke of paint renders the passage of time tangible: the worn threshold, the weathered shutters, the subtle irregularities in the facades. Only here does the full geometry of Vermeer’s composition align; the receding alleyway to the left and the small figures at their chores become clear, and the quiet order of Delft’s houses unfolds with all its muted dignity.

Johannes Vermeer lived and worked in Delft, where the exact streets and houses around him shaped his daily world. He chose to paint a view of houses in Delft, known as 'The Little Street', around 1658, focusing on a scene that included property belonging to his aunt, van der Minne. Because he made this decision, the painting now preserves a specific place and moment from 17th-century Delft, linking his family and city directly to the work you see here. Painted in oil on canvas, “The Little Street” measures just over 54 by 44 centimeters—a modest scale that belies its complexity. Vermeer’s attention to every brick, each plank and tile, is meticulous—an act of preservation as much as depiction. It is known that Vermeer first painted the door to the house closed, then chose to open it, allowing a shaft of light and a glimpse of domestic activity within; in this, the work reveals its layered construction, the artist’s evolving intent. Created at a moment when cityscapes were rare in Dutch painting, this work stands apart for its intimacy and restraint. Vermeer, then in his mid-twenties, had not yet painted the celebrated interiors for which he would later be known. The painting’s stillness is weighted with centuries: the roughness of the plaster, the rhythm of the windows, the unhurried pace of daily life. As with the frozen pond of the first room, time here holds its breath.

Directions: Next: The Threatened Swan (De bedreigde zwaan).

Stop 3: De bedreigde zwaan (The Threatened Swan)

Address: Museumstraat 1, 1071 XX Amsterdam, Netherlands

Coordinates: 52.36, 4.8852

Orientation: As you stand here, let yourself take in the sheer scale of The Threatened Swan (De bedreigde zwaan): at 144 by 171 centimeters, it looms at eye level, the swan’s outstretched wings nearly life-sized. Position yourself so the full arc of the bird’s body dominates your view—this angle makes the drama between the swan and the invading dog utterly inescapable.

Jan Asselijn painted The Threatened Swan around 1650, working in oil on canvas. Because of that choice, the painting has remained in the museum’s collection ever since, and it is still on view at the Rijksmuseum today. Asselijn, known for his ability to charge animal scenes with human emotion, crafted this confrontation with meticulous layers of oil paint—each feather rendered with a precision that amplifies the tension. The mute swan, wings arched, rises in defense as a lanky dog lunges in from the canvas’s edge. At her feet lies a nest, the reason for this fury, barely visible but central to the story. This allegorical reading emerged long after Asselijn’s lifetime, but it gave the painting an entirely new layer of meaning. Asselijn completed the painting when he was already an established figure in the Dutch Golden Age, channeling the political anxieties of his era into an image that could outlast any speech or pamphlet. The decision by the Nationale Kunst-Galerij’s founders to select this as their inaugural purchase set the tone for a collection built around works that resonate with Dutch identity and resilience. Standing here, the emotional charge is unmistakable—the threat, the defense, the sense that something beyond a single bird’s nest is at stake. After enjoying the quiet geometry of View of Houses in Delft on your last stop, the raw confrontation in Asselijn’s swan seems to jolt the room awake. In a museum built to guard cultural treasures, this painting becomes both symbol and sentinel.

Directions: Proceed to The Milkmaid.

Stop 4: Het melkmeisje (The Milkmaid)

Address: Museumstraat 1, 1071 XX Amsterdam, Netherlands

Coordinates: 52.375917, 4.883463

Orientation: From a few paces back and slightly to the right, The Milkmaid by Johannes Vermeer reveals its full composition: the small window’s light spills gently across the oil-on-canvas surface, catching both the rough bread on the table and the quiet focus of the maid as she pours milk.

Her world is defined by the essentials: a stone wall, coarse bread, ceramic pots, and above all, the stream of milk. With The Milkmaid, he did not choose a scene of opulence or religious drama, but instead devoted careful attention to a single figure and her absorbed gesture. This sense of wonder—how the smallest gesture can become lasting art—anchors The Milkmaid within the museum’s collection. By making The Milkmaid a centerpiece of the exhibition, Dibbits underscored how this modest canvas continues to shape the museum’s view of Dutch painting and its ability to elevate everyday culture into high art.

Continue to De grote golf bij Kanagawa (The Great Wave off Kanagawa).

Stop 5: De grote golf bij Kanagawa (The Great Wave off Kanagawa)

Address: Museumstraat 1, 1071 XX Amsterdam, Netherlands

Coordinates: 52.36, 4.8852

Orientation: Stand directly before The Great Wave off Kanagawa (De grote golf bij Kanagawa), centering your gaze where the arc of the towering wave nearly encircles a distant, snow-capped Mount Fuji. From this vantage, the deliberate contrast becomes clear: Hokusai’s clever use of perspective collapses the immensity of sea and mountain into a single, unified plane, a tension you might miss from the side.

In the hush of this gallery, long after the footsteps from The Milkmaid have faded, a different current carries you: a story told by paper, pigment, and the memory of tides. Its surface is a field of blue and white, where a colossal wave—about to break—hangs above three vulnerable boats, each manned by fishermen bracing against the sea’s ferocity. Hokusai’s compositional choice, framing Fuji within the wave’s embrace, is said to embody a visual structure where natural force, human vulnerability, and lasting order coexist. Hokusai, at the time of this series, was in his seventies—an age when most artists would have quieted. The technique—color woodblock printing—meant that each impression was the result of a carefully orchestrated collaboration between artist, carver, and printer, producing an edition that allowed this image to travel far beyond Edo. This work w. The consequence of this decision was profound: Hokusai’s wave reached viewers across Japan and, centuries later, across the globe. Its composition is said to have inspired works like Debussy’s La Mer and Rilke’s Der Berg, ensuring its reputation as an icon of world art. The Rijksmuseum includes this print as part of its purpose to represent not only Dutch history but the global currents that shape art and culture. Standing before The Great Wave off Kanagawa, what lingers is awe—a sense of the frailty of those who ride the sea, the enduring calm of Fuji, and the balance struck between chaos and order. Here, centuries fold into a single moment, much as the milkmaid you saw earlier once distilled daily life into ritual. Soon, you will encounter how common celebrations—feasts and gatherings—were fixed in Dutch art, and how their ordinariness became enduring. This work was created by Katsushika Hokusai around 1831 as part of his series Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji. To produce each impression, Hokusai designed the image, which was then carved into woodblocks by skilled artisans and printed by hand. The publisher, Nishimuraya Yohachi, commissioned and financed the project, overseeing both its subject and its commercial distribution.

Your final stop in Rijksmuseum: A Militiaman Holding a Berkemeyer, Known as the ‘Merry Drinker’.

Stop 6: A Militiaman Holding a Berkemeyer, Known as the ‘Merry Drinker’

Address: Museumstraat 1, 1071 XX Amsterdam, Netherlands

Coordinates: 52.36, 4.8852

Orientation: Stand a few paces back from A Militiaman Holding a Berkemeyer, Known as the ‘Merry Drinker’ and look directly at the canvas. From this vantage, the militiaman’s raised glass—a Berkemeyer with its wide, flared bowl—catches the light and seems to invite you into his moment of festivity, just as the skating figures in Winter landscape with skaters, which you viewed earlier, draw you into their communal joy.

He took up his brushes and painted A Militiaman Holding a Berkemeyer, capturing a man mid-toast with a lively, free execution. Because he created it, this work now hangs in the Rijksmuseum. The painting, measuring 81 by 66.5 centimeters, is executed in oil on canvas. Hals applied his paint in strokes that move swiftly across the surface, as if the artist himself could barely contain the exuberance of the moment. Standing before the Merry Drinker, the atmosphere is unmistakable: open laughter, a shared toast, and the energy of a gathering. Hals, painting in the late 1620s, chose to present the sitter not in stiff formality but alive in the company of others—a choice that, for centuries, has made this work a living reflection of Dutch sociability, fitting the museum’s aim to celebrate shared culture.
