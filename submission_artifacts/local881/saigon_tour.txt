Stop 1: Notre-Dame Cathedral Basilica of Saigon

Coordinates: 10.779785, 106.699020

Type/Specialty: Historical Landmark

Specific Examples: Stained glass windows, French colonial architecture, and the iconic statue of the Virgin Mary.

Orientation: This tour spans approximately 6 kilometers, guiding you from the Notre-Dame Cathedral Basilica of Saigon to Thi Bar Saigon, through the bustling streets and iconic landmarks of the city. As you traverse the central area, you will uncover layers of history and experience a blend of colonial architecture and modern vibrancy, revealing stories of resilience and transformation that define this city. Your first stop is Notre-Dame Cathedral Basilica of Saigon. You'll notice the stunning French colonial buildings lining the street. The red brick walls, weathered but steadfast, frame the imposing facade, while steps lead up to the main doors beneath stained glass windows that catch afternoon light. The murmur of city life swirls around you, mixing with the faint echo of bells that mark the hour, anchoring this spot as a crossroads between past and present.

The cathedral before you was completed in 1880, replacing an earlier wooden church built in 1863 along the Charner Canal to serve the French Catholic community in Saigon. That modest building on Ngo Duc Ke Street soon proved too small for the growing Catholic population as Saigon transformed under French rule. French administrators originally named the new structure the Church of Saigon. The red bricks, imported from Marseille, France, are a visible imprint of distant hands shaping the city’s skyline. Two bell towers rise symmetrically, reaching high, signaling the cathedral’s stature both physically and culturally. In 1959, the installation of the statue of Our Lady of Peace in the forecourt officially gave the cathedral its current name: Notre-Dame Cathedral Basilica of Saigon. This statue now stands beneath the towers, a silent witness to generations who have gathered here for mass, celebrations, and community rites over more than 140 years. The cathedral remains a focal point where urban sounds meet the solemn tolling of bells, and where the imported bricks still catch sunlight in a way that recalls the faraway French coast, embedding this place in the intertwined histories of Saigon and colonial France.

Stop 2: Saigon Central Post Office

Coordinates: 10.779852, 106.699844

Type/Specialty: Architectural Landmark

Specific Examples: Grand vaulted ceiling, antique postal equipment, and a large mural of Vietnam.

The Saigon Central Post Office, completed between 1886 and 1891, was designed by architect Alfred Foulhoux with the assistance of Auguste Henri Vildieu. The building stands on the site of an earlier structure—the Saigon Steel Wire Department—designed by Gustave Eiffel between 1860 and 1863. This layering of architectural effort marks a continuum of French colonial engineering and design in Saigon, bridging the mid-19th century to the late 19th century.

The vaulted ceiling above you, with its delicate iron ribs, reflects the European craftsmanship that Foulhoux brought to the building, blending Gothic, Renaissance, and French colonial styles into an eclectic whole. The iron and glass structure offers a tactile contrast between the cool metal framework and the smooth, worn wooden counters beneath.

In 1864, from this city, the first letters bearing Vietnam’s inaugural ‘stork’ stamp were dispatched globally, marking the country’s entry into international postal networks. This stamp, simple yet emblematic, opened channels for commerce, news, and personal communication that reshaped Vietnam’s connections beyond its borders. The sounds of sorting and stamping letters mingle with the distant chime of a clock, anchoring the space in its dual role as a hub of communication and a keeper of history.

Directions: From the Saigon Central Post Office, head east on Công xã Paris, then turn left onto Nguyễn Văn Bình Street and walk for about 200 meters. You'll start to see colorful book stalls lining the street. Continue to Nguyễn Văn Bình Book Street.

Orientation: Your attention is drawn to rows of colorful book stalls lining both sides, interspersed with small cafés and shaded benches, the rustle of turning pages blending with distant city sounds.

You are standing on Nguyễn Văn Bình Book Street, a pedestrian-only cultural hub named after the intellectual Nguyễn Văn Bình since July 4, 2000. This small, tranquil road is located in the heart of District 1, right next to landmarks like Notre Dame Cathedral and the Ho Chi Minh City Post Office. Here, you find thousands of books from all over the world, covering various genres and often sold at prices lower than standard markets. The street lies opposite the Independence Palace and is about three kilometers from Bui Vien Street. The name Nguyễn Văn Bình also reflects an appreciation for both knowledge and religion.

Stop 4: Independence Palace

Coordinates: 10.776994, 106.695305

Type/Specialty: Historical Site

Specific Examples: The main hall, underground tunnels, and gardens.

Orientation: Standing on the wide pavement just outside the main gates of this imposing building, the smooth concrete walls and broad windows come into view, framed by neatly trimmed gardens. The low hum of city traffic blends with distant voices, but here the air feels held in a different time. Look up to see the clean lines of a modernist structure that quietly commands attention amid Saigon’s bustling streets.

You stand just outside the main gates of the Independence Palace, its smooth concrete walls and broad windows framed by neatly trimmed gardens. This modernist building quietly commands attention amid Saigon’s bustling streets, a place where history was made. On 30 April 1975, a tank from the Liberation Army of South Vietnam crashed through these very gates, marking the Fall of Saigon and the end of the Vietnam War. Inside, the palace reveals layers of power and protest, including photographs of Thich Quang Duc, the Buddhist monk who self-immolated in 1963 to protest government persecution. The palace’s concrete walls and polished floors hold memories of leadership, resistance, and moments that changed the city’s future.

The walls, lined with black-and-white photographs, bear silent witness to scenes of destruction and human suffering, their texture absorbing the low murmur of visitors’ footsteps. The museum’s founding in 1975 marked a deliberate effort by the Ho Chi Minh City government to expose what they termed crimes committed by the United States and its allies. This followed a tradition already established: earlier exhibitions had revealed French colonial abuses, and this museum continued that thread by focusing on American military actions. Stop 6: Saigon Opera House

Coordinates: 10.776613, 106.703170

Type/Specialty: Cultural Venue

Specific Examples: The ornate facade, performances, and guided tours.

You stand before the Saigon Opera House, a jewel box of French colonial architecture facing Lam Sơn Square, its balconies and arched windows framed by sculpted stone. Originally opened in 1900 as a venue for opera performances, this building’s purpose shifted dramatically in the mid-20th century. From 1956 to 1967, it served as the house of the unicameral National Assembly of South Vietnam, and later housed both the Lower House and the Senate until 1975. In 1975, the Opera House hosted the People's Congress of Deputies of the Provisional Revolutionary Government of the Republic of South Vietnam, marking a pivotal moment in the city’s history. During these years, the stage was no longer just for performances but became a platform for political debate and governance. After 1976, the building gradually returned to its artistic roots, and its façade was restored in 1998 to celebrate Saigon’s 300th anniversary. Today, as you look across the street to another symbol of governance, you can sense how deeply this city’s public life has been intertwined with its public spaces.

Directions: Head west for roughly 250 meters, and you'll reach Ho Chi Minh City Hall, also known as the People's Committee Building. Enjoy the stroll! Continue to Ho Chi Minh City Hall (People's Committee Building).

Stop 7: Ho Chi Minh City Hall (People's Committee Building)

Coordinates: 10.776543, 106.700910

Type/Specialty: Government Building

Specific Examples: The impressive facade, surrounding gardens, and historical significance.

Orientation: The low hum of city life drifts past, mingling with the rustle of leaves from the manicured gardens that frame the structure. From this vantage point, the symmetrical lines and tall windows invite a closer look.

This building, completed in 1908 and officially opened in 1909, was designed by the French architect Fernand Gardè. Construction began in 1898, making it a decade-long project and a key colonial-era structure in Saigon. Its architectural style blends French colonial and Renaissance influences, visible in the elegant arches and ornamental details that crown the exterior.

Originally called Hôtel de ville, or the municipal hall, it served as the administrative center during the French colonial period. The building witnessed the city’s shifts in governance through decades of upheaval, including the Vietnam War, when it functioned under various authorities. Its walls hold the echoes of decisions that shaped Saigon’s—and later Ho Chi Minh City’s—political life.

Today, it houses the offices of the Ho Chi Minh City People’s Committee, continuing its function as the heart of municipal government. On 30 April 2023, for the first time, the city hall opened its doors to the public, allowing visitors to explore beyond the exterior view. This event marked a shift in how the building engages with the community, sharing its spaces and stories directly with those who come to see it.

Stop 9: Bạch Đằng Pier

Coordinates: 10.775263, 106.706880

Type/Specialty: Scenic Waterfront

Specific Examples: River views, sunset cruises, and waterfront dining.

Orientation: Standing near the water’s edge at Bạch Đằng Pier, your view stretches over the Saigon River, with boats moored along the wharf and the city skyline reflecting in the calm surface. This is the spot where waterborne journeys from Saigon’s core have converged for decades, making the pier a gateway framed by the rising sun’s glow or the dusk’s soft, cooling air.

Bạch Đằng Pier has long served as a vital river transport hub, linking Saigon with its outlying districts. Historically, it was a boat quay, a key point for river traffic and goods entering the city. Later, it became a ferry terminal connecting to Thủ Thiêm Pier across the Saigon River, highlighting its continued importance in the city’s transport network. This shift established Bạch Đằng not only as a commercial link but also as a connector of communities separated by the river.

The pier is also known as a departure point for tours to the Củ Chi Tunnels, a network of underground passages northwest of the city that were used during the Vietnam War. Visitors leaving from Bạch Đằng Pier embark on journeys that trace Vietnam’s complex wartime history, making the pier a gateway from the urban bustle to a subterranean world once crucial for Viet Cong fighters. This modern role echoes the site’s past as a conduit for movement and strategy, linking it to the conflict that shaped Saigon.

Walking along the pier, the textured concrete underfoot and the cool river breeze remind you that this is a place where water and city life meet. Near the Café Apartments, with their vertical maze of balconies, the pier offers a contrast: open sky and flowing water versus dense urban life. This duality—the pier as a liminal space—remains steady in the calm of the river, inviting quiet reflection on movement, history, and place. As the sun shifts, the view across the river reveals a bar perched above the water, offering a vantage point to watch the city’s pulse slow and gather at dusk.
