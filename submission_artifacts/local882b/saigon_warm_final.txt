In 1959, a statue of Mary was installed directly in front of the cathedral. This addition became a focal point for locals and visitors alike, drawing daily visitors who pause in quiet reverence or gather during religious ceremonies. Stop 2: Post Office

Coordinates: 10.780000, 106.700000

Type/Specialty: Historic Post Office

Specific Examples: Grand interior, vintage postal services, stunning architecture.

Orientation: The façade of the Saigon Central Post Office stretches wide before you, its arched windows and ornate ironwork inviting closer inspection. Approach the central entrance beneath the curved iron and glass canopy to fully appreciate the space within.

Completed between 1886 and 1891, this building was designed by Alfred Foulhoux, though it is often mistakenly attributed to Gustave Eiffel, whose earlier work included the original post office here from 1860 to 1863. The current structure embodies an eclectic architectural style, merging Gothic and Renaissance elements with decorative details inspired by local motifs. This reflects the French colonial ambition to imprint their vision on Indochina while acknowledging local aesthetics. The soaring vaulted ceilings frame the long hall, where antique telephone booths stand like sentinels of a vanished era of communication. Their polished wood and brass fittings contrast with the soft echo of footsteps on tiled floors. Amidst the chaos and uncertainty of conflict, the Post Office remained a nerve center, linking Saigon with the wider world through cables and couriers. Its role transcended mere architecture; it was a lifeline, a place where hope was dispatched in envelopes and telegrams. The Post Office embodies centuries of Saigon’s evolving narrative within its walls.

Stop 4: Independence Palace

Coordinates: 10.777050, 106.695370

Type/Specialty: Historical Site

Specific Examples: War history exhibits, beautiful gardens, iconic architecture.

Orientation: Approach the palace’s broad gates and stand just outside where the pavement meets the expansive forecourt. From here, the building’s clean lines and modernist roof catch the sunlight, and you can feel the weight of history in the wide open space where tanks once rolled through. The air carries a faint hum of city life, the distant chatter blending with the rustling of leaves from the gardens that frame this place.

You stand just outside the broad gates of the Independence Palace, where on 30 April 1975, a Liberation Army tank crashed through these very gates, marking the Fall of Saigon and the end of the Vietnam War. Completed on 31 October 1966 and designed by architect Ngô Viết Thụ, this modernist building replaced the earlier palace destroyed by bombing. Construction began on 1 July 1962, ordered by President Ngô Đình Diệm, who never saw its completion due to his assassination in 1963. General Nguyễn Văn Thiệu officially opened the palace in 1966, using it as his home and office during a turbulent era. The palace’s clean lines and modernist roof stand firm amid Saigon’s centre, bearing the scars of that decisive day when history changed here forever.

The War Remnants Museum in Ho Chi Minh City is dedicated to exposing the consequences of conflict, with a focus on both the First Indochina War and the Vietnam War. The museum follows a tradition of exhibitions that reveal the actions of foreign powers—first the French, then the Americans—in this land. Stop 6: Opera House

Coordinates: 10.776390, 106.702780

Type/Specialty: Cultural Venue

Specific Examples: Stunning façade, live performances, historical significance.

Orientation: Standing just across from Lam Sơn Square, the best vantage point to appreciate the Saigon Opera House is from the broad sidewalk facing its front façade. The street hums with the chatter of scooters and the occasional clink of café cups nearby, giving life to the scene. From here, the entire building’s elaborate exterior—its ornate balconies and sculpted details—unfolds clearly against the urban backdrop.

The Saigon Opera House was designed by Félix Olivier and completed on January 1, 1900, marking the turn of the century with a flourish of French colonial architecture. Its venue auditorium was crafted not just for performance but also for public gathering, reflecting the ambitions of the era’s French administration in Saigon. The façade, rich with flamboyant curves and intricate stucco work, echoes the style of other French colonial theaters in Hanoi and Haiphong, creating a trio of cultural landmarks that once connected the region under colonial rule. But this building’s story takes a sharp turn from art to politics. When the government shifted to a bicameral legislature, this same building housed both the Lower House (Hạ Nghị Viện) and the Senate (Thượng Nghị Viện). Imagine the weight of political decisions, debates, and legislative sessions filling the very rooms where you might later hear an aria or a symphony. This makes the Opera House a rare venue where culture and governance have intersected so literally. After that, the Opera House returned to its original function as a theatre in 1976, closing a loop between the arts and the nation’s turbulent history. The tactile sensation of the cool marble steps beneath your feet contrasts with the delicate ironwork overhead, while the distant buzz of motorbikes blends with the building’s enduring presence. Here, Saigon’s layered identity—colonial past, political upheaval, and cultural resilience—stands quietly, waiting to be heard beyond the ornate façade.

Stop 7: City Hall

Coordinates: 10.776470, 106.700970

Type/Specialty: Government Building

Specific Examples: Imposing architecture, beautiful lighting at night, historical significance.

Orientation: The light here catches the pale yellow walls and white stone trim to reveal the precise symmetry and classical details of its design. The clock at the building’s apex marks time steadily, a quiet witness to the city’s public rhythms.

The building before you, completed in 1908 and officially opened in 1909, was designed by the French architect Fernand Gardè. It rose between 1898 and 1908 during the French colonial period and was originally known as the Hôtel de Ville de Saïgon. This structure served as a clear symbol of French colonial authority in Vietnam. The building’s scale and symmetry articulate the colonial vision of power imposed on the city at that time.

In 1975, the building’s role shifted from colonial administration to the seat of the people’s committee, symbolizing the profound political transformation that reshaped the everyday lives of Saigon’s residents. The clock’s steady ticking anchors time here as it did through colonial proclamations and now through the city’s municipal affairs. At this spot, the sounds of the city—street vendors calling, scooters weaving, the occasional drone of construction—blend with the building’s quiet dignity. Nearby buildings like the Café Apartments 42 reflect Saigon’s ongoing dance between the old and the new, reuse and reinvention, all part of the living pulse that defines this city.

Stop 9: Bạch Đằng Pier

Coordinates: 10.775240, 106.707002

Type/Specialty: Scenic Pier

Specific Examples: River views, boat rides, sunset scenery.

Orientation: Standing near the edge of Bạch Đằng Pier, take in the expanse of the Saigon River flowing beside you, its steady murmur meeting the occasional clatter of moored boats. The pier stretches along the water’s surface, framed by simple metal railings and weathered wooden planks underfoot, worn smooth by countless footsteps over decades.

Bạch Đằng Pier’s role as a boat quay is evident in every detail—the sturdy bollards for securing vessels, the wharves where wooden junks once unloaded goods, and the ripples left by motorboats ferrying passengers. This connection created a vital artery for commuters and goods, linking urban districts across the river’s current. The pier’s practical design, with straightforward docks and waiting areas, emphasizes function and historical continuity, making the river a conduit not just for trade, but also for memory and knowledge. The sounds of river traffic and the tactile grain of time-worn wood testify to Bạch Đằng Pier’s past as a ferry terminal to Thủ Thiêm and its ongoing life as a gateway bridging city, river, and the earth beneath.

A second chapter of history unfolds with the pier’s modern role as the departure point for tours to the Củ Chi Tunnels outside the city. This network of underground passages was a strategic lifeline during the Vietnam War, allowing Viet Cong fighters to move unseen beneath the earth. From this pier, visitors board vessels bound for those tunnels, linking the river’s surface to the subterranean corridors that shaped the conflict. Here, the river’s slow flow meets history’s quiet pressure, connecting past commerce with present-day engagement.
