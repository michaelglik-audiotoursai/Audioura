Stop 1: Notre-Dame Cathedral Basilica of Saigon

Coordinates: 10.779722, 106.699167

Type/Specialty: Historic Cathedral

Specific Examples: Stained glass windows, French colonial architecture, the iconic statue of the Virgin Mary.

Operational Details: Open daily from 8 AM to 5 PM, free entry.

Orientation: This tour will take you from the iconic Notre-Dame Cathedral Basilica of Saigon to the lively Thi Bar Saigon, a distance of approximately 5 km. Your first stop is Notre-Dame Cathedral Basilica of Saigon. From the main street, head east on Lê Duẩn Street, then turn left onto Công xã Paris Street. The cathedral will be on your right. You'll notice the bustling atmosphere of the city as you approach. Standing on the broad square before the cathedral, the eye is drawn upward to twin bell towers that stretch skyward, their red brick surface glowing softly in the sunlight. The ringing of bells echoes faintly from the towers, mingling with the hum of city life around you. This is the spot to take in the full height and scale of the cathedral’s façade, framed by the gentle rustle of leaves from nearby trees.

The cathedral you face rises from the ground with a deliberate weight, its bricks shipped all the way from Marseille—a material choice that reveals French ambitions to recreate a slice of Paris in the tropical climate of Saigon. These bricks, laid between 1877 and 1880, form the shell of what was originally named the Church of Saigon by French colonial administrators. The structure’s twin bell towers climb 58 meters (190 feet), marking a vertical claim in the cityscape that was once a colonial outpost. This architectural effort was more than aesthetic; it was a physical expression of the French presence and the spread of Catholicism during the colonial era. The story begins when Bishop Isidore Colombert laid the first stone, a ceremonial act that launched construction in the late 19th century. Colombert’s role was essential—his blessing and presence formalized this building as a spiritual anchor for the French Catholic community, which had outgrown its original church on Ngo Duc Ke Street. That first stone symbolized a commitment to establish a permanent religious center amid the rapidly expanding colonial city. Another chapter arrived in 1959, when the cathedral was renamed Notre-Dame Cathedral following the installation of the statue of Our Lady of Peace in its forecourt. This statue, standing serene and commanding, gave the church its new identity and became a focal point for devotion in the city. The name Notre-Dame, evoking the Virgin Mary, linked this Vietnamese cathedral to its French counterparts, anchoring it in a global Catholic tradition while standing firmly in Saigon’s evolving urban fabric. The cathedral’s walls, the imported bricks, and the soaring towers hold the memory of these moments—each a gesture of faith, power, and cultural exchange etched into the city’s pulse.

Stop 2: Saigon Central Post Office

Coordinates: 10.780000, 106.700000

Type/Specialty: Historic Post Office

Specific Examples: Beautiful arched ceilings, vintage mailboxes, the large map of Vietnam.



Orientation: The building’s wide arched entrance and green-painted ironwork invite your gaze upward, where the vaulted ceiling begins its sweep.

Between 1886 and 1891, this post office rose under the direction of Alfred Foulhoux, an architect whose name is often overshadowed by a common but mistaken attribution to Gustave Eiffel. Yet Foulhoux’s hand shaped the building you see: a grand hall inspired by Gothic and Renaissance motifs, blending French colonial ambition with local adaptation. The ironwork, soaring arches, and detailed tilework form a space both functional and majestic, designed to handle the increasing demands of postal services at the height of French Indochina. This structure’s inauguration was notably delayed. The journal Architecte constructeur: Revue du monde architectural et artistique, dated 15 September 1891, reported that the opening scheduled for 14 July had to wait until the Governor General returned to Saigon. This postponement underscored the colonial administration’s prioritization of official presence in ceremonial acts. The building thus entered Saigon’s urban life not as a mere utility but as a symbol of governance and modernity asserted through architecture. Before this post office was built, Gustave Eiffel had designed an earlier communication-related structure in Saigon: the Steel Wire Department in 1860. That building’s pioneering use of steel wire technology laid a foundation for the city’s connection to global networks. The sound of clerks sorting mail and the sight of vintage brass mailboxes along the walls ground this place in its original purpose.


Directions: From the post office, head south on Công xã Paris Street, then turn left onto Nguyễn Văn Bình. The book street will be on your left. The scent of fresh coffee wafts from nearby cafes as you stroll along.



Stop 3: Nguyễn Văn Bình Book Street

Coordinates: 10.780573, 106.699695

Type/Specialty: Book Street

Specific Examples: Numerous book stalls, cozy reading spots, local authors' works.



Orientation: From here, your ears catch the low murmur of pages turning, the distinct rustle of paper mingling with the city’s distant hum—a deliberate pedestrian lane punctuated by rows of book stalls and modest reading nooks. This is where Nguyễn Văn Bình Book Street unfolds, its compact presence woven into the fabric of Ho Chi Minh City’s cultural rhythm.

Nguyễn Văn Bình Book Street was created with the explicit aim of promoting reading culture in an age dominated by digital screens. Amid a city whose population surpassed 14 million as of 2025, this pedestrian street acts as a rare deliberate space for physical books—a refusal to let print vanish beneath modern technology’s glare. On arriving, the immediate impression comes from the narrowness of the street lined with small wooden bookstalls, their surfaces worn from decades of use but carefully arranged. Books of all kinds—literature, history, and local authors’ works—crowd shelves, some protruding into the street to invite casual browsing. This tactile atmosphere is punctuated by the subtle scent of ink and aged paper, a grounding contrast to the sleek glass towers visible beyond. Conversations here revolve quietly around texts, leaving space for introspection amid the city’s frenetic pulse. The story that anchors this venue is its deliberate transformation into a pedestrian cultural street, made concrete by the municipal authorities in an effort to hold onto the printed word's relevance. This reclaiming of public space for literature reflects a concern for preserving an essential form of knowledge transmission in Ho Chi Minh City, the country’s largest financial center whose economy contributes about a quarter of Vietnam’s total GDP.

Stop 4: Independence Palace

Coordinates: 10.777050, 106.695370

Type/Specialty: Historical Site

Specific Examples: The war rooms, beautiful gardens, the iconic tank at the entrance.

Operational Details: Open daily from 7:30 AM to 5 PM, entry fee is 40,000 VND.

Orientation: Standing at the wide entrance of the Independence Palace, you face its sturdy gates where history took a sudden, forceful turn. The palace’s low, angular silhouette contrasts with the taller buildings nearby, inviting you to step closer and feel the weight of its presence in this bustling city.

Its design, by architect Ngô Viết Thụ, reflects both modernity and authority, with clean lines and a sense of openness that belies the tense political climate of its era. Inside, the contrast between the calm gardens and the palace’s role in violent upheaval becomes clear. The tank’s impact shattered not just the gates but decades of conflict. The crash symbolized the sudden shift in power as General Nguyễn Văn Thiệu, who had used this palace as his home and office, fled the country just days earlier on 21 April 1975. This moment anchors the palace in a specific historical reality: a place where political power was both exercised and upended in a matter of days. The palace’s basement still holds the war command center, a claustrophobic space where officials once coordinated responses to a war now lost. Though today the building functions as a convention hall and museum, its walls carry the echoes of those decisive days. The Independence Palace stands as a living pulse of a community caught between power, conflict, and transition, connecting directly to the rhythms of Ho Chi Minh City you’ve traced from the harp you stopped at earlier on Nguyễn Văn Bình Book Street to the cathedral’s bells downtown.

Stop 5: War Remnants Museum

Coordinates: 10.779500, 106.692000

Type/Specialty: Museum

Specific Examples: Exhibits on the Vietnam War, military vehicles, powerful photography.

Operational Details: Open daily from 7:30 AM to 6 PM, entry fee is 40,000 VND.

Orientation: Stand just before the museum’s entrance on Võ Văn Tần Street, where the hum of Ho Chi Minh City’s traffic mixes with the distant calls of street vendors. The imposing façade of the War Remnants Museum rises ahead, a reminder that history here is not distant but etched deeply into the city's fabric.

The War Remnants Museum occupies the former United States Information Agency building, a detail that roots this place in the complex aftermath of conflict. The museum’s exhibits span the First Indochina War and the Vietnam War, making it a living ledger of the struggles that shaped this land. Within these walls, the weight of history settles in the silence between photographs and relics. One photograph, taken by Associated Press photographer Nick Ut on June 8, 1972, captures nine-year-old Phan Thị Kim Phúc running down a road, her skin blistered and burning from a napalm attack. This image is no mere snapshot; it froze a moment of agony that became an emblem of the war’s brutal reality. The blaze that scarred Kim Phúc was not just physical but symbolic, searing into global consciousness the devastating human cost of chemical warfare. A few steps away, another harrowing story unfolds through the lens of American photographer Ron Haeberle. His photographs, taken seconds before the My Lai massacre in 1968, show civilians moments before they were shot by U.S. soldiers. These images circulated widely, sparking international outrage and forcing a reckoning with the horrors committed in the name of war. The sound of the city pulses outside these walls, alive and relentless, yet inside, time is measured by the enduring presence of these stories.

Stop 6: Saigon Opera House

Coordinates: 10.776390, 106.702780

Type/Specialty: Cultural Venue

Specific Examples: Stunning façade, performances of ballet and opera, guided tours.

Operational Details: Open for tours daily from 9 AM to 5 PM, performance schedules vary.

Orientation: Standing just across from the Saigon Opera House, the best view unfolds straight ahead. The crisp lines of its façade frame an inviting stage-like presence, while the hum of city life brushes past your ears, mixing with the faint rustle of palm fronds nearby. From here, the fine details of the building’s architecture are in clear sight, the delicate plasterwork and wrought-iron balconies catching the light with subtle grace.

Unlike the sackbut you stopped at a moment ago, which echoed the city's religious heritage, this opera house brought the contours of European performance venues to Saigon’s bustling center. The choice of a 500-seat capacity was deliberate, accommodating ambitious productions while keeping a sense of intimacy. The theatre’s ornate façade, with its French colonial ornamentation, speaks not only of aesthetic taste but of a deliberate cultural statement in a city then under French influence. By 2025, the area known as Saigon transformed administratively into Phường Sài Gòn, becoming one of 168 wards in Ho Chi Minh City’s sweeping reform. The opera house witnessed this shift firsthand, standing where Saigon's historic core once was and now functioning within this new administrative landscape, a constant amid change. One story traces back to its designer, Eugene Ferret, whose task was not simply to build a venue but to craft a cultural beacon. His design balanced grandeur with practical scale, marking a venue capable of hosting high society's spectacles and public events alike. This approach shaped performances that required a refined setting without losing the engagement of the gathering crowd.

Stop 7: Ho Chi Minh City Hall (People's Committee Building)

Coordinates: 10.776470, 106.700970



Operational Details: Open to the public for photos, best viewed from outside.

Orientation: Standing at the corner of Lê Duẩn and Công xã Paris streets, the broad stone steps underfoot lead your eyes upward to a grand façade. The building’s pale walls catch the sunlight, their plasterwork crisp and detailed. To your right, neat gardens stretch out, their clipped greenery framing the formal symmetry of the structure.

The building before you was erected between 1898 and 1907, originally serving as the administrative center during French colonial rule. Its architecture merges French colonial and Renaissance elements, evident in the rounded arches and pilasters that rise along its exterior, the ornate ironwork on balconies, and the balustrades that crown the roofline. This combination was deliberately chosen to project the power and order of the colonial government, echoing styles used in France to assert cultural authority overseas. One story emerges from this period: as the French consolidated control over Saigon, this building became the nerve center for managing the city’s affairs. Decades later, after the tumultuous years marked by the independence struggle and conflict, this building transitioned from a symbol of foreign rule to one associated with Vietnamese governance. Near the Saigon Opera House you passed earlier, this hall’s gardens invite a quieter contrast to the ornate performance venue’s bustling activity. The air here carries a faint scent of jasmine from the nearby hedges, blending with the city’s concrete and greenery. This space, once reserved for colonial officials, now hums with the pulse of the city’s daily administration, a place where the rhythms of governance unfold amid the echoes of its layered past.

Stop 8: Café Apartments, 42 Nguyễn Huệ

Coordinates: 10.774089, 106.704056

Type/Specialty: Café Complex

Specific Examples: Various themed cafes, local snacks, rooftop views.

Operational Details: Open daily from 8 AM to 10 PM, prices vary by café.

Orientation: Standing on Nguyễn Huệ promenade, face the tall, narrow façade ahead, marked by a grid of open windows and balconies stacked vertically like stages of a theatrical play.

The building before you, known as the Café Apartments at 42 Nguyễn Huệ, reveals a remarkable transformation from its original role as a residential block. Within these ordinary apartments, lives interwove – foreign officials lived upstairs while local government workers resided below, a quiet testament to the city’s divided yet interconnected existence in a time of conflict. Each former apartment unit now blossoms as an individual café or shop, their unique decorations visible through open doors: mosaic tiles, wooden tables, hanging plants, aged brick walls breathing the texture of decades past. The soaring height of the building, once a place of private residence, becomes a vertical marketplace and a cultural hub, allowing visitors to ascend through varied atmospheres — from a coffee corner steeped in jazz records to rooftop terraces where Saigon’s pulse can be seen stretching toward the horizon. Amid this vibrant milieu, a surprising historical parallel lies scattered in plain sight. Though this tour winds through grand civic landmarks such as the People's Committee Building, a few streets away, the Café Apartments embody the city's gritty and flexible heart, where lives collided in wartime and now mingle freely in peace. As the sound of clinking glasses and soft chatter fills the stairwells, the building’s complex past — from wartime residency to present-day café complex — leaves a distinct impression. Nearby, the ward in Hanoi reminds us how Vietnam’s urban landscapes often reshape boundaries and functions: once under the Từ Liêm rural district’s jurisdiction at the time of Emperor Minh Mệnh’s 1831 order replacing Ming-style garrisons with Qing-style provincial governance, it now forms part of the modern urban district, reflecting the country’s evolving relationship with its spaces and identities.

Stop 9: Bạch Đằng Pier

Coordinates: 10.775240, 106.707002

Type/Specialty: Waterfront

Specific Examples: Boat tours, scenic views of the river, riverside dining.



Orientation: Standing near the edge of Bạch Đằng Pier, position yourself to face the broad sweep of the Saigon River. The pier’s open-air platforms and cluster of moored boats frame the water’s surface, inviting careful observation of the river’s movement and the connection this point holds to the lands beyond the city.

This pier’s roots run deep into the city's lifeblood — not just a place for watching ferries, but a crucial node in Ho Chi Minh City’s transport network, historically linking this bank of the Saigon River with Thủ Thiêm Pier on the opposite side. Boats loaded with goods, passengers, and news crossed between them, threading together lives and livelihoods. This function wasn’t merely local convenience; it anchored this waterfront as a vital artery of commerce and communication, shaping daily rhythms for neighborhoods that cluster around the riverbanks. The ferry terminal’s place as a transit hub reveals itself in the worn bollards and the steel cleats bolted into the pier’s edge — scuffed evidence of constant mooring through decades. The pier’s role in linking river communities remains woven into that geography. Beyond its daily motion, Bạch Đằng Pier holds a story of journeys that stretch history forward. One chapter began when this berth served as a departure point for those heading toward the Củ Chi Tunnels. In moments of calm, as the river shifts under your feet, the very air carries the quiet persistence of trade and travel. This isn’t simply a point on the map or a photographed vista; it is a locus where river currents once bore vessels laden with commerce and passengers. The worn surfaces of the pier’s decking, the metallic glints of mooring cleats, the distant splash of ferry wakes, these are the tangible reminders of connections that endure. Bạch Đằng Pier holds the river’s soft pulse and the city’s restless move onward, where countless crossings linked neighborhoods and histories, quiet now under the steady arc of the sun.
