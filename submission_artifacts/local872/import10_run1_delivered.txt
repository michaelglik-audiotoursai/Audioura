Stop 1: Notre-Dame Cathedral Basilica of Saigon

Coordinates: 10.779722, 106.699167

Type/Specialty: Historic Cathedral

Specific Examples: Stained glass windows, French colonial architecture, the iconic statue of the Virgin Mary.

Orientation: This tour will take you from the iconic Notre-Dame Cathedral Basilica of Saigon to the lively Thi Bar Saigon, a distance of approximately 5 km. Your first stop is Notre-Dame Cathedral Basilica of Saigon. The cathedral will be on your right. Standing on the broad square before the cathedral, the eye is drawn upward to twin bell towers that stretch skyward, their red brick surface glowing softly in the sunlight. The ringing of bells echoes faintly from the towers, mingling with the hum of city life around you. This is the perfect spot to appreciate the full height and scale of the cathedral’s façade, framed by the gentle rustle of leaves from nearby trees.

The cathedral before you rises with deliberate weight, its bricks shipped all the way from Marseille—a material choice that reveals French ambitions to recreate a slice of Paris in the tropical climate of Saigon. The twin bell towers climb 58 meters, marking a vertical claim in a city that was once a colonial outpost. This architectural effort was more than aesthetic; it was a physical expression of the French presence and the spread of Catholicism during the colonial era.

The story begins when Bishop Isidore Colombert laid the first stone, a ceremonial act that launched construction in the late 19th century. His blessing and presence formalized this building as a spiritual anchor for the French Catholic community, which had outgrown its original church on Ngo Duc Ke Street. That first stone symbolized a commitment to establish a permanent religious center amid the rapidly expanding colonial city.

A statue, standing serene and commanding, gave the church its new identity and became a focal point for devotion in the city. The name Notre-Dame, evoking the Virgin Mary, linked this Vietnamese cathedral to its French counterparts, anchoring it in a global Catholic tradition while standing firmly in Saigon’s evolving urban fabric. The cathedral’s walls, the imported bricks, and the soaring towers hold the memory of these moments—each a gesture of faith, power, and cultural exchange etched into the city’s pulse.

Directions: Enjoy the stroll! Continue to Saigon Central Post Office — it is about a minute's walk away.

Stop 2: Saigon Central Post Office

Coordinates: 10.780000, 106.700000

Type/Specialty: Historic Post Office

Specific Examples: Beautiful arched ceilings, vintage mailboxes, the large map of Vietnam.

Orientation: The building’s wide arched entrance and green-painted ironwork invite your gaze upward, where the vaulted ceiling begins its sweep.

Between 1886 and 1891, this post office was constructed under the direction of Alfred Foulhoux, an architect whose name is often overshadowed by the common but mistaken attribution to Gustave Eiffel. The ironwork, soaring arches, and detailed tilework create a space that is both functional and majestic, designed to meet the growing demands of postal services during the height of French Indochina. The inauguration of the building was notably delayed, highlighting the colonial administration’s emphasis on official presence in ceremonial acts. As a result, the post office entered Saigon’s urban life not just as a utility, but as a symbol of governance and modernity expressed through architecture.

Before this post office was built, Gustave Eiffel had designed an earlier communication-related structure in Saigon: the Steel Wire Department in 1860. That building’s pioneering use of steel wire technology laid a foundation for the city’s connection to global networks. Today, the sound of clerks sorting mail and the sight of vintage brass mailboxes along the walls ground this place in its original purpose.

Stop 4: Independence Palace

Coordinates: 10.777050, 106.695370

Type/Specialty: Historical Site

Specific Examples: The war rooms, beautiful gardens, the iconic tank at the entrance.

Orientation: Standing at the wide entrance of the Independence Palace, you face its sturdy gates where history took a sudden, forceful turn. The palace’s low, angular silhouette contrasts with the taller buildings nearby, inviting you to step closer and feel the weight of its presence in this bustling city.

Inside, the contrast between the calm gardens and the palace’s role in violent upheaval becomes clear. The tank’s impact shattered not just the gates but decades of conflict, symbolizing the sudden shift in power. General Nguyễn Văn Thiệu, who had used this palace as his home and office, fled the country just days earlier, on 21 April 1975. This moment anchors the palace in a specific historical reality—a place where political power was both exercised and upended in a matter of days.

The palace’s basement still holds the war command center, a claustrophobic space where officials once coordinated responses to a war now lost.

Stop 5: War Remnants Museum

Coordinates: 10.779500, 106.692000

Type/Specialty: Museum

Specific Examples: Exhibits on the Vietnam War, military vehicles, powerful photography.

Orientation: Stand just before the museum’s entrance on Võ Văn Tần Street, where the hum of Ho Chi Minh City’s traffic mixes with the distant calls of street vendors. The imposing façade of the War Remnants Museum rises ahead, a reminder that history here is not distant but etched deeply into the city's fabric.

The War Remnants Museum occupies the former United States Information Agency building, rooting this place in the complex aftermath of conflict. The museum’s exhibits span the First Indochina War and the Vietnam War, serving as a living ledger of the struggles that shaped this land. Within these walls, the weight of history settles in the silence between photographs and relics.

One photograph, taken by Associated Press photographer Nick Ut on June 8, 1972, captures nine-year-old Phan Thị Kim Phúc running down a road, her skin blistered and burning from a napalm attack. This image is no mere snapshot; it froze a moment of agony that became an emblem of the war’s brutal reality. The blaze that scarred Kim Phúc was not just physical but symbolic, searing into global consciousness the devastating human cost of chemical warfare.

A few steps away, another harrowing story unfolds through the lens of American photographer Ron Haeberle. His photographs, taken seconds before the My Lai massacre in 1968, show civilians moments before they were shot by U.S. soldiers. These images circulated widely, sparking international outrage and forcing a reckoning with the horrors committed in the name of war.

The sound of the city pulses outside these walls, alive and relentless, yet inside, time is measured by the enduring presence of these stories.

Stop 6: Saigon Opera House

Coordinates: 10.776390, 106.702780

Type/Specialty: Cultural Venue

Specific Examples: Stunning façade, performances of ballet and opera, guided tours.

Orientation: Standing just across from the Saigon Opera House, the best view unfolds straight ahead. The crisp lines of its façade frame an inviting stage-like presence, while the hum of city life brushes past your ears, mixing with the faint rustle of palm fronds nearby. From here, the fine details of the building’s architecture are in clear sight, the delicate plasterwork and wrought-iron balconies catching the light with subtle grace.

The Saigon Opera House was designed by Eugene Ferret, whose task was not simply to build a venue but to craft a cultural beacon. His design balanced grandeur with practical scale, resulting in a 500-seat capacity that accommodated ambitious productions while maintaining a sense of intimacy. The theatre’s ornate façade, with its French colonial ornamentation, reflects both aesthetic taste and a deliberate cultural statement in a city then under French influence. This approach created a venue capable of hosting high society's spectacles as well as public events, shaping performances that required a refined setting without losing the engagement of the audience. Over time, the opera house has remained a constant amid change. By 2025, the area known as Saigon was transformed administratively into Phường Sài Gòn, becoming one of 168 wards in Ho Chi Minh City’s sweeping reform. The opera house witnessed this shift firsthand, standing where Saigon's historic core once was and now functioning within this new administrative landscape.

Stop 7: Ho Chi Minh City Hall (People's Committee Building)

Coordinates: 10.776470, 106.700970

Type/Specialty: Government Building

Specific Examples: Impressive façade, beautiful gardens, historical significance.

Orientation: Standing at the corner of Lê Duẩn and Công xã Paris streets, the broad stone steps underfoot lead your eyes upward to a grand façade. The building’s pale walls catch the sunlight, their plasterwork crisp and detailed. To your right, neat gardens stretch out, their clipped greenery framing the formal symmetry of the structure.

The building before you was erected between 1898 and 1907, originally serving as the administrative center during French colonial rule. Its architecture merges French colonial and Renaissance elements, evident in the rounded arches and pilasters that rise along its exterior, the ornate ironwork on balconies, and the balustrades that crown the roofline. This combination was deliberately chosen to project the power and order of the colonial government, echoing styles used in France to assert cultural authority overseas. As the French consolidated control over Saigon, this building became the nerve center for managing the city’s affairs. Decades later, after years marked by the independence struggle and conflict, the building transitioned from a symbol of foreign rule to one associated with Vietnamese governance.

The air here carries a faint scent of jasmine from the nearby hedges, blending with the city’s concrete and greenery. This space, once reserved for colonial officials, now hums with the pulse of the city’s daily administration, a place where the rhythms of governance unfold amid the echoes of its layered past.
