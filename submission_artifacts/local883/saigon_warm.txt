Stop 1: Cathedral

Coordinates: 10.779722, 106.699167

Type/Specialty: Historic Landmark

Specific Examples: Stunning stained glass windows, impressive neo-Romanesque architecture, and a serene atmosphere.

Orientation: You are about to embark on a walking journey through Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam. This exploration will take you from the iconic Cathedral to the lively Thi Bar, covering an approximate straight-line distance of 5 km. As you traverse this bustling urban landscape, you will witness a blend of colonial architecture, bustling streets, and the rich history that has shaped the city’s vibrant character. Your first stop is Cathedral. From the central square at Nguyễn Huệ Street, head northeast on Lê Duẩn Street for until you reach the Cathedral. You'll notice the shift from modern buildings to the historic charm of the Cathedral. Stand by the square facing the cathedral’s soaring twin bell towers, where the broad façade of red bricks rises against the sky.

The Saigon Notre Dame Cathedral, completed in 1880 and designed by architect Jules Bourard, stands at Paris Commune Square where Lê Duẩn Boulevard meets Nguyễn Du Street. This location is often called the city’s kilometre zero, the point from which distances are measured outward. In 1959, a striking statue of Mary was installed in front of the cathedral. Carved from white granite, the statue stands 4.6 meters tall and weighs eight tons. Its presence transformed the square, creating a shared ritual space for both the faithful and visitors. Amid Ho Chi Minh City’s status as Vietnam’s largest financial centre, the cathedral offers a quiet counterpoint to the city’s economic bustle—a place where tradition meets rapid urban growth. The soundscape here blends the faint murmur of traffic and nearby vendors with the occasional ringing of church bells. These layers of noise and quiet, stone and human activity, reflect the rhythms that have shaped this square for over a century.

Directions: Head east for about a minute or two, and you'll find the Post Office waiting for you. Enjoy the stroll! It is about a minute's walk away.

Stop 2: Post Office

Coordinates: 10.780000, 106.700000

Type/Specialty: Historic Building

Specific Examples: Beautiful colonial architecture, intricate interior details, and a bustling atmosphere.

Orientation: This edifice’s arched windows and ornate ironwork invite a closer look. The broad, vaulted entrance opens into a vaulted hall where echoes mingle with the shuffle of visitors and the occasional ring of a bell from the postal counters.

Between 1886 and 1891, this structure was completed under the design of architect Auguste Henri Vildieu and his assistant Alfred Foulhoux. The Saigon Central Post Office’s architectural plan is often misattributed to Gustave Eiffel, famed for his Paris tower, but it was Vildieu who blended Gothic, Renaissance, and French influences with decorative details inspired by the East. This fusion appears in the building’s curved ironwork and the stylized motifs that frame the interior walls, creating a tactile dialogue between continents. Inside, the cool interior carries a soft murmur of conversation and the steady tick of postal business. The walls curve high overhead, with painted maps and vintage telephone booths standing as silent witnesses to the passage of messages across oceans and decades. The air holds the faint scent of paper and ink, a reminder that this has long been a crossroads of communication.

A pivotal chapter in this building’s history reaches back to 1864. Though the current post office had not yet risen, it was from Saigon that the first letters bearing Vietnam’s earliest postage, the “stork” stamp, were sent. These letters embarked from here to destinations worldwide, marking the city’s place in the global postal network. The creation of this stamp established a tangible identity for Vietnam’s mails, a symbol still remembered as the starting point of modern correspondence in the region.

Here, the flow of messages and human connection carried on beneath the same sky as the prayers and bells from across the square. The building stands as a monument to the era when the city was shaped by foreign hands and local life intertwined in the rhythm of the late nineteenth century.

Directions: From the Post Office, head east on Công xã Paris Street, then turn left onto Nguyễn Văn Bình Street; Book Street will be ahead after about 200 meters. You'll notice the transition to a quieter, more relaxed atmosphere filled with the scent of coffee and books.

Stop 3: Book Street

Coordinates: 10.780573, 106.699695

Type/Specialty: Cultural Spot

Specific Examples: Charming book stalls, cozy reading areas, and occasional literary events.

Orientation: Book Street is a cultural spot in Ho Chi Minh City. It is known for charming book stalls, cozy reading areas, and occasional literary events.

You are standing on Đường sách Nguyễn Văn Bình, also called Ho Chi Minh City Book Street, which opened on January 9, 2016. This 100-meter pedestrian lane in District 1 is lined with 20 book stalls, small bookstores, and cozy reading areas. Here, you can find books on literature, politics, society, science, and culture among other topics. The street is near landmarks like Chợ Bến Thành, Independence Palace, and the Waterfront, as well as old apartments converted into shops. You can often join cultural activities here, such as book fairs, author signings, discussions, and workshops. Book Street is a quiet place where readers, families, students, and tourists gather to enjoy books and art. Stop 4: Independence Palace

Coordinates: 10.777050, 106.695370

Type/Specialty: Historic Site

Specific Examples: Grand architecture, historical exhibits, and lush gardens.

Orientation: Approaching the Independence Palace, you find yourself drawn to its wide, low silhouette and the clean lines of Vietnamese modernist design. Main gates is where the drama of history first unfolds before your eyes, the heavy steel bars and the gate’s imposing structure framing the quiet gardens beyond.

The Independence Palace, completed on 31 October 1966, was designed by architect Ngô Viết Thụ in a style known as Vietnamese modernist. It served as the presidential residence and workplace of the Republic of Vietnam’s leaders during the period when Saigon was the nation’s capital. The palace’s calm, spacious gardens and clean geometric shapes stand in sharp contrast to the tension that once gripped this place.

On 27 February 1962, two dissident pilots from the South Vietnamese Air Force, Nguyễn Văn Cử and Phạm Phú Quốc, launched a bombing attack on the palace in an attempt to assassinate President Ngô Đình Diệm. Although the attack failed to kill Diệm, it severely injured his sister-in-law, Madame Nhu, a prominent and controversial figure, and tragically killed three palace staff members.

The most defining event in the palace’s history occurred on 30 April 1975, when communist North Vietnamese tanks crashed through its gates. The televised image of those tanks barreling through marked a profound shift for both the city and the country. After this event, the palace’s role changed from seat of power to symbol of reunification and peace.

Today, the soft rustling of leaves and the scent of tropical flowers offer a striking contrast to the intensity of the events that once unfolded here. The transition from the quiet of Book Street, where daily rhythms unfold in pages and ink, to the Independence Palace shows how the pulse of history can shift suddenly, through moments of conflict and resolution.

Directions: Head northwest for roughly 500 meters, and you'll arrive at the War Remnants Museum. Enjoy the stroll!

Stop 5: War Remnants Museum

Coordinates: 10.779500, 106.692000

Type/Specialty: Museum

Specific Examples: Powerful exhibits, historical artifacts, and poignant photography.

Orientation: Stand before the modest façade of 28 Võ Văn Tần Street, where the imprint of history is palpable in the building’s quiet solidity. The street hums faintly with urban life—the distant chatter, the occasional rattle of motorbikes—and the air carries a faint trace of diesel and dust.

The War Remnants Museum occupies the former United States Information Agency building, a fact that in itself speaks to the shifting tides of power and memory. First known as the Exhibition House for U.S. and Puppet Crimes, its name marked an explicit purpose: to document and expose the actions of the American forces and their Vietnamese allies during the conflict that ravaged this land. The museum’s opening in 1975 was not an isolated act but part of a continuing narrative of unveiling war crimes. It followed earlier exhibitions that revealed atrocities committed first by French colonial forces and later by the Americans, marking a continuum of exposure and reckoning.

Inside, the galleries house artifacts and images from both the First Indochina War and the Vietnam War, anchoring visitors in the physical evidence of decades-long turmoil. The exhibits are unvarnished and confrontational. The weight of iron helmets, tattered uniforms, and photographs bearing the scars of napalm burns confronts the senses. This legacy of confrontation with painful truths is embedded in the very structure of the museum. Every artifact and photograph is a fragment of a larger story, a testimony to lives disrupted and landscapes reshaped. The War Remnants Museum remains a solemn witness to the consequences of conflict, its presence a stark reminder amid the vibrant streets of Saigon. Beyond its walls, the city moves forward, its resilience forged not in forgetting but in enduring the marks of history.

Directions: Head east for about 1.2 kilometers, and you'll arrive at the beautiful Opera House. Enjoy the stroll!

Stop 6: Opera House

Coordinates: 10.776390, 106.702780

Type/Specialty: Cultural Venue

Specific Examples: Elegant architecture, vibrant performances, and a lively atmosphere.

The curved balconies, ornate arches, and floral motifs of the Saigon Opera House stretch upward, inviting a closer look at the craftsmanship and design. Completed on January 1, 1900, the Opera House was designed by the French architect Félix Olivier. Its flamboyant style stands out with elegant curves and intricate stonework, a deliberate statement of French presence and artistic taste at the turn of the century.

Between 1956 and 1975, the building’s story took a sharp political turn. When the Assembly changed to a bicameral legislature, the Opera House adapted to host both the Lower House (Hạ Nghị Viện) and the Senate (Thượng Nghị Viện). Imagine the building’s interior, once filled with the voices of lawmakers rather than opera singers, the red velvet seats beneath the gilded balconies witnessing the weight of political decisions. This transition from a cultural venue to a political chamber and back again reveals a rare layering of purpose.

The Opera House’s restoration in 1995 brought back its original grandeur and improved acoustics, ensuring that the building continues to host performances today while carrying the imprint of its complex past. The soft hum of the city outside contrasts with the refined architectural voice here—a symbol of Saigon’s blend of old colonial elegance and modern vitality.

Directions: Head west for roughly 200 meters, and you'll find yourself at City Hall. Enjoy the stroll!

Stop 7: City Hall

Coordinates: 10.776470, 106.700970

Type/Specialty: Government Building

Specific Examples: Stunning façade, well-maintained gardens, and historical significance.

The building before you was once known as the Hôtel de Ville, or Municipal Hall, a name that reflects its origins under French colonial administration. Constructed between 1898 and 1909, it was modeled after Parisian civic buildings, though with less ornate detail. It first served as the administrative house for colonial officials and was a stage for decisions that shaped Saigon’s political landscape well into the Vietnam War years. The local Vietnamese called it Dinh xã Tây, or French municipal palace, acknowledging both its foreign origins and its role as the city’s seat of power. Today, this building houses the Ho Chi Minh City People’s Committee, the modern government body that continues the work begun over a century ago. A steady breeze stirs the leaves in the garden that softens the stone front, while the quiet authority of the place speaks to the evolving rhythms of Saigon’s governance.

Stop 9: Bạch Đằng Pier

Coordinates: 10.775240, 106.707002

Type/Specialty: Scenic Spot

Specific Examples: Beautiful river views, bustling boat activity, and a relaxing atmosphere.

Orientation: Standing near the edge of Bạch Đằng Pier, the expansive view along the Saigon River opens up directly before you.

Bạch Đằng Pier has long been a vital part of the city’s river transport network, beginning as a boat quay that supported commerce and travel along the Saigon River. Over time, it evolved into a ferry terminal, linking this side of the river to Thủ Thiêm Pier across the water. This connection mirrored the older Thủ Thiêm Ferry line, creating a steady route for people and goods moving between districts. The simple, purposeful structure—its worn planks and bollards—bears the marks of countless arrivals and departures, each boat docking to unload passengers or cargo.

Today, the pier’s role extends beyond ferry service. It serves as a departure point for river tours to destinations such as the Củ Chi Tunnels. From here, travelers see a mix of natural and industrial scenes along the riverbanks, a reminder of the river’s ongoing role in connecting past and present. Bạch Đằng Pier stands as a thread tying together the city’s evolving transport infrastructure with the leisure journeys that now begin here, linking daily life to historical sites along the Saigon River.
