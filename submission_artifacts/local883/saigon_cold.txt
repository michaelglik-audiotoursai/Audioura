This is a step-by-step audio-guided walking tour of Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam.

Stop 1: Cathedral

Coordinates: 10.779722, 106.699167

Type/Specialty: Historical Landmark

Specific Examples: Stunning neo-Romanesque architecture, stained glass windows, and a peaceful courtyard.

Orientation: You are about to embark on a walking journey through Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam. This tour takes you on a 5 km path from the Cathedral to Thi Bar, transitioning through a dynamic urban landscape marked by colonial architecture and bustling streets. As you traverse this vibrant city, you will encounter a narrative woven through its notable landmarks, each revealing fragments of Saigon's forgotten past and its transformation over time. Your first stop is Cathedral. The Cathedral will be on your left. You might notice the vibrant street life as you approach. The Cathedral rises just beyond the roundabout of Paris Commune Square, where Lê Duẩn Boulevard and Nguyễn Du Street meet. Standing at the square’s edge provides a full view of its striking twin towers and the open courtyard before it, where a monumental statue commands attention. The surrounding bustle of Ho Chi Minh City’s centre filters in with the hum of traffic and distant voices, while the cathedral’s red brick exterior holds a quiet presence amid the city’s pulse.

The Saigon Notre Dame Cathedral, completed in 1880 and designed by French architect Jules Bourard, stands out in Ho Chi Minh City’s urban landscape with its neo-Romanesque style. Its sun-warmed walls are built from red bricks imported from Marseille, and the twin bell towers frame the sky, reflecting the influence of the French colonial era on the city’s architecture. The cathedral has long been central to the Catholic community, hosting numerous important religious ceremonies. Within these walls, thousands of priests have been ordained and archbishops welcomed, making the cathedral a living site of faith and leadership in Vietnam.

In 1959, a statue of Mary was installed in front of the cathedral. Carved from eight tons of white granite, the statue anchors the courtyard with its cool, smooth surface, contrasting with the warm brick of the cathedral and the surrounding tropical greenery. The installation of this statue marked a shift in the cathedral’s identity—from being known simply as Saigon’s church to being called the Cathedral of Our Lady—reflecting a deepening religious symbolism for the community.

Amid the dynamic cityscape, the cathedral remains a reminder of the layers of history and tradition woven into a rapidly modernizing metropolis. The sounds of daily commerce, the scent of nearby street food, and the steady flow of people around the square all connect this sacred space to the everyday rhythms of Saigon life.

Directions: Enjoy the stroll! Continue to Post Office — it is about a minute's walk away.

Stop 2: Post Office

Coordinates: 10.780000, 106.700000

Type/Specialty: Historic Postal Building

Specific Examples: Beautiful colonial architecture, a giant map of Vietnam, and bustling postal services.

Orientation: Face the grand façade adjoining the Saigon Notre-Dame Basilica, where the broad, arched windows and green-painted ironwork frame the entrance to this building. Pause here at the wrought iron gates to take in the symmetrical structure rising before you, its scale and detail inviting a closer step inside.

The Saigon Central Post Office you see before you was constructed between 1886 and 1891, designed by Auguste Henri Vildieu and his assistant Alfred Foulhoux. This building presents a deliberate fusion of Western architectural styles with Eastern decorative elements—a blend that reflects the complexity of Saigon under French colonial rule. The vaulted iron arches painted in emerald green and the intricate floral motifs woven through the moldings reveal this cultural interplay. Inside, the walls are lined with large maps, including a striking depiction of Vietnam and its surroundings, illustrating the geographical scope once vital to postal and colonial administration.

This space served not only to send letters and parcels but also to connect Saigon to the world. That connection began in 1864, when the first letters bearing Vietnam’s inaugural “stork” stamp were dispatched from this city to global destinations. Named for the bird depicted on it, the stamp marked Vietnam’s initial step into international postal communication, anchoring Saigon in the expanding networks of the nineteenth century.

Directions: From the Post Office, head south on Công xã Paris Street, then turn left onto Nguyễn Văn Bình Street. Book Street will be on your left after a short walk. You'll see locals enjoying coffee while browsing books.

Stop 3: Book Street

Coordinates: 10.780573, 106.699695

Type/Specialty: Cultural Book Market

Specific Examples: Rows of book stalls, cozy reading spots, and local coffee shops.

Orientation: Look for Book Street in Ho Chi Minh City.

Book Street is a cultural book market in Ho Chi Minh City. It is known for rows of book stalls, cozy reading spots, and local coffee shops. Book Street in Ho Chi Minh City officially opened and began operations on January 9, 2016. Book Street has transformed from a regular traffic street into a model public cultural space and a vibrant symbol of knowledge and human values in the modern urban center over 10 years of operation.

Stop 4: Independence Palace

Coordinates: 10.777050, 106.695370

Type/Specialty: Historical Site

Specific Examples: The grand architecture, historical exhibits, and the presidential rooms.

Orientation: Standing at the broad entrance of this imposing building, the visitor is met with a striking contrast between the calm, orderly gardens and the heavy, modernist concrete structure ahead. The spacious forecourt and the wide, flat steps invite a moment of pause before crossing the threshold into a place where history’s weight lingers palpably.

Completed on 31 October 1966, this building was designed by architect Ngô Viết Thụ in a style that blends modernist sensibilities with Vietnamese architectural elements. It replaced the old Norodom Palace, which had been bombed by dissident Republic of Vietnam Air Force pilots. Earlier, on February 27, 1962, President Ngô Đình Diệm survived an assassination attempt when two Republic of Vietnam Air Force pilots bombed the Independence Palace, but one bomb failed to detonate near him. After its completion, the palace served as the official residence and workplace of the president of the Republic of Vietnam, embodying both political power and the aspirations of a divided nation. One defining moment in its history occurred on 30 April 1975, a day whose echo still lingers in the corridors where decisions and destinies once unfolded.

Directions: Head northwest for roughly 500 meters, and you'll reach the War Remnants Museum. Enjoy the stroll!

Stop 5: War Remnants Museum

Coordinates: 10.779500, 106.692000

Type/Specialty: Museum

Specific Examples: Powerful exhibits on the Vietnam War, military vehicles, and impactful photography.

Orientation: Stand near the entrance on Võ Văn Tần Street, where the steady hum of the city outside dims slightly.

The War Remnants Museum first opened its doors to the public in 1975, when the city’s wounds from years of conflict were still raw. It began as the Exhibition House for U.S. and Puppet Crimes, a name that unmistakably declared its purpose: to present the harsh realities of the Vietnam War through images, objects, and stories. The museum chronicles not only the Vietnam War but also reaches back to the earlier First Indochina War, tracing the long arc of conflict that shaped this land. The opening of the museum was a deliberate act to confront the city and its visitors with the cost of decades-long conflict. It was not merely a collection of artifacts but a statement carved into the urban fabric, marking the transition from war to post-war remembrance. The name itself, later changed to the War Remnants Museum, carries the weight of history that the city would neither forget nor erase. Outside the front, the distant sound of motorbikes blends with occasional voices, contrasting sharply with the weight of what lies within these walls. Here, the story resumes its raw and unvarnished form, grounded in the aftermath of that event.

Directions: Head east for about 1.2 kilometers, and you'll reach the Opera House. Enjoy your stroll!

Stop 6: Opera House

Coordinates: 10.776390, 106.702780

Type/Specialty: Cultural Venue

Specific Examples: Stunning façade, classical performances, and elegant interiors.

Orientation: Standing on Lam Sơn Square, with the bustling Đồng Khởi Street stretching out behind you, the Saigon Opera House faces you directly, its delicate cream façade framed by ornate balconies and a graceful arched entrance.

The Saigon Opera House, or Nhà hát Thành phố, opened its doors on January 1, 1900, designed by French architect Félix Olivier. This venue, completed as the Opéra de Saïgon, is a prime example of French colonial architecture in Vietnam, sharing stylistic roots with counterparts in Hanoi and Haiphong. Its flamboyant façade, with elegant curves and carved motifs, stands in contrast to the modern cityscape around it. The delicate rustle of leaves and distant city sounds mingle here, blending the past with the present. What sets this building apart is not just its design but its complex role in Vietnam’s mid-20th century political scene. When South Vietnam adopted a bicameral legislature, the building became the seat for both the Lower House (Hạ Nghị Viện) and the Senate (Thượng Nghị Viện). This transformation turned a space built for performances into a stage of political deliberation during a turbulent era, intertwining culture and governance in one venue. The walls that once echoed with arias then carried debates shaping a nation’s fate in the Cold War’s shadow. The opera house’s story extends beyond politics. By 1995, it underwent careful restoration to preserve its original grandeur and improve acoustics for modern performances. This renewal wasn’t just cosmetic—it ensured that the blend of tradition and modernity continued, allowing the building to host theatrical and musical events that reflect Saigon’s evolving cultural identity. The subtle scent of aged wood and fresh paint still lingers, a reminder of this careful stewardship.

Directions: Head west for roughly 200 meters, and you'll arrive at City Hall. Enjoy the stroll!

Stop 7: City Hall

Coordinates: 10.776470, 106.700970

Type/Specialty: Government Building

Specific Examples: Grand architecture, illuminated at night, and surrounding gardens.

Orientation: Standing on the broad courtyard facing the building, the pale cream walls of City Hall rise with a solid grace.

This building was originally designed by Alfred Foulhoux and completed in 1890 as the Museum of Commercial Trade under French Indochina. Its first purpose was to showcase Southern Vietnam’s products and goods, reflecting the colonial drive to catalogue and control regional commerce. Soon after, it was transformed into the residence of the Governor of Cochinchina. Henri Éloi Danel, who held this post until 1898, lived here, marking the shift from a commercial exhibition space to a seat of colonial power. During the World War II era, control of the building changed hands multiple times, reflecting the turbulence of a city caught between colonial powers and emerging national movements. The building’s enduring presence mirrors the complex layers of governance that have shaped Saigon’s civic identity.

Stop 9: Bạch Đằng Pier

Coordinates: 10.775240, 106.707002

Type/Specialty: Scenic Waterfront

Specific Examples: Beautiful views of the river, boat tours, and relaxing promenades.

Bạch Đằng Pier was once a bustling boat quay, where river traffic—both cargo and passengers—converged. Over time, it became a ferry terminal linking to Thủ Thiêm Pier, facilitating transport across the water. The ferry service between Bạch Đằng and Thủ Thiêm echoed the older Thủ Thiêm Ferry line, which for decades connected communities separated by the Saigon River. This route shaped daily life, allowing workers, traders, and families to cross efficiently before bridges transformed transit in the region.

Today, midday sunlight flickers off the worn wooden planks beneath your feet, and the salty, moist air carries the scent of river and diesel, mingling with distant chatter. Though peaceful now, the pier still serves as a point of embarkation. One story ties this pier to a pivotal chapter in modern Vietnamese history: Bạch Đằng Pier is the departure point for tours to the Củ Chi Tunnels, an extensive network of underground passages constructed during the Vietnam War to provide concealment, supply routes, and living quarters for Viet Cong fighters. The tunnels, a labyrinth of survival carved into the earth, symbolize the resilience and strategy of that conflict. From here, visitors set off to trace those subterranean paths, connecting this tranquil riverfront directly to a fraught chapter in the nation’s past.

Having already passed the Café Apartments at 42 Nguyen Hue, where life found new rhythms in repurposed residential blocks, you now stand at a place that offers another dimension of Saigon’s layered identity: a site of departure, continuity, and remembrance, poised between the gentle flow of river waters and the weight of history.
