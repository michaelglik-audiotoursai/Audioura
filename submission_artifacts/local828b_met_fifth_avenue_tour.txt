Stop 1: Washington Crossing the Delaware

Coordinates: 40.779444, -73.963333

The museum is open daily except Wednesday, 10:00 to 5:00. Admission is 30 dollars for adults; children 12 and under go free.

Your first stop is Washington Crossing the Delaware. Stand directly in front of Washington Crossing the Delaware, close enough to see the crisp outlines of each figure yet far enough to take in the entire composition. From this vantage point, the painting’s dramatic diagonal sweep of the boat cutting through icy waters becomes clear, revealing the tension and resolve embodied in every gesture. The interplay of light on the frozen river and the determined faces of the men emerges fully here.

Because the painting entered The Met’s permanent collection, it has been preserved and displayed for visitors to study and appreciate since 1897. The painting was rediscovered in The Met’s basement between 1931 and 1932 after being out of public view. This rediscovery led to its reinstallation in the museum galleries. Because of this action, the painting became visible to a wider audience again, ensuring its continued significance within The Met’s holdings. The medium allows for rich layering of paint that captures the cold, sharp atmosphere of a winter night. The composition arranges figures in a tightly packed boat, leaning forward against the swirling river ice, conveying a palpable sense of struggle and unity. Leutze’s choice to depict the scene with a luminous dawn breaking behind them illuminates the central figure of George Washington, who stands upright, steady and commanding, symbolizing leadership amid uncertainty. At the time Leutze painted this piece, he was living in Germany, where he sought to inspire democratic ideals with history painting. This work marked a turning point by connecting American revolutionary history with the broader European movements for liberty through grand, emotional imagery. The painting’s dramatic composition and heroic portrayal sought to evoke determination and hope, qualities Leutze believed essential for both nations. This act was a decisive event in the American Revolutionary War, boosting the morale of the revolutionaries. The painting reflects this pivotal decision, showing not only a physical crossing but the crossing into renewed possibility. The size of the canvas and the detailed brushwork preserve the tension and fragile balance between danger and determination. The cold, splintered ice around the boat and the expressions of the men evoke the harsh conditions faced, anchoring the historical moment within the physical realities of winter warfare. The interplay of shadow and light both dramatizes and clarifies the narrative, revealing the painting’s purpose as much more than decoration: it is a visual hymn to resolve in the face of adversity.

Stop 2: The Death of Socrates

Coordinates: 40.779444, -73.963333

In 1787, Jacques-Louis David painted The Death of Socrates, depicting the scene recorded in Plato's Phaedo. Sentenced to drink poison hemlock on charges of introducing strange gods and corrupting the youth of Athens, Socrates refused to flee when given the opportunity. Because the painting remains in the museum’s collection, it continues to shape public understanding of classical philosophy and art history. This oil on canvas measures just over seven feet tall and nearly six feet wide, allowing David to render every figure with striking clarity. The work’s Neoclassical style, characterized by a crisp, linear technique and balanced composition, underscores the moral gravity of the scene. Socrates, illuminated with a serene dignity, reaches for the hemlock while his followers display a range of grief and disbelief. The use of sculptural light and anatomical precision reflects David’s commitment to classical ideals and his desire to elevate Socrates as a symbol of reason and sacrifice. At the moment David painted this, he was establishing himself as a leading voice in French art, just two years before the French Revolution would radically reshape his life and career. The Death of Socrates marks a turning point where David embraces themes of civic virtue and moral steadfastness, which would later inform his political allegiance and artworks. The emotional weight of the scene is palpable—the stoicism of Socrates contrasts with the anguished gestures of his disciples, capturing the contrast between philosophical conviction and human loss. This tension echoes the historical drama of sacrifice and principle, resonating with earlier stops on this tour, such as Washington Crossing the Delaware, where courage in the face of danger also defined a pivotal moment. The Metropolitan Museum of Art is the fourth-largest museum in the world by floor area, and it holds this painting as part of its commitment to preserving works that document pivotal moments in Western thought and history. The scale and technique of David’s painting allow it to stand alongside other grand historical canvases here, offering a lens on the classical past that shaped modern ideals. A second story of note is that David’s portrayal shaped later visual culture by fixing the image of Socrates’ death in the collective imagination.

Directions: Next: The Harvesters.

Stop 3: The Harvesters

Coordinates: 40.779444, -73.963333

Orientation: As you stand just a few feet from The Harvesters, focus your gaze on the entire panel to the left side of the painting. From this vantage point, the layered depth of the scene unfolds clearly—the sprawling golden fields in the foreground, the cluster of figures busy with sickles and baskets, and the distant village framed by a calm sky. This position reveals the rhythm of the composition, where human activity and landscape interlock, a detail lost if viewed too close or from the center.

Pieter Bruegel the Elder completed this oil painting on wood in 1565. A wealthy merchant from Antwerp commissioned it as part of a six-part cycle depicting the seasons of the year, designed for a dining room. Five of those original six paintings survive today. The painting depicts a summer scene of peasants harvesting wheat, with a broad view that extends from workers cutting grain to others resting under a tree, and finally to a village and distant hills. Bruegel’s use of oil on wood allowed for meticulous layering and subtle tonal shifts, lending the wheat fields a tactile quality—the golden hues and their shadows form a textured carpet that both grounds and energizes the scene. The composition avoids a singular focal point; instead, it encourages the eye to wander through various human moments within the natural environment. Unlike the dramatic moral intensity you might remember from the Death of Socrates, here there is a subdued, almost meditative attention to the daily labor sustaining these lives. At this moment, he was moving away from purely religious or allegorical subjects toward scenes rooted in observation of everyday people. This shift marked a turning point in his work, as he sought to record the rhythms of peasant life with a sympathetic, steady gaze rather than moral judgment. His choice of oil on wood was typical for Netherlandish painters of the time but allowed him to achieve remarkable detail in both figures and landscape. The Harvesters conveys a quiet serenity that contrasts sharply with the tension you encountered in the Death of Socrates. The scene’s steady pulse—the bending postures, the shared meal, the distant church steeple—speaks to a world shaped by the seasons and human toil, a world where time moves slowly and predictably. This work is part of a collection that benefits from The Met’s status as the largest art museum in the Americas and one of the top five most visited globally, drawing millions annually who witness firsthand this intimate portrayal of 16th-century rural existence.

Directions: Proceed to La gran ola.

Stop 4: The Great Wave off Kanagawa

Coordinates: 40.779444, -73.963333

Orientation: Stand slightly to the left of this framed print to catch the full sweep of the wave’s curl and the intricate tonal gradations on the glossy surface, which reveal the technical mastery of the photographic process used.

La gran ola, Sète, made in 1857 by Gustave Le Gray, a 19th-century French photographer known for innovation, is a silver albumen print produced from glass negatives—an exacting photographic medium that allowed Le Gray to capture remarkable detail and texture. In turn, this innovation changed photography by enabling clearer and more dynamic portrayals of natural scenes, a breakthrough that affected photographers’ ability to depict light and movement more faithfully. His work here stands as an important moment in photographic history, demonstrating a blend of artistic vision and technical experimentation. Here, nature’s enormity overwhelms the human scale altogether.

Directions: Your final stop in The Metropolitan Museum of Art: The Musicians.

Stop 5: The Musicians

Coordinates: 40.779444, -73.963333

Orientation: Stand slightly to the left of the canvas, where the soft overhead light reveals the subtle gradations in shadow and the delicate textures of the musicians’ skin and garments. From this vantage, Caravaggio’s mastery of chiaroscuro—the interplay of light and dark—becomes most evident, bringing forward the figures with a three-dimensional presence that would fade if viewed from dead center.

Around 1595, Caravaggio entered the household of Cardinal Francesco Maria del Monte, an avid supporter of music who owned a collection of expensive instruments. Del Monte commissioned The Musicians, which is believed to be the first painting the artist produced expressly for him. To compose the four figures, Caravaggio had models pose directly from life and used the Cardinal's own instruments as props. This commission placed the painting within the cardinal’s distinguished collection, ensuring its eventual arrival here at The Metropolitan Museum of Art. The work is an oil on canvas, a medium that Caravaggio manipulated with painstaking attention to texture and light, allowing the figures to emerge from the darkness as if illuminated by a single, unseen candle. One holds a lute, another a violin, while the others appear to be tuning or preparing to play, each figure linked by an unspoken, shared purpose. The use of dramatic light and shadow in The Musicians not only animates the figures but also prefigures techniques that would influence Baroque painting across Europe. The joy in The Musicians arises from this vivid sense of presence—the momentary suspension of time when music and companionship converge. It is a scene that celebrates human creativity and connection, made luminous through Caravaggio’s deliberate choice to envelop the figures in shadow and light.

In 2000, its permanent collection had over two million works; it currently lists a total of 1.5 million works.
