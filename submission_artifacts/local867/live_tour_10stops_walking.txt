Stop 1: Notre-Dame Cathedral Basilica of Saigon

Coordinates: 10.779722, 106.699167

Type/Specialty: Historical Cathedral

Specific Examples: Stained glass windows, French colonial architecture, peaceful gardens.

Orientation: As you traverse the urban landscape, you will encounter a blend of iconic colonial architecture and modern life, set against the backdrop of a city that pulses with energy and history. Your first stop is Notre-Dame Cathedral Basilica of Saigon. From Nguyễn Huệ Walking Street, head southeast on Nguyễn Huệ toward Lê Lợi, turn right onto Lê Lợi, then turn left onto Công xã Paris. The cathedral will be on your right. Stand near the broad front steps of the cathedral, where the two tall bell towers rise above the city square. From here, the red brick exterior catches the afternoon light.

The cathedral before you was erected by French colonial administrators between 1877 and 1880, during a time when Saigon was transforming under French control. Its walls are built from bricks imported from Marseille, creating a physical link between this place and France’s distant empire. These bricks, worn smooth by decades of tropical rains and sun, bear witness to the persistence of colonial ambitions that shaped the city’s skyline. Originally called the Church of Saigon, the building was intended to serve the growing French Catholic community after the first church nearby proved too small. The two bell towers soar 58 meters high, serving as visible landmarks that once announced the rhythm of church life to a city still finding its identity under colonial rule.

In 1959, a statue of Our Lady of Peace was installed in the forecourt, transforming the space into a destination for religious pilgrimage. This statue connected local Vietnamese Catholics with a symbol of hope and calm amid the turbulence of the mid-20th century, marking a shift in the cathedral’s role from a colonial monument to a focal point of local faith and community gathering.

During the Vietnam War, the cathedral became a sanctuary for many. As bombs fell and streets emptied, locals found refuge within its walls, turning to prayer amid the conflict. The quiet here today—from the faint ringing bells to the gentle rustle of leaves in the courtyard—carries the memory of those who sought solace in a city divided. As you prepare to leave this square, the old Saigon shaped by these forces continues just beyond the cathedral steps.

Directions: Enjoy the stroll! Continue to Saigon Central Post Office — it is about a minute's walk away.

Stop 2: Saigon Central Post Office

Coordinates: 10.780000, 106.700000

Type/Specialty: Historical Post Office

Specific Examples: Architectural details, old postal services, local souvenirs.

Orientation: Standing just beside the Saigon Notre-Dame Basilica, where you last paused, direct your gaze toward the grand façade across Paris Commune Square. The arched windows and curved ironwork of the Saigon Central Post Office draw the eye upward, framing a structure that holds more than the mail it once sorted. Step close enough to hear the murmur of clerks and the faint click of typewriters through open doorways.

Alfred Foulhoux, the architect credited with the design of the Saigon Central Post Office, created a building that blends Gothic, Renaissance, and French stylistic elements into a cohesive whole. The inauguration in 1891 marked not just the opening of a postal facility, but the establishment of a nexus for Saigon’s connections to the wider world. Inside, the scent of aged wood from counters and pigeonhole mail slots mixes with the faint hum of modern postal activity. The tiled floor beneath your feet reflects filtered light from ornate windows, each pane a silent witness to over a century of messages sent and received.

Delicate iron arches span the interior space, carrying the weight of the roof and reflecting the legacy of industrial design. Gustave Eiffel’s name is often mistakenly linked to this building, but his actual contribution to Saigon’s architectural heritage came earlier, in 1860, with the design of the Saigon Steel Wire Department. That earlier work introduced iron construction techniques that influenced later projects, including the engineering details seen here. These arches echo a lineage of French engineering, even without Eiffel’s direct involvement.

The post office stands not far from the cathedral, forming a dialogue between sacred space and the secular pulse of daily life in Saigon. In 2023, Architectural Digest ranked the Saigon Central Post Office second among the world’s most beautiful post offices, recognizing its enduring elegance and the detailed craftsmanship visible in its ironwork and façades. The building remains a living monument to the era when French colonial administration sought to imprint permanence and order through architecture, and when communication was a tangible, tactile act. The square outside carries conversations and footsteps, but within these walls, the weight of time is tangible in every arch and counter. The story of this place is both a record of colonial ambition and a chapter in Saigon’s evolving urban fabric.

Directions: From the post office, head east on Công xã Paris, turn right onto Nguyễn Văn Bình, and walk straight. The smell of coffee and the sight of readers engrossed in books will greet you. Continue to Nguyễn Văn Bình Book Street — it is about a minute's walk away.

Stop 4: Independence Palace

Coordinates: 10.777050, 106.695370

Type/Specialty: Historical Site

Specific Examples: Historical exhibits, beautiful gardens, war rooms.

Orientation: Standing just outside the wide gates, your gaze meets the solid lines of a building that once held the full weight of a divided nation’s government. The broad courtyard before you is not just open space but the stage where history shifted with a crash that echoed far beyond these walls. From here, the green of the palace gardens frames the modernist concrete and glass structure designed by Ngô Viết Thụ, inviting you to step closer and trace the moments that shaped Vietnam’s recent past.

The palace’s appearance reflects a new era of governance and authority, rooted in mid-20th-century ideals of progress. One day here, April 30, 1975, transformed the course of Vietnamese history, when communist forces ended the Vietnam War by seizing this very palace. After reunification, the palace continued to function as a government and presidential office until 1976, when the capital moved to Hanoi and its official role shifted. Today, the building serves as a convention hall for state events and as a museum open to the public, where visitors encounter the layers of political power and upheaval. The Independence Palace holds the tangible imprint of a pivotal day in history, standing as a reminder of how the rhythms of governance, conflict, and change converge in a single place.

Stop 5: War Remnants Museum

Coordinates: 10.779500, 106.692000

Type/Specialty: Museum

Specific Examples: War exhibits, photographs, military vehicles.

Orientation: Stand just inside the entrance on Võ Văn Tần Street, where the hum of the city feels distant, and the weight of the building’s dark façade draws the eye.

The War Remnants Museum occupies a space once used by the United States Information Agency. Among the most harrowing stories here is that of a single photograph taken by Nick Ut, a Vietnamese-American photojournalist. Elsewhere, the museum preserves the names of victims from a February 1969 U.S. attack. Over time, the museum has evolved from a place named explicitly to indict foreign crimes to one that now focuses on education and reflection, mirroring the city’s own passage from war to peace. The stories and objects gathered here hold fast the memory of destruction and resilience, their silence louder than any call for victory.
