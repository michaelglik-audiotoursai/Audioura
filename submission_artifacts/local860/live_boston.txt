Stop 1: Old North Church

Coordinates: 42.366325,-71.0544

Type/Specialty: Historic Church

Specific Examples: Famous for its role in the American Revolution, the iconic steeple, and beautiful colonial architecture.

Orientation: You are about to embark on a walking journey through Boston Common and Old North Church walk, Boston. Your first stop is Old North Church. Continue straight for about 0.2 miles until you reach the church on your left. Notice the quaint cobblestone streets that add to the historic charm of the area. The church’s brick walls rise solid and familiar, their worn surface whispering of centuries of footsteps and footsteps yet to come.

The Old North Church, officially Christ Church in the City of Boston, has stood here since 1723, making it the oldest surviving church building in Boston. Its wooden steeple, visible for miles, became a beacon not just of faith but of revolution. On the night of April 18, 1775, Robert Newman and John Pulling, both members of the church congregation, climbed the narrow stairs to the bell tower. The church’s role in this moment of anticipation and danger links it directly to the birth of independence. The scent of aged wood and the soft echo of footsteps inside the nave mingle with the maritime breeze drifting from nearby Boston Harbor. Today, the Old North Church continues to hold Episcopal services, its congregation maintaining the rhythms of worship alongside the steady presence of tourists and history enthusiasts. As one of the landmarks on Boston’s Freedom Trail and a designated National Historic Landmark, this church stands as a tangible bridge between colonial daily life and the extraordinary calls to action that shaped a nation.

Stop 2: Boston Common

Coordinates: 42.35509,-71.06573

Type/Specialty: Public Park

Specific Examples: Expansive green spaces, the Frog Pond, and the iconic Soldiers and Sailors Monument.

Orientation: Stand near the central green expanse of Boston Common, where the wide stretch of grass opens beneath the sky, framed by the bustle of Tremont, Park, Beacon, Charles, and Boylston Streets. The distant rustle of city life meets the soft footsteps on the park’s pathways, inviting a moment to absorb the layers beneath this familiar scene.

Boston Common holds the distinction of being the oldest public park in the United States, a title it has carried since its establishment in 1634. Spanning 50 acres, its boundaries form a pentagon that anchors downtown Boston—a green heart amidst the city’s relentless pulse. This park, part of the Emerald Necklace that threads through Boston’s neighborhoods, stands as a living witness to centuries of transformation.

One chapter of this land’s history reveals a stark contrast to the peace found here today. In 1768, British Redcoats set up camp on this very ground, beginning an eight-year occupation that stretched through the tense years before the American Revolution. The uniformed soldiers pitched tents and drilled with muskets, their presence a constant reminder of imperial authority and the growing unrest that would soon ignite rebellion across the colonies. Beneath the grass, traces of colonial fortifications linger—silent evidence of a time when this park was both a stage and a stake in the fight for control.

Another story is etched into the Central Burying Ground on the Boylston Street side of the Common. Amid the current sounds—the laughter of children near the Frog Pond, the crunch of leaves underfoot, the steady hum of nearby traffic—Boston Common remains a place where the struggles and aspirations of the past persist alongside daily life. As you take in the stretch of grass and the monuments beyond, this space continues to serve as a gathering place—its legacy inspiring new movements and assemblies, echoing the voices that once filled its open air.
