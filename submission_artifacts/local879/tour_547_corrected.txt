Stop 1: Notre-Dame Cathedral Basilica of Saigon

Coordinates: 10.779785, 106.699020

Type/Specialty: Historical Landmark

Specific Examples: Stunning neo-Romanesque architecture, impressive stained glass windows, and the iconic statue of the Virgin Mary.

Orientation: This exploration stretches approximately 6 kilometers from the Notre-Dame Cathedral Basilica of Saigon to Thi Bar Saigon, guiding you through the heart of a sprawling city where French colonial architecture coexists with the undeniable pulse of modern life. In this vibrant urban landscape, stories of survival and transformation are etched into both the bustling street life and the silent remnants of history, revealing a city that has continuously reinvented itself. Your first stop is Notre-Dame Cathedral Basilica of Saigon. From the main street of Lê Duẩn, walk towards the intersection with Công xã Paris, and you'll see the cathedral ahead. Standing on the broad square facing the cathedral, begin by noting the twin bell towers rising sharply against the sky, each reaching 58 meters high. The red brick walls catch the sunlight, their imported hue distinct amid the urban buzz. The statue of the Virgin Mary stands near the entrance, drawing a steady flow of visitors. The façade, with its large stained glass windows, fills the space with shifting colored light that flickers on the pavement.

The Notre-Dame Cathedral Basilica of Saigon was established by French administrators under the name Church of Saigon, with construction spanning from 1877 to 1880. Designed by Jules Bourard, the cathedral’s walls are built from red bricks imported from Marseille, France, a tangible trace of its colonial origins. The twin bell towers, each soaring 58 meters, punctuate the city skyline and were part of the original design that marked a new religious and cultural presence in Saigon.

In 1959, the cathedral’s forecourt gained a new focal point with the installation of the statue of Our Lady of Peace, known locally as the Virgin Mary. This statue transformed the space from a colonial-era churchyard into a site of active pilgrimage and became a gathering point for Catholic communities throughout Vietnam, reflecting a ritual rhythm of devotion that continues today. From this moment, the name Notre-Dame Cathedral came into common use, linking the building’s identity directly to the presence of this figure.

A striking episode occurred in 2005 when the statue reportedly shed tears, an event that drew thousands of believers and visitors to this spot. The crowd’s murmur, the soft footsteps on the stone square, and the distant ringing of the bells create a layered soundscape that blends history with present devotion.

Directions: Exit the cathedral and head southeast on Công xã Paris, then turn left onto Lê Duẩn; the post office will be on your right. The sound of bustling tourists fills the air as you walk. Continue to Saigon Central Post Office — it is about a minute's walk away.

Stop 2: Saigon Central Post Office

Coordinates: 10.779852, 106.699844

Type/Specialty: Architectural Landmark

Specific Examples: Beautiful colonial architecture, intricate interior details, and historical maps of Vietnam.

Orientation: Standing before the grand façade of the Saigon Central Post Office, your gaze is first drawn to the soaring vaulted ceilings and the ornate ironwork framing the glass roof above. The building faces the open expanse of Paris Commune Square, with the red brick and cream stucco walls unfolding beside you.

The present building here was constructed between 1886 and 1891, designed by the French architect Auguste Henri Vildieu and his assistant Alfred Foulhoux, which marked a transition in Saigon’s urban landscape during the French colonial era. The post office’s architecture blends Gothic and Renaissance elements, visible in the pointed arches and the intricate ironwork decorating its interior. The choice of the stork, an elegant, long-legged bird, became a recognizable emblem of Vietnam’s entry into global correspondence. Inside, the vaulted ceiling arches over rows of counters where clerks once handled telegrams and letters, a tangible echo of the colonial administration’s efforts to modernize communication. Nearby, Nguyễn Văn Bình Book Street awaits, inviting a quieter reflection on the legacy of letters and stories carried through this very building.

Directions: From the post office, head northeast on Lê Duẩn, then turn right onto Nguyễn Văn Bình; the book street will be on your left. You might catch the scent of fresh coffee wafting through the air. Continue to Nguyễn Văn Bình Book Street — it is about a minute's walk away.

Stop 4: Independence Palace

Coordinates: 10.776994, 106.695305

Type/Specialty: Historical Site

Specific Examples: The grand architecture, historical exhibitions, and the famous tank from the fall of Saigon.

Orientation: Stand just outside the main gates, where the scars of history remain visible in the cracked concrete and the heavy ironwork that once barred entry under tension. The wide, open plaza invites a slow gaze up to the rectangular windows and sharp edges crafted by architect Ngô Viết Thụ, whose design began taking shape on 1 July 1962 under the order of President Ngô Đình Diệm.

After the old Norodom Palace was bombed by dissident pilots, Diệm commissioned Ngô Viết Thụ to create a new seat of government. The palace was completed and inaugurated on 31 October 1966 by General Nguyễn Văn Thiệu, who made it his home and workplace. Though it once housed power and turmoil, the palace today serves as a government convention hall and museum open to the public. Inside, an exhibition displays photographs of Buddhist monk Thich Quang Duc, whose self-immolation in protest against Diệm’s regime remains a stark reminder of the fierce conflicts beneath the surface of this place’s history. The low murmur of footsteps and occasional clicks of camera shutters mingle with the faint scent of aged wood and polished stone, grounding visitors in the very site where modern Vietnam’s future pivoted. The Reunification Palace is a remarkable, well-preserved historical site with an authentic 1960s retro-modern interior. The Independence Palace is also publicly and officially known as the Reunification Convention Hall or simply Reunification Hall.

Directions: Head northwest for roughly 400 meters, and you'll find the War Remnants Museum waiting for you. Enjoy the stroll!

Stop 5: War Remnants Museum

Coordinates: 10.779511, 106.692090

Type/Specialty: Museum

Specific Examples: Powerful exhibits on the Vietnam War, military equipment, and poignant photographs.

Orientation: Position yourself to best view this location.

The War Remnants Museum was established on 4 September 1975 under the stewardship of the Ho Chi Minh City government. It was first known as the Exhibition House for U.S. and Puppet Crimes. The museum occupies the former United States Information Agency building—a structure once dedicated to American cultural diplomacy, now transformed into a space that critiques that very presence and its consequences. Its opening marked a continuation of a practice that began much earlier: exhibitions exposing the crimes of foreign powers engaged in Vietnam, first the French colonizers and then the Americans from 1954 onward. This lineage places the site not only as a repository of historical objects but also as a chapter in ongoing efforts to confront and document the brutalities witnessed on this soil.

Inside, the chilling presence of war is neither softened nor abstracted. The walls display photographs and documents that recount the First Indochina War alongside the Vietnam War, presenting a raw chronicle rarely found in other city landmarks. The museum’s exhibits extend that story, placing the political history inside the palace into the wider context of the human cost documented here. The metal of rusted war machines outside, the faded images inside, and the very building itself together bear witness to a history carved into the city’s soul.

Stop 6: Saigon Opera House
Coordinates: 10.776613, 106.703170
Type/Specialty: Performing Arts Venue
Specific Examples: Stunning French colonial architecture, various performances, and guided tours.
Orientation: Standing on Lê Lợi Boulevard, you face the ornate façade of the Saigon Opera House, its intricate details catching the midday light. The façade faces Lam Sơn Square, where the hum of city life — honking scooters and distant chatter — blends with the occasional clip-clop of horses from a nearby carriage. The opera house’s delicate arches, carved stone balustrades, and floral motifs invite close inspection, revealing craftsmanship that draws the eye upward toward the roofline, where elegant curves meet the sky. The Saigon Opera House opened on January 1, 1900, designed by Félix Olivier along with Eugène Ferret and Ernest Guichard, French architects who crafted this 500-seat venue in the flamboyant style popular in France at the time. It was built as the Opéra de Saïgon, a symbol of French colonial culture, its architecture reflecting European design sensibilities transposed onto Vietnamese soil. One remarkable chapter unfolded decades later, when the opera house ceased being just a theatre. Imagine this: where you now hear the murmur of tourists and the rustle of palm fronds, politicians debated laws and policies shaping a nation amid the same elegant arches that once framed operatic performances. Then, in 1975, the drama inside these walls reached a different climax. The theatre lay silent for a year after that, only reopening for performances in 1976. You might notice that the façade you see today was restored in 1998, including ornate details that had been partially removed back in 1943. This careful restoration revived the original grandeur, allowing the building to reclaim its role as a cultural landmark amid the modern bustle. As you turn away, the official heart of Saigon comes into view, carrying this legacy forward in its streets and squares.
Directions: Head west for roughly 250 meters, and you’ll arrive at Ho Chi Minh City Hall (People's Committee Building). Enjoy the stroll!

Stop 7: Ho Chi Minh City Hall (People's Committee Building)

Coordinates: 10.776543, 106.700910

Type/Specialty: Government Building

Specific Examples: Impressive façade, beautiful gardens, and historical significance.

Look for the ornate clock tower rising above the entrance, its Beaux-Arts details catching the daylight.

The building before you took 11 years to complete, beginning with its construction in 1898 and reaching completion in 1908. It was designed by Fernand Gardè, a French architect who brought the Beaux-Arts style to this city hall. The building officially opened in 1909, marking 50 years of French presence in Saigon, then part of French Indochina. The imposing scale and classical symmetry were meant to project stability and governance, standing in deliberate contrast to the chaotic growth of the city around it. After the communist conquest of Saigon in 1975, the building transitioned to house the government of the unified city now called Ho Chi Minh City. It remains the seat of the city’s People’s Committee and People’s Council, including departments like Transportation and Industry & Trade. The sound of officials moving through the halls today is the modern pulse of governance, an echo of the building’s layered civic function. Beyond here, the nearby Café Apartments show another side of the city’s ongoing story: the everyday lives and evolving traditions of its people.

Stop 9: Bạch Đằng Pier

Coordinates: 10.775263, 106.706880

Type/Specialty: Scenic Spot

Specific Examples: Stunning views of the Saigon River, boat tours, and riverside dining.

Orientation: Stand close to the river’s edge at Bạch Đằng Pier, where the sunlight shimmers on the Saigon River’s surface, framing the layered interplay of water and sky. Your gaze follows the line of boats moored here, their hulls creaking softly against the wooden berth, while the chatter of dockworkers blends with a faint oily tang from the river below.

Bạch Đằng Pier once served as a vital boat quay, where merchants and travelers gathered before modern bridges changed the city’s transit patterns. Later, it became a ferry terminal connecting directly across the river to Thủ Thiêm Pier. Today, the pier’s role has shifted again: it is now the departure point for tours heading toward the Củ Chi Tunnels. These tunnels, remnants of the Vietnam War, offered refuge and strategic advantage through their underground passageways. The tours departing from Bạch Đằng remind visitors that the present-day city, with its gently lapping waters and the laughter of passersby, rests atop layers of historical complexity. The boats lined up at the pier are not merely vessels; they are portals to understanding a significant aspect of Vietnam’s 20th-century history. Bạch Đằng Pier offers that same calm—a pause beside the flowing river before the final stop tonight, Thi Bar Saigon, welcomes you with the city’s lively evening.
