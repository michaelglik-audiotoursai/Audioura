In the mid-17th century, Louis I Phélypeaux de La Vrillière envisioned a grand ensemble of history paintings to adorn the Galerie dorée of the Hôtel de La Vrillière in Paris. Carlo Maratta, a prominent Italian Baroque painter renowned for his mastery of chiaroscuro, created a work that would become integral to this artistic vision. Between 1655 and 1657, Maratta crafted "L'Empereur Auguste ordonne de fermer les portes du temple de Janus," an oil on canvas that now confronts you with its powerful imagery. This painting holds a crucial place in Maratta's career, exemplifying his adeptness at intertwining narrative with artistic skill. The choice of chiaroscuro — the dramatic contrast between light and dark — not only highlights the figures but also underscores the themes of authority and transition. Much like the quieter hues of "La Plage de Berck," which you visited earlier, Maratta’s work uses color and composition to evoke historical gravitas. In a separate yet intertwined legacy, Maurice Masson, a dedicated art collector and friend to the museum, ensured that works such as Maratta’s resonate within the Musée des Beaux-Arts de Lille. As you depart this piece, consider how Maratta's collaboration with patrons laid the groundwork for the museum's diverse and significant compendium of history.

Stop 4: Jésus au jardin des Oliviers

Address: Musée des Beaux-Arts de Lille, 18 Rue de la République, 59000 Lille, France

Coordinates: 50.4, 1.585

Orientation: Stand a few feet back from the painting "Jésus au jardin des Oliviers" to fully appreciate its poignant narrative and striking composition. This vantage point allows you to take in the angelic figure and the chalice it presents to Christ, elements less common in traditional depictions.

In 1918, during World War I, El Greco's "Jésus au jardin des Oliviers" was moved from the Musée des Beaux-Arts de Lille due to the German occupation of northern France. To protect it from potential harm, the painting was evacuated and temporarily exhibited at the Musée des Beaux-Arts in Valenciennes. This careful relocation ensured its preservation, allowing it to return to Lille, where it continues to captivate visitors today. The painting, created between 1600 and 1610, features an angel presenting a chalice to Christ, a scene that is both a spiritual and emotional turning point. The use of bold contrasts in light and shadow highlights the tension between divine intervention and human vulnerability. El Greco, born Doménikos Theotokópoulos in Crete, was renowned for his expressive style, which is evident here in the dynamic brushstrokes and elongated figures that embody a sense of spiritual urgency. At this point in El Greco's career, he was well-established in Toledo, Spain, having developed a distinctive style characterized by vivid color and dramatic compositions. "Jésus au jardin des Oliviers" exemplifies his ability to convey profound emotion, making the viewer pause and reflect on the complexity of human experience and divine influence. Standing before this work, one is struck by the angel's presence, which adds a layer of hope amidst the agony depicted. This painting, much like the portrait of Marguerite Elisabeth de Largillierre you observed earlier, speaks to the intimate connection between the celestial and the earthly. As you gaze upon it, consider the resilience and dedication required to preserve such works through history's tumultuous times.

This tour highlights Maurice Masson's artistic legacy, showcasing how his dedication as a collector has enriched the museum's collection and preserved significant works like El Greco's "Jésus au jardin des Oliviers."
