"Paris Along the Seine" at the McMullen Museum of Art invites you to let your gaze drift over the canvas. This captures the spirit of Paris circa 1895, a city alive with energy and artistic promise.

In 1895, William James Glackens, an American artist living in Paris, painted "Sailing Boats, Paris." The Seine scene brims with small crafts, bustling with life and movement. Glackens, who had honed his observational skills working as a newspaper illustrator, used his fresh perspective to capture the pulsating life along the river. This painting marked a significant shift in his career, allowing his artistic voice to blossom in the thriving European art scene. The Seine was a continuous source of inspiration for him, with its bustling waterways offering endless motifs for his art. Stop 2: Saints and Sinners: Caravaggio and the Baroque Image

Address: 2100 Commonwealth Ave, Boston, MA 02135

Coordinates: 42.3482, -71.1464

Orientation: Position yourself near the center of the gallery to fully appreciate "The Taking of Christ," the mesmerizing focal point of "Saints and Sinners: Caravaggio and the Baroque Image." This vantage point allows you to experience the dynamic interplay of shadow and light Caravaggio is renowned for, while also letting you appreciate the dramatic tension captured in the composition.

The curators at the McMullen Museum of Art at Boston College demonstrated their vision by organizing the exhibition "Saints and Sinners: Caravaggio and the Baroque Image." They crafted a comprehensive catalog edited by Franco Mormando, an Italian studies scholar, who collaborated with the University to publish it. The exhibition marked a significant cultural moment as it featured "The Taking of Christ" by Michelangelo Merisi da Caravaggio, a masterful piece that made its North American debut at this venue. Caravaggio, living a tumultuous life often marred by legal troubles, channeled his inner turmoil into his work. In 1602, amidst this backdrop of unrest, he painted "The Taking of Christ," capturing the harrowing moment of Judas's betrayal. The scene's raw emotion is depicted vividly through oil on canvas, showcasing Caravaggio's characteristic use of chiaroscuro—an intense play of light and shadow—which serves as a metaphor for the moral conflicts within the scene. As you stand before this painting, you see the anguish on Christ’s face and the resolute betrayal in Judas’s glare, each figure's contours boldly delineated by stark shadows. This exhibition's inclusion of Caravaggio's work underscores how his dramatic reimagining of biblical stories laid a foundation for Baroque art, influencing generations of artists to follow. As modern interpretations of historic art continue to inspire contemporary creators, one wonders how Caravaggio's vision will echo through future artistic endeavors.

Stop 3: A retrospective of the work of Surrealist Roberto Matta

Address: 2100 Commonwealth Ave, Boston, MA 02135

Coordinates: 42.3482, -71.1464

Orientation: As you stand in front of A retrospective of the work of Surrealist Roberto Matta, let your eyes drift across the vivid canvases that capture his unique fusion of Surrealism and Abstract Expressionism. The placement of each work has been carefully considered to reveal the evolving techniques and insights that Matta explored throughout his career.

In 2004, the McMullen Museum of Art organized a comprehensive retrospective of Roberto Matta's work. This significant effort was led by university faculty from the romance languages, art history, and theology departments, who collaborated to bring a detailed collection of Matta's work to a wider audience. This collaboration resulted in a scholarly catalogue, spanning roughly 200 pages of full-color reproductions, offering a rich resource for both scholars and enthusiasts. Among the works on display is a striking oil on canvas from 1942. Matta created this piece using the Surrealist technique of automatism, where the natural flow of the brush reveals subconscious thoughts. Matta believed that this technique allowed him to depict a continuously transforming vision of time and space, inviting viewers into a reality beyond normal perception. It was around this time that Matta left Le Corbusier's atelier in 1937 and joined the Surrealist movement, marking a pivotal shift from architecture to painting. The canvas before you pulsates with vibrant energy, reflecting Matta's artistic journey and personal transformations. As you move through the exhibit, consider the groundbreaking techniques he developed, which resonate with the modern facilities of the museum. This journey through Matta's work provides a portal into the myriad ways in which artistic styles and techniques evolved during the 20th century, suggesting echoes of both the past and the future in each stroke.

Stop 4: Dura-Europos: Crossroads of Antiquity

Address: 2100 Commonwealth Ave, Boston, MA 02135

Coordinates: 42.3482, -71.1464

Orientation: To fully appreciate "Dura-Europos: Crossroads of Antiquity," pause in front of the central display. It features artifacts from a civilization that existed at the confluence of cultures, allowing you to see the depth and intricacy that this cross-cultural interchange produced long before Roberto Matta's work, which you viewed earlier, explored identity intersections.

In 256 CE, Sasanian forces besieged and sacked the multicultural border city of Dura-Europos, leading to its complete abandonment. Desert sands eventually buried the settlement, leaving its ruins undisturbed until Yale University launched major excavations at the site in 1928. For the 2011 exhibition Dura-Europos: Crossroads of Antiquity, the McMullen Museum of Art brought these excavated treasures together from the Yale University Art Gallery. This joint venture highlighted both institutions' shared commitment to exploring the complexities of ancient civilizations. Among the key figures was Yale archaeologist James Henry Breasted, who spearheaded early excavations following a notable discovery by Captain M.C. Murphy in 1920. Murphy's find—a stunning array of wall paintings—prompted Breasted to delve deeper into the site. This led to the unearthing of a synagogue decorated with biblical scenes, a discovery that challenged long-held assumptions about ancient Jewish art. The remnants preserved here are physical echoes of an intricate past where Hellenistic, Parthian, and Roman influences converged along the Euphrates River. As you reflect on the exhibition, consider how this ancient crossroads mirrors the breadth of the McMullen Museum's collection, known for its global diversity. The exhibition also prompts contemplation on universal themes—coexistence, transformation, and perseverance—echoing the moral narratives found in the "Saints and Sinners: Caravaggio and the Baroque Image" exhibition you previously explored.

Stop 5: Meal at the House of Simon the Pharisee

Address: 2100 Commonwealth Ave, Boston, MA 02135

Coordinates: 42.3482, -71.1464

Orientation: To truly appreciate "Meal at the House of Simon the Pharisee," stand directly before the canvas, allowing your eyes to trace the dramatic contrasts of light and shadow across the scene. From this vantage, you can witness the delicate interplay of expressions and gestures that bring out the painting's moral narrative.

Pierre Subleyras left France in 1728, seeking new opportunities and artistic inspiration in Italy. This decision was transformative for Subleyras's career, as he immersed himself in the rich artistic traditions of Rome. By 1737, he had completed the painting "Meal at the House of Simon the Pharisee," an oil on canvas that exemplified his mastery of the medium. One significant episode during Subleyras's time in Italy was when he received a commission from an influential religious institution in Rome, which enabled him to create works for prestigious locations. This affirmed his growing reputation in Italian art circles and provided him with the means to undertake large-scale projects. The painting is now held at the McMullen Museum of Art at Boston College, where it continues to captivate viewers with its historical and artistic significance. The work depicts a biblical scene from the Gospel of Luke, capturing a moment charged with moral introspection and human emotion. Just as you pondered the vivid chiaroscuro of Caravaggio's works in the Saints and Sinners exhibition you saw earlier, here too, Subleyras employs a similar play of light, inviting quiet contemplation of themes like forgiveness and redemption. Standing before this piece, one cannot help but notice the way Subleyras skillfully uses composition and light to invoke a sense of spiritual reflection, drawing the viewer into an intimate dialogue with the past.
