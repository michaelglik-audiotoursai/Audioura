Stop 2: Saints and Sinners: Caravaggio and the Baroque Image

Address: 210 Longwood Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3445, -71.0942

Orientation: Stand directly before Caravaggio's towering "The Taking of Christ," positioned under the museum's gentle lighting that echoes the dramatic chiaroscuro of the work itself. From this vantage, the intense emotional charge and dynamic composition of the piece are fully apparent.

In 1999, a collaborative effort between the McMullen Museum of Art, the Jesuit Community of Leeson Street, Dublin, and the National Gallery of Ireland led to a historic exhibition. They arranged for Caravaggio's "The Taking of Christ" to make its North American debut. This monumental event expanded the museum's reach, enhancing its reputation as an institution capable of curating internationally significant works. Caravaggio, born Michelangelo Merisi da Caravaggio in 1571, created this masterpiece in 1602. The painting captures the chilling moment of betrayal as Judas kisses Christ, setting the scene for his imminent arrest. This crucial work showcases Caravaggio's mastery of tenebrism—a technique that uses stark contrasts between light and dark to amplify the psychological intensity of the scene. In this piece, the interplay of shadow and illumination not only heightens the drama but also directs the viewer's gaze, drawing them into the narrative. During his tumultuous years in Rome, Caravaggio's life was as controversial as his paintings. After fleeing Milan due to a violent altercation, he arrived in Rome destitute but determined to pursue his art. There, Caravaggio honed his revolutionary approach to painting, a technique that would forever alter the course of Baroque art. His emotive use of light influenced not only his contemporaries but also later movements, shaping the trajectory of Western art. The disciples' anguish, Judas's treachery, and Christ's resignation are all encapsulated within a single, frozen moment. Here, the ancient crossroads of spirituality and human frailty converge, inviting modern interpretations of timeless themes. Through this masterwork, Caravaggio offers a profound exploration of human emotion, intensified by Baroque innovation, resonating with viewers across the ages.

Stop 3: Dura-Europos: Crossroads of Antiquity

Address: 210 Longwood Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3445, -71.0942

Orientation: As you stand before the "Dura-Europos: Crossroads of Antiquity" exhibition, take a moment to absorb the remarkable preservation of this ancient city, often called the "Pompeii of the Desert." From this vantage, the details of how this site lay dormant for centuries after its abandonment become almost palpable.

In 2011, the McMullen Museum of Art organized the exhibition "Dura-Europos: Crossroads of Antiquity," marking a significant moment for both the museum and the broader academic community. Lisa R. Brody and Gail L. Hoffman played pivotal roles in this initiative. Brody, known for her work on Hellenistic and Roman archaeological sites, co-edited the 413-page exhibition catalog with Hoffman, a scholar specializing in ancient culture studies. The exhibition changed the landscape of historical scholarship on Dura-Europos, providing an in-depth resource for scholars and enthusiasts alike. It highlighted not only the historical significance of Dura-Europos but also the McMullen Museum's growing influence in ancient studies. Hoffman was instrumental in emphasizing Dura-Europos as a site of cultural exchange, showcasing artifacts that spanned Hellenistic, Parthian, and Roman influences. Captain M.C. Murphy and James Henry Breasted were among the notable figures in its excavation. Murphy discovered the first significant wall paintings in the early 1920s, which drew Breasted, a renowned archaeologist, to further investigate the site. This sequence of events paved the way for Yale University and the French Academy to undertake more detailed excavations, revealing buildings like a synagogue adorned with biblical scenes. The cultural intersections at Dura-Europos evoke reflections on how such ancient dialogues inform contemporary artistic endeavors—much like the Caravaggio's interplay of light and narrative you saw earlier.

Stop 4: A retrospective of the work of Surrealist Roberto Matta

Address: 210 Longwood Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3445, -71.0942

Orientation: As you enter this transformative exhibit, stand amidst the paintings of Roberto Matta. From this vantage point, you can best appreciate the way his canvases blend with the environment, casting you into a realm where Surrealism meets Abstract Expressionism.

In 2004, the McMullen Museum of Art organized a retrospective of the work of Surrealist Roberto Matta, drawing together the expertise of university faculty from the romance languages, art history, and theology departments. This interdisciplinary collaboration brought a unique perspective to the exhibition, highlighting diverse aspects of Matta's work. In 1940, Roberto Matta held his first solo exhibition at the Julian Levy Gallery in New York, marking a significant moment in his artistic career as it introduced his work to the American audience. When Matta met Salvador Dalí during his European travels, it had a profound impact on his artistic direction, leading him to delve deeper into Surrealism and eventually influencing the Abstract Expressionist movement. With oil on canvas as his medium, Matta embraced the Surrealist technique of automatism, which allowed him to explore and portray a multi-dimensional reality that defied conventional perception. Matta’s early training in architecture informed his compositions, providing a sense of structure amid the chaos of his imaginative landscapes. As you observe these pieces, Matta's influence on 20th-century art movements becomes evident, creating a bridge between the Parisian surrealism and the burgeoning Abstract Expressionism of New York. This exhibition not only celebrates Matta’s legacy but also prompts us to consider how his innovations continue to shape our understanding of art in the modern era.

Stop 5: Pollock Matters

Address: 210 Longwood Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3445, -71.0942

Orientation: To truly grasp the depth of "Pollock Matters," step towards the center of the gallery. From this vantage, the nuanced interplay of paint in Jackson Pollock's drip works reveals layers that merge into a chaotic harmony, distinct from the carnal intensity evident in Caravaggio's imagery you admired earlier.

In 2002, an extraordinary find emerged when Alex Matter unearthed a trove of small experimental paintings in a Long Island storage facility previously used by his parents, Herbert and Mercedes Matter. Wrapped meticulously in brown paper, the labels attributed to Herbert Matter suggested these were the creations of Jackson Pollock from the years 1946 to 1949. As visitors roam through over 150 exhibited works, they find themselves navigating an artistic dialogue spanning paintings, drawings, and sculptures. Pollock, who revolutionized art with his drip technique beginning in 1947, experimented with freeing enamel paint across large canvases laid flat. This technique not only brought international fame but also broke away from traditional brushwork methods, influencing abstract expressionism in the mid-20th century. Standing here, one can almost feel the intellectual currents and personal histories that converge within these walls. These works testify not only to Pollock's artistic innovation but also to the collaborative and sometimes tumultuous interactions that fueled his creative process. Unlike the retrospective on Roberto Matta you visited earlier, which contemplatively dissected surreal dreams, "Pollock Matters" confronts the raw materiality and methodical chaos of Pollock's vision, leaving an indelible mark on the trajectory of modern art. In 2002, Alex Matter discovered a cache of small drip paintings in a storage locker, stored in a wrapper marked with Jackson Pollock's name. When curators Ellen Landau and Claude Cernuschi organized the 2007 exhibition "Pollock Matters" at the McMullen Museum of Art, accounts differed over the hoard, with some observers reporting more than twenty paintings and others counting about two dozen. Before the show, a forensic scientist reported that several of the works contained paints and materials unavailable until after Pollock died in 1956.
