Stop 1: Cathedral

Coordinates: 10.779722, 106.699167

Type/Specialty: Historical Landmark

Specific Examples: Stained glass windows, French Gothic architecture, and a serene courtyard.

Orientation: You are about to embark on a walking journey through Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam. As you traverse the vibrant streets of Saigon, you will uncover hidden narratives of resilience and transformation amidst the city's iconic landmarks. Your first stop is Cathedral. From the main street, head southeast on Lê Duẩn, then turn left onto Công xã Paris. The Cathedral will be on your right. You might notice the lush greenery surrounding the area. Standing on the pavement outside the Cathedral, your view opens onto a broad square framed by the red brick of the building’s twin spires. The square, known as Paris Commune Square, is a central roundabout where the bustling energy of Ho Chi Minh City pulses. Look up to catch how the sunlight hits the cathedral’s façade, its French Gothic arches contrasting sharply with the surrounding modern cityscape.

The Saigon Notre-Dame Cathedral was completed in 1880, its spires fashioned entirely from materials imported from France. This deliberate choice connects the building both physically and symbolically to its colonial origins, anchoring a piece of 19th-century European craftsmanship in the heart of Vietnam’s busiest city. Around the cathedral spreads Paris Commune Square, which marks the city’s kilometre zero—meaning all distances in Ho Chi Minh City are officially measured outward from this point. This designation establishes the cathedral as a true geographical and cultural center. Together, the cathedral and the square frame a space where the city’s urban rhythm gathers, and where tradition, commerce, and the legacy of past eras intersect. Nearby, the faint echo of church bells blends with the hum of city life, and the stone steps are worn smooth under countless footsteps. This spot embodies a unique layering: a European-style basilica standing firm amid the canals and rivers that define Ho Chi Minh City’s geography and the vibrant commerce that fuels Vietnam’s largest GDP contributor.

Directions: Enjoy the stroll! Continue to Post Office — it is about a minute's walk away.

Stop 2: Post Office

Coordinates: 10.780000, 106.700000

Type/Specialty: Historical Building

Specific Examples: Grand interior, vintage postal service artifacts, and beautifully painted ceiling.

Orientation: Stand just inside the main entrance facing the vaulted ceiling and the sweeping arched windows. The cool echo of footsteps on the tiled floor mixes with distant street sounds filtering through the open doorway.

The Saigon Central Post Office you see before you was constructed between 1886 and 1891, shaped by the vision of architect Auguste Henri Vildieu and his assistant Marie-Alfred Foulhoux. Their work replaced an earlier structure designed by Gustave Eiffel, which had been built from 1860 to 1863. The current building’s style is Eclecticism—a careful blend that marries Western architectural form with subtle Eastern decorative touches. This synthesis is visible in the soaring arched ceiling and the delicate ironwork that supports it, contrasting with the robust masonry framing the vast interior.

It has long been a hub in a city defined by its rivers and canals, with the nearby Saigon River shaping its geography and commerce. A significant chapter in the post office’s legacy began in 1864, when the first letters bearing the ‘stork’ stamp—the very first postage stamp issued in Vietnam—were sent from Saigon to destinations worldwide. This moment marked Saigon’s emergence onto the global postal network, connecting it to far-flung places through these small, printed emblems of communication.

Vildieu and Foulhoux’s design brought together the functionality needed for a modern postal hub with stylistic flourishes that acknowledged local aesthetics. Their fusion of Western principles and Eastern ornamentation created a space for the exchange of letters and parcels, and for the encounter of cultures etched into its columns and ceiling. The echoes of stamping machines and the rustle of dispatches linger faintly, reminders of a building that has long stood as a crossroads of communication in a city shaped by both history and relentless growth.

Directions: From the Post Office, walk east along Phố Bến Nghé, then turn left onto Nguyễn Văn Bình. Book Street will be ahead. You may catch the scent of fresh coffee wafting from nearby cafés. It is about a minute's walk away.

Orientation: Standing where Nguyen Van Binh Book Street begins, notice how this narrow, tree-lined lane stretches just about 100 meters ahead, flanked on both sides by rows of book stalls and cozy cafés.

You are on Book Street, a pedestrian lane dedicated to books and reading in District 1 of Ho Chi Minh City. This street is about 100 meters long and lined with numerous book stalls and shops. Here, you can find a wide variety of printed books covering genres like literature, politics, society, science, and culture. Book Street was created to promote a reading culture and provide a gathering place for book lovers. You will notice that the street hosts regular events such as book fairs, author signings, and cultural activities. In the evenings, locals and tourists come here to browse books and enjoy the lively atmosphere. Book Street has become a cultural landmark symbolizing the city's commitment to education and literacy.

Stop 4: Independence Palace

Coordinates: 10.777050, 106.695370

Type/Specialty: Historical Site

Specific Examples: Historical rooms, beautiful gardens, and the iconic tank.

Orientation: Standing at the wide entrance plaza of the Independence Palace, you face a modernist building with clean, geometric lines and a light façade. The smooth concrete and glass here catch the sunlight softly. Ahead, the heavy gates and the paved courtyard invite your gaze to the heart of this site.

The Independence Palace was designed by architect Ngô Viết Thụ and completed on 31 October 1966. Its Vietnamese modernist style marks a deliberate break from colonial-era architecture, embodying a new vision for the Republic of Vietnam’s leadership. Construction began on 1 July 1962, ordered by President Ngô Đình Diệm to replace an older palace that had been damaged by bombing from dissident pilots in the Republic of Vietnam Air Force. Diệm and his brother, Ngô Đình Nhu, were assassinated in a coup led by General Dương Văn Minh in November 1963, before the palace was completed. After fleeing the palace and taking refuge in a church in Cholon, Diệm and his brother were arrested and locked inside an Army vehicle.

The palace served as the home and office for the country’s president and government officials during a turbulent era. Inside, an exhibition presents photographs of the Buddhist monk Thich Quang Duc, who self-immolated in protest against Diệm’s regime’s persecution of Buddhists. This moment of solemn resistance is preserved here, connecting the palace not only to political power but also to the social and spiritual life that shaped Vietnam’s 20th century.

One of the defining moments at the Independence Palace occurred on 30 April 1975, when a tank from the Liberation Army of South Vietnam crashed through the palace gates. The tank, now preserved on the grounds, remains a tangible echo of that day’s thunderous arrival and the close of decades of conflict. The Independence Palace stands as a witness to rupture and resilience, its concrete walls holding the weight of multiple histories.

Directions: Head northwest for about 500 meters, and you'll arrive at the War Remnants Museum. Enjoy the stroll!

Stop 5: War Remnants Museum

Coordinates: 10.779500, 106.692000

Type/Specialty: Museum

Specific Examples: War exhibits, historical photographs, and military vehicles.

Orientation: Stand just outside the entrance on Võ Văn Tần Street, where the hum of scooters fades beneath the shade of leafy trees. Look for the stark, utilitarian façade that once housed the United States Information Agency.

The building before you became the War Remnants Museum in 1975, initially opening its doors as the Exhibition House for U.S. and Puppet Crimes. This name alone signals the museum’s founding purpose: a deliberate presentation of the consequences and realities of conflict as seen through the lens of those who endured it. The government of Ho Chi Minh City operates the museum here, a role it has maintained since that early post-war moment. Inside, you encounter photographs and artifacts that chronicle not only the Vietnam War but also reach back to the First Indochina War. The building itself, once a symbol of American information efforts, now holds a reversed narrative, housing the material evidence of conflict’s toll. Here, endurance and the harsh consequences of war are recorded without filter, inviting a somber reflection on the human cost beneath the political grandeur. As you prepare to leave, the bright, open streets of Saigon return gradually to your senses, and ahead, the Opera House stands ready to receive you—a reminder of cultural life continuing beyond the shadows preserved here.

Directions: Head east for about 1.2 kilometers, and you'll arrive at the stunning Opera House. Enjoy your stroll!

Stop 6: Opera House

Coordinates: 10.776390, 106.702780

Type/Specialty: Cultural Venue

Specific Examples: Stunning architecture, live performances, and elegant interior.

Orientation: Standing on Lam Sơn Square, your gaze meets the Opera House’s elegant façade facing Đồng Khởi Street. The cream-colored exterior, ornate with delicate French colonial flourishes, invites a closer look at this building that has quietly witnessed the city’s dramatic shifts.

The building before you opened its doors on January 1, 1900. Designed by the French architect Félix Olivier to seat guests, its architecture is a clear echo of French colonial style, sharing a lineage with the opera houses in Hanoi and Haiphong, but here it carries a distinct note. Its ornate balconies and heavy velvet curtains were first intended for performances and the arts.

Later, when the government shifted to a bicameral system, the opera house became home to both the Lower House (Hạ Nghị Viện) and the Senate (Thượng Nghị Viện). The clash of voices and the weight of decisions made here occurred in a building originally designed for art, not politics. The same balconies and curtains that once framed performances now witnessed the birth of laws and the turmoil of a nation trying to define itself. The Opera House stands as a reminder that art and politics have often shared the same stage in Saigon.

Directions: Head west for about 200 meters, and you’ll arrive at City Hall. Enjoy the stroll!

Stop 7: City Hall

Coordinates: 10.776470, 106.700970

Type/Specialty: Government Building

Specific Examples: French colonial architecture, beautiful gardens, and impressive façade.

The hum of city life flows around you—motorbikes buzzing, distant street vendors calling, and the occasional flutter of pigeons through the open square.

The building before you was completed in 1908, designed by Fernand Gardè, and opened in 1909 as a hotel named Hotel de Ville. Modeled after the grand municipal hall in Paris, but in a simpler style, it reflects the colonial desire to transplant French civic architecture onto Saigon’s streets. This hotel became a gathering place for colonial officials and visitors, serving as a tangible piece of France’s presence on this distant shore.

In the garden courtyard, a statue of Ho Chi Minh teaching a child was installed, symbolizing the connection between leadership and future generations of Vietnam. The building’s illuminated façade has become part of Saigon’s vibrant nightlife, a landmark that anchors the city’s rhythm. Looking around, you can hear the murmurs of daily life—the chatter of pedestrians and the rustle of leaves stirred by the tropical breeze—threads in the ongoing story of this place. Down Nguyễn Huệ Boulevard, the contrast continues with the unconventional residential mosaic at Café Apartments 42, where everyday community life unfolds in new ways.

Stop 9: Bạch Đằng Pier

Coordinates: 10.775240, 106.707002

Type/Specialty: Waterfront Area

Specific Examples: Boat rides, scenic views of the river, and sunset spots.

Orientation: Along the broad stretch where the water meets the promenade, locate the rows of moored boats and the pier extending into the flowing current.

Bạch Đằng Pier holds its place in Saigon’s history as a humble yet vital node in the city’s waterways. Originally, it functioned as a boat quay—a place where goods and people embarked and disembarked along the Saigon River. Over time, this practical role evolved as the pier became a ferry terminal, connecting to Thủ Thiêm Pier across the river and linking parts of Ho Chi Minh City by water. Today, the pier’s quiet presence belies its significant wartime connection: it serves as the departure point for tours to the Củ Chi Tunnels, a sprawling underground network northwest of the city. From here, visitors set out to explore that subterranean history, bridging the serene riverfront with the resilience forged below ground. The engines of tour boats start and stop at the pier, their metallic clatter punctuating the river’s calm. Nearby, beyond this tranquil waterfront, the nightlife of Thi Bar awaits—a reminder that the city’s layers of history and leisure unfold side by side. Bạch Đằng Pier stands as a quiet gateway: once a practical ferry point linking city districts, now a launchpad to a significant historical site beneath the earth. It anchors you in the present while connecting you to the layers of transport, war, and urban expansion that shaped modern Ho Chi Minh City.
