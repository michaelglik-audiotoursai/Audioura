Standing on the broad pavement of Công trường Công xã Paris, the square that frames the Saigon Notre-Dame Cathedral Basilica, the best vantage point is near the white granite statue of Mary. The cathedral’s red bricks, imported from Marseille, mark a tangible thread of French influence woven into the fabric of Ho Chi Minh City’s busiest district. The rough texture of the bricks contrasts with the polished green lawns and the smooth patina of the statue of Mary, which was installed here in 1959. The nearby flow of the Saigon River is a reminder that this urban pulse is shaped by waterways and commerce, while the cathedral remains a steady landmark amid change. The interplay of the French-made bricks, the monumental statue, and the kilometre zero marker creates a layered narrative of place, where tradition, history, and the ceaseless movement of Ho Chi Minh City meet.

Stop 2: Post Office

Coordinates: 10.780000, 106.700000

Type/Specialty: Historical Building

Specific Examples: Beautiful interior with wooden counters, vintage telephone booths, and a large mural of Vietnam.

Orientation: Stand near the main entrance, facing the grand façade of the building. The large mural of Saigon hangs opposite, a detailed map from the late 19th century that anchors the room in its historical moment.

This building blends Gothic and Renaissance forms with decorative touches inspired by Eastern motifs, reflecting a fusion of Western architectural style and local influences. The interplay of arches and ironwork overhead, combined with the warm grain of polished wood, creates a space that is both functional and ornamental, designed to serve as the city’s central hub for communication. The sound of footsteps on the tiled floor echoes faintly beneath the hum of present-day activity, while the scent of aged wood and ink lingers softly in the air.

Before this current building stood here, in 1864, something remarkable happened just beyond these very walls: the first letters bearing Vietnam’s inaugural postage stamp, known as the ‘stork’ stamp, were sent from Saigon to the wider world. This stamp marked Vietnam’s entry into the global postal network, transforming Saigon into a nexus of international exchange. The image of the stork symbolized communication and connection, setting a precedent for the post office’s role in linking distant lands through the written word.

Next door, Book Street awaits with its own stories, rooted in the written word that once passed through this very place. It was built in 1860 and completed in 1891.

Stop 4: Independence Palace

Coordinates: 10.777050, 106.695370

Type/Specialty: Historical Site

Specific Examples: Grand architecture, historical war rooms, and lush gardens.

Orientation: Stand just outside the palace’s main gates, where the broad driveway opens onto the building’s low, modernist silhouette. The wide steps lead into a structure that rises to, its clean lines and pale concrete walls offering a calm surface against the bustle of Ho Chi Minh City’s streets.

Designed by architect Ngô Viết Thụ, this place was completed on 31 October 1966 as a symbol of a new era, replacing the old palace that had been damaged by bombing. Its Vietnamese modernist style, unusual for the time, blends geometric form with function, creating wide-open rooms and corridors that allowed government leaders to move freely. This design reflected a desire for transparency and control during a turbulent period.

Before the palace was completed, its construction was ordered by President Ngô Đình Diệm. However, he was assassinated in a coup in November 1963, never seeing the building finished. He and his brother, Ngô Đình Nhu, were captured and executed by rebel forces shortly after the palace was attacked. The coup leaders opposed Diệm’s autocratic rule, which they believed neglected the fight against the Viet Cong insurgency. The unfinished palace thus carried the weight of a regime’s fall even before it opened its doors.

The palace witnessed one of its most defining moments on 30 April 1975. The tanks remain here as mute sentinels, their cold steel a stark contrast to the peaceful gardens that surround the palace today. The low hum of the city blends with the distant calls of birds, a quiet pulse that belies this site’s fierce past. Here, the story of power and conflict is etched in concrete and steel, a reminder that history moves in both public pages and private moments.

Stop 6: Opera House

Coordinates: 10.776390, 106.702780

Type/Specialty: Cultural Venue

Specific Examples: Impressive architecture, classical performances, and a vibrant cultural scene.

Orientation: Standing on Lam Sơn Square, you face the elegant façade of the Saigon Opera House, its delicate balconies and ornate arches catching the afternoon light. The street hums softly with the chatter of passersby and the distant rumble of motorbikes. The polished stone steps beneath you lead up to a building whose presence commands attention without shouting—its charm lies in the details, from the sculpted ironwork to the softly arched windows.

The municipal theater was completed on January 1, 1900, and designed by the French architect Félix Olivier in the flamboyant style popular in Europe at the time. With an original capacity of venue, it offered a surprisingly intimate setting for performances and gatherings. The Opera House is one of a trio of French-built theaters in Vietnam, alongside those in Hanoi and Haiphong, each serving as a relic of colonial ambitions rendered in stone and steel.

What distinguishes this Opera House is its history beyond the arts. After the Assembly split into two chambers, the building became the seat for both the Lower House, known locally as Hạ Nghị Viện, and the Senate, or Thượng Nghị Viện. Politicians once debated here, their voices filling the same balconies that had framed opera singers. This shift transformed the building from a stage for performances to a stage for national decisions during a turbulent era.

The air still carries a faint scent of old wood and varnish, reminders of its theatrical past and careful restorations. Renovations in 1995 aimed to restore its original grandeur and improve acoustics, allowing the Opera House to host modern performances while preserving its historical aura.

Stop 7: City Hall

Coordinates: 10.776470, 106.700970

Type/Specialty: Government Building

Specific Examples: Stunning French colonial architecture, illuminated at night, and a lovely plaza.

From here, the full scale of City Hall spreads before you—its broad windows and whitewashed walls catching the sunlight. The wide plaza in front invites a moment’s pause, where the sounds of city life mix with the steady hum of government activity inside.

Completed in 1908 and officially opened in 1909, the building was designed by Fernand Gardè in the Beaux-Arts style, a hallmark of French colonial architecture in Saigon. In 1945, City Hall was the setting for a pivotal moment in Vietnam’s history, when Ho Chi Minh stood here and declared Vietnam’s independence from French colonial rule. The echoes of that announcement still resonate in the walls, linking this place to the hopes and struggles of millions. Three decades later, in 1975, the building was again at the heart of history when its capture marked a turning point that reshaped the nation’s political landscape.

Today, City Hall blends its colonial past with its present purpose. Here, the layers of history and governance converge, standing firm amid Saigon’s ever-moving pulse.

The pier extends modestly into the water, edged by simple railings and a smooth concrete surface patterned faintly by water stains and the sun’s shifting angles. Nearby, the gentle lapping of river waves contrasts with distant city murmurs—a calm break from Saigon’s bustle.

This stretch of riverfront, known as Bạch Đằng Pier, has long served as a practical hub for water travel and transport. Historically, it was a boat quay—a place where vessels moored to load and unload passengers and cargo, stitching together the rhythms of river traffic that have shaped this city. This ongoing connection kept the river alive with movement, as trading and commuting traffic threaded through Saigon’s growing urban fabric.

Today, the pier remains a gateway for journeys with deeper meaning. From here, tours depart to the Củ Chi Tunnels, a vast network dug by Vietnamese soldiers during the Vietnam War. Although the tunnels themselves are hidden beneath fields some distance from the city, this pier serves as their urban gateway—where visitors begin their passage from the river’s calm surface into the underground labyrinth that shaped military tactics and survival during the conflict.

Nearby, the Saigon River’s steady murmur blends with city sounds—a bird’s call, the distant hum of engines—offering a natural counterpoint to the vibrant streets left behind at the Café Apartments 42. Bạch Đằng Pier stands as a subtle reminder that this city’s evolution unfolds along its waterways, where past and present converge quietly on the river’s edge.
