You are about to explore The National Gallery in London, home to renowned artworks including The Supper at Emmaus, The Toilet of Venus ('The Rokeby Venus'), and The Hay Wain. Your first stop is The Supper at Emmaus. As you enter the room at The National Gallery housing "The Supper at Emmaus," position yourself directly in front of this commanding canvas. From this vantage point, the dramatic interplay of light and shadow becomes apparent, drawing you into the profound moment of recognition.

Ciriaco Mattei, a Roman nobleman, commissioned Caravaggio to paint "The Supper at Emmaus" in 1601. Mattei paid 150 scudi for this work, which was a significant investment at the time. Because of this commission, Caravaggio completed the painting that year, and it eventually found its way to London, where it is now held at The National Gallery. Michelangelo Merisi da Caravaggio masterfully uses oil on canvas to depict a pivotal Gospel scene. The dramatic illumination highlights their astonishment and the spiritual revelation they experience. This period was crucial for Caravaggio; he was at the height of his fame, recognized for his groundbreaking use of chiaroscuro, a technique that enhances the emotional intensity of his subjects. The painting’s figures are rendered with lifelike precision, showcasing Caravaggio’s commitment to realism. Observing the disciples' expressions, one can sense the awe and realization dawning upon them, as if they, and we, are part of this divine moment. In every fold of the clothing and every nuanced shadow, Caravaggio invites us into a dialogue about faith and recognition. This work not only cemented his reputation as a leading figure of the Baroque period but also influenced countless artists who followed, ensuring his legacy within the canon of Western art.

Stop 2: The Toilet of Venus ('The Rokeby Venus')



Stand directly in front of The Toilet of Venus ('The Rokeby Venus'), painted by Diego Velázquez between 1647 and 1651, to fully appreciate the unique perspective of Venus's face reflected in the mirror. This angle reveals the masterful play of light and shadow that defines the work.

Velázquez, the leading artist of the Spanish Golden Age, painted this work during a time when he was deeply engaged with the nuances of realism and the human form. Her figure reclines languidly, with her back turned to the viewer, a pose that invites both admiration and contemplation. In this composition, Velázquez employs a mirror held by Cupid, the god of desire and attraction, not merely as a reflection of her visage, but as a sophisticated exploration of perception and beauty. To stand before this painting is to engage with Velázquez’s exploration of beauty, perception, and the passage of time—echoing the themes of self-portraiture seen in works like the Self Portrait in a Straw Hat, which you have already encountered on your journey.
