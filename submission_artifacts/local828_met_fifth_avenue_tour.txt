Stop 1: Washington Crossing the Delaware

Coordinates: 40.7794, -73.9626

The museum is open daily except Wednesday, 10:00 to 5:00. Admission is 30 dollars for adults; children 12 and under go free.

Orientation: Your first stop is Washington Crossing the Delaware. Standing directly across from Emanuel Leutze’s Washington Crossing the Delaware, you see the full sweep of the frozen river and the cluster of figures balancing on the precarious boat. From this vantage, the sharp contrasts of light and shadow reveal the determination etched on each face, a detail that might be lost at a distance.

Washington Crossing the Delaware depicts George Washington’s daring decision to lead a surprise attack by crossing the icy Delaware River on Christmas night, 1776. The composition centers Washington at the bow of the boat, his figure sharply outlined against a dark sky, embodying steadfast leadership. The figures are caught in a tense balance between treacherous ice flows and trusting each other, the frozen river itself a symbol of the harsh risks endured. The scene symbolizes courage against oppression. This painting makes visible the human cost of revolutionary resolve—the cold, danger, and uncertainty faced by Washington and his men. It captures a moment that foreshadowed the larger success of the Continental Army, formed in 1775 to oppose British rule, reminding viewers of the power of decisive action under extreme conditions. Emanuel Leutze’s work thus immortalizes both an individual and a pivotal event whose consequence was a turning point in the fight for American independence. German-American artist Emanuel Leutze painted this work in Düsseldorf, Germany. After his studio suffered a fire that damaged the original painting, Leutze set to work recreating it.

Directions: Continue through The Metropolitan Museum of Art — next is The Death of Socrates (Jacques-Louis David).

Stop 2: The Death of Socrates

Coordinates: 40.7794, -73.9626

Orientation: Stand at a moderate distance facing the canvas, allowing your eyes to take in the full grouping of figures and the architectural setting. From this vantage, the precise arrangement of gestures and the interplay of light and shadow across the figures’ forms become legible, revealing the tension and solemnity embedded in the composition.

In 1787, Jacques-Louis David completed this depiction of the execution of Socrates. Sentenced to die by drinking hemlock, the philosopher chose not to flee when given the opportunity. Because it is held at the Met—the largest art museum in the Americas and the fourth-largest museum in the world by floor area—this painting is now accessible to a wide public and preserved within one of the largest museums in the United States. The composition is marked by sharp contours and a limited palette dominated by earthy tones and the crisp whites of Socrates’ robe. His students display varied reactions—grief, despair, and resignation—each captured in sculptural poses that contrast with Socrates’ composed demeanor. David’s choice of oil on canvas allowed him to achieve a fine gradation of light and shadow, emphasizing the physical and emotional gravity of the scene. Painted several years before the French Revolution, this work reflects David’s engagement with themes of civic virtue and sacrifice, which would come to dominate his later political works. Standing before the painting, the viewer encounters the tension between death and moral steadfastness. Socrates’ calm acceptance contrasts with the anguish of his disciples, drawing attention to the cost of adhering to principle. This moment, frozen in oil, embodies an intellectual act of defiance that transcends time. Colloquially known simply as the Met among art circles, the museum situates this painting within a broader European tradition of historical narrative painting. Jacques-Louis David painted this scene during a critical juncture in his career, when he was exploring classical themes as vehicles for contemporary political ideas. The painting demonstrates his commitment to neoclassical ideals, which sought to revive the moral clarity of ancient art as a model for modern society. A second episode underscores the painting’s lasting influence: after its completion, David’s meticulous portrayal of Socrates’ death became a reference point for artists and intellectuals who debated the duties of the citizen and the artist’s role in political life. The painting’s composition and ideological content contributed to the emergence of history painting as a vehicle for public education, a mission that aligns with the Met’s dedication to works that challenge and illuminate public understanding.

Directions: Next: The Harvesters.

Stop 3: The Harvesters

Coordinates: 40.7794, -73.9626

Orientation: Stand a few steps back from The Harvesters to take in the entire wide wooden panel at once.

In 1565, the Antwerp merchant banker Nicolaes Jonghelinck commissioned Pieter Bruegel the Elder to produce a six-part painted cycle illustrating the seasons of the year. Bruegel executed the panels in oil on wood, creating this work to represent the late summer harvest. While one of the original works from the cycle was eventually lost, five paintings from the group survived. Instead, Bruegel chose to place peasants harvesting grain at the center, making the landscape itself the subject and shifting the focus to everyday life rather than grand narratives. The work itself is oil on wood, measuring 119 by 162 centimeters—a sizable panel that allows Bruegel to pack in multiple layers of action. His choice of oil on wood as a medium lets him achieve subtle textures—the golden wheat gleams, the leafy shadows cool, and the figures’ varied activities feel distinctly tactile. Unlike the grand stoicism in the Death of Socrates that you saw earlier, here life moves quietly, without heroic poses or dramatic gestures. The focus on peasants and their work brings an unvarnished humanity to the scene. The painting registers the relentlessness of labor ahead of the communal moments of pause, weaving together endurance and fleeting respite. It reflects a world so different from Washington Crossing the Delaware but no less real—a reminder that history unfolds not just in political upheaval, but in everyday toil. A modern reflection on identity and culture can be seen as carried forward from Bruegel’s lens on the ordinary.

Directions: Proceed to Camouflage Self-Portrait.

Stop 4: Camouflage Self-Portrait

Coordinates: 40.7794, -73.9626

Orientation: Stand slightly to the left of Camouflage Self-Portrait to catch the way its layered patterns interact with the light, revealing the subtle contours of the artist’s face beneath the camouflage motif. From here, the tension between concealment and exposure becomes legible, a visual puzzle that demands careful inspection beyond the initial glance.

Camouflage Self-Portrait, created by Andy Warhol in 1986, is a silkscreen on canvas—a technique Warhol favored for its ability to replicate images with mechanical precision while allowing for variations in color and texture. In this work, Warhol overlays his own image with a camouflage pattern, a design originally developed for military fabric meant to conceal. Here, the pattern both obscures and accentuates his visage, suggesting layers of self-perception that are simultaneously hidden and revealed. The face emerges from a dense matrix of shapes and colors, echoing the complexity of identity in a media-saturated culture.

Warhol created this self-portrait during the last decade of his life, a period when he was revisiting themes of fame, mortality, and his own public persona. The choice of camouflage reflects a moment when he explored the tension between visibility and anonymity, perhaps responding to his experience as a cultural icon under increasing scrutiny. Standing before the painting, one confronts the uneasy coexistence of presence and absence—the way we try to mask parts of ourselves yet cannot escape visibility.

The Metropolitan Museum of Art received Camouflage Self-Portrait through the Rogers Fund, which enriched its contemporary art holdings. The painting was later included in a museum exhibition that brought it into dialogue with other pieces from the same period, allowing viewers to situate it within the broader trajectory of late 20th-century art. The curatorial decision to include this painting in a recent exhibition shaped how visitors understand Warhol’s late work, framing it within conversations about self-image and cultural camouflage.

Directions: From here, make your way to The Great Wave off Kanagawa.

Stop 5: The Great Wave off Kanagawa

Coordinates: 40.7794, -73.9626

Orientation: Stand close and level with The Great Wave of Kanagawa to absorb the scale and the layering of the ink and color. From this vantage, the carved outlines and the subtle gradations of Prussian blue become apparent, revealing the precise artistry of woodblock printing that might be lost from a distance.

Katsushika Hokusai created The Great Wave of Kanagawa around 1830-1833 during the Edo period. He carved and printed this image using the Japanese woodblock printing technique. Because of this process, multiple copies were made and some have been preserved in prominent museums like The Metropolitan Museum of Art. This print is one sheet from a series titled Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji, designed to capture the mountain’s presence from varying perspectives. Here, the wave dominates the foreground, its towering crest curling over three small boats. The wave’s claw-like fingers seem both threatening and sculptural, crafted through careful carving of woodblocks that allowed Hokusai to replicate the image multiple times in shades of blue derived from imported Prussian pigment. This technique, ukiyo-e, was a popular form of mass-produced art in early 19th-century Japan, but Hokusai elevated it through compositional innovation: the juxtaposition of the colossal wave against the distant, serene Mount Fuji creates a tension between nature’s force and permanence. At this time, Hokusai was in his seventies, a veteran artist whose career had spanned decades of experimentation with painting and printmaking. The Great Wave marks a turning point where he embraced color woodblock prints to reach a broader audience. The sharp contrasts and dynamic rhythm of the wave’s form echo the same human tensions observed earlier on this tour in the Camouflage Self-Portrait, where surface and identity also play with concealment and revelation. This print, measuring roughly 10 by 15 inches, is a delicate yet resilient object—thin washi paper pressed with ink from carved cherry woodblocks. Its survival through nearly two centuries testifies to the durability of traditional techniques in the face of modernization.

In 2000, its permanent collection had over two million works; it currently lists a total of 1.5 million works.
