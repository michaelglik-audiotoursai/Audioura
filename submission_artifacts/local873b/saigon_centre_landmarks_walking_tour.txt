From Nguyễn Huệ Walking Street, head east on Nguyễn Huệ, then turn left onto Lê Lợi. Continue straight until you reach Công xã Paris, where the cathedral is located. The Notre-Dame Cathedral Basilica of Saigon stands on a site first marked by a wooden church erected in 1863 along the Charner Canal. This original structure was later replaced by the present cathedral, completed in 1880. Designed by the French architect Jules Bourard, the cathedral was built between 1877 and 1880. Its two bell towers rise 58 meters above the city, forming a steadfast presence in the skyline. The cathedral’s exterior is clad in red bricks imported from Marseille, which have withstood decades of tropical weather and still display their warm tones—a subtle reminder of the materials that traveled far to build this landmark.

In 1903, the colonial government placed a bronze statue in the square outside the cathedral, depicting Pierre Pigneau de Béhaine, a French Catholic priest, alongside the juvenile Prince Nguyễn Phúc Cảnh. This statue commemorated Pigneau de Béhaine’s role in Vietnamese history and served as a visual anchor for the square’s former name, Place Pigneau de Béhaine. The presence of these figures in the garden reflects the intertwined histories of French colonialism and local leadership, visible to passersby each day.

Today, the cathedral remains a central gathering place during Catholic celebrations in Ho Chi Minh City. At Christmas, its bells ring out over the square, joining the voices of locals and expatriates who gather for mass and festivities.

Stop 2: Saigon Central Post Office

Coordinates: 10.779852, 106.699844

Type/Specialty: Historic Post Office

Specific Examples: Intricate interior design, vintage postal services, iconic clock tower.

The building before you was constructed between 1886 and 1891, designed by architect Auguste Henri Vildieu with his assistant. Before this, an earlier structure stood here from 1860 to 1863, designed by Gustave Eiffel, whose name is more commonly linked to Paris’s iron tower but also touched this city’s architecture. In 1864, just a few blocks from here, the first letters bearing Vietnam’s initial ‘stork’ stamp were dispatched from Saigon to destinations worldwide. The post office you see today was built to accommodate that growing web of correspondence and remains a witness to that transformation.

Inside, the echoes of clattering typewriters and ringing telephone bells seem almost tangible amid the building’s vaulted ceilings and curved iron beams. Original wooden phone booths remain, their worn surfaces a tactile memory of a time when communication meant waiting and patience, not instant messages. This is a place where the city’s pulse once beat through the flow of letters and parcels, linking Saigon to a world far beyond its streets. Both buildings frame the square as the city’s historic core, a crossroads of faith, empire, and daily life. Here, the weight of colonial ambitions and the rhythms of ordinary Saigonese existence meet beneath this grand roof. The aroma of aged wood and ink mingles faintly with the city’s modern bustle outside.

Stop 5: War Remnants Museum

Coordinates: 10.779511, 106.692090

Type/Specialty: Museum

Specific Examples: Powerful war exhibits, historical photographs, military vehicles.

The air holds a faint trace of aged metal and concrete, mingling with the distant hum of traffic from Ho Chi Minh City’s bustling streets.

The museum’s collection brings the violence and resilience of those times into immediate focus, not through abstract history but through visceral evidence. One story emerges from its origins: the museum began as the “Exhibition House for Crimes of War and Aggression,” its name signaling a government intent to document the devastation inflicted by foreign powers. The decision to open this institution in 1975, directly following the war’s end, ensured that these materials were displayed with immediacy and authority, anchoring public memory in undeniable proof rather than contested narratives. The museum’s presence in this financial and cultural hub of Vietnam, which contributes roughly a quarter of the nation’s GDP, underscores how the past’s imprint endures amid rapid modernization. Another episode traces the museum’s role in international awareness. The museum is not merely a place of remembrance but a center where the material consequences of war are catalogued, ensuring that the city’s story includes the weight of those years alongside its present-day vitality. The low murmur of footsteps against polished floors and the faint metallic echo of tanks just beyond the walls remind visitors that these remnants are not distant relics but tangible links to a history that shaped this city’s identity. Leaving this place, the pulse of a new era in Ho Chi Minh City continues beyond its gates, framed by the relentless flow of the Saigon River and the city's ceaseless growth.

Stop 6: Saigon Opera House

Coordinates: 10.776613, 106.703170

Type/Specialty: Cultural Venue

Specific Examples: Elegant performances, stunning façade, historical architecture.

Orientation: Standing on Lam Sơn Square, your gaze meets the ornate façade that faces Đồng Khởi Street, where the Saigon Opera House announces itself with a flourish of French colonial design. The carved arches and balustrades frame the entrance, and nearby the hum of city life blends with the occasional strain of music drifting from inside.

The Saigon Opera House opened its doors on January 1, 1900, after construction began in 1898. It was designed by the French architect Félix Olivier, with oversight from Eugène Ferret and Ernest Guichard. Originally, it served as an opera house seating around 500 people, providing a place where French colonial society gathered for entertainment. The building’s flamboyant architecture, with its intricate stonework and curved lines, reflects the style popular in that era and suggests a delicate theatricality.

However, the building was not always dedicated to music and dance. From 1955 until 1975, the Saigon Opera House became the parliament building of the Republic of Vietnam. During those twenty years, the voices inside shifted from performers to politicians debating the future of a divided nation. Outside, the square where you stand figured in a famous literary moment: Graham Greene’s novel The Quiet American includes a bombing scene set right here. The solidity of its stone and the soft glow of its stage lights connect the colonial past with the present, bridging history in this vibrant city.

Stop 7: Ho Chi Minh City Hall (People's Committee Building)

Coordinates: 10.776543, 106.700910

Type/Specialty: Government Building

Specific Examples: Impressive façade, beautiful gardens, historical significance.

Orientation: The building’s grand Beaux-Arts style immediately draws the eye — from the intricately carved pilasters to the tall arched windows.

The Ho Chi Minh City Hall was designed by Fernand Gardè, with construction beginning in 1898 and completed in 1908. Officially inaugurated in 1909 as the Hôtel de ville, or municipal hall, it served the colonial administration and became a direct symbol of French governance. Its fusion of French colonial and Renaissance styles is evident in the symmetrical façade, sculpted cornices, and the elegant clock tower rising 30 meters above the street. The building’s scale and style were intended to communicate authority and order during a period marked by French colonial influence in Vietnam’s urban development, as the city was reshaped with European architectural forms.

The decade-long construction reflected the challenges of adapting European techniques to Saigon’s tropical climate and local materials. Fernand Gardè’s design balanced grandeur with practicality, resulting in structural choices that resisted humidity and heat while maintaining ornate Beaux-Arts elegance. This allowed the city hall to endure the tropical environment and remain a centerpiece of civic administration through successive political changes. Today, the grand façade of Ho Chi Minh City Hall stands in contrast to the everyday life bustling just outside, where locals and visitors weave through the shaded gardens and wide boulevard.

Stop 9: Bạch Đằng Pier

Coordinates: 10.775263, 106.706880

Type/Specialty: Riverfront Area

Specific Examples: Scenic boat rides, riverside dining, beautiful sunset views.

Orientation: From the corner of 42 Nguyễn Huệ where the Café Apartments stood, continue east toward the Saigon River. You will soon reach a broad promenade stretching along Ton Duc Thang street. Look for the docks extending into the current, and the cluster of boats moored alongside, a reminder of this place’s function as Saigon’s river gateway.

Bạch Đằng Pier was once a vital node in the city’s waterborne transit system, serving as a connector long before urban road traffic became dominant. It later evolved into a ferry terminal linking to Thủ Thiêm Pier across the river, providing an essential route for commuters and goods moving between Saigon’s District 1 and the growing urban wards to the east. This ferry service gave the pier practical importance within Ho Chi Minh City’s transport network, even as its surroundings changed.

Today, the pier is defined by its role as both a leisure destination and a transit junction. The broad walkways of the riverfront park offer a tactile contrast to nearby streets, with smooth stone underfoot and the occasional cool spray from passing boats. The sounds of murmuring water and boat engines create a lively atmosphere, making the daily rhythm of river life visible. Unlike the vertical density of the Café Apartments, here the space opens horizontally, framing a skyline that blends colonial-era silhouettes with new glass towers.

Bạch Đằng Pier now serves as a key departure point for speedboat tours, including trips to the Cu Chi Tunnels, linking the riverside urban core with more distant historical sites. Its current use as a launchpad for these tours continues its legacy as a transit hub while adding new layers of activity. The pier’s identity is revealed in its physical presence—stone and water, legacy ferry routes, and the flow of tourist traffic—standing as a threshold where Saigon’s transformation from a colonial river quay to a dynamic metropolis can be felt beneath your feet.
