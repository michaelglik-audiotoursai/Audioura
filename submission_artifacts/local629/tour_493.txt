Stop 1: Portrait de Madame Cézanne



Follow the thematic thread of The Influence of Monet's Water Garden as you delve into the profound influence of nature on the works of these renowned artists. Your first stop is Portrait de Madame Cézanne. Standing before "Portrait de Madame Cézanne" at the Musée de l'Orangerie, direct your attention to the subtle transitions of color and form — an insight into the intimate and contemplative nature of the piece that might elude a more cursory glance.

Paul Cézanne, the renowned French Post-Impressionist painter known for bridging artistic styles, created "Portrait de Madame Cézanne" between 1885 and 1886. This oil on canvas measures 81 by 65 centimeters and depicts his wife, Marie-Hortense Fiquet, with intentional restraint. Cézanne met Fiquet in Paris, where she worked as an artist's model and painter, and their marriage had a significant impact on his artistic work. During their marriage, Cézanne painted a series of portraits of Fiquet, exploring emotions and character depth beyond mere external appearance.

In this portrait, Cézanne's signature compositional choices are evident, with some parts deliberately left unfinished, especially along the lower sections and edges of Fiquet's clothing. The emotional impact of the artwork lies in the contrast between these unfinished areas and the more defined depiction of Hortense's face. Cézanne's decision to leave parts incomplete aimed to capture transient emotions and internal life rather than striving for pictorial perfection.

Some historians suggest a connection between Cézanne's approach and Claude Monet's dedication to portraying essence and impermanence in works like "Nymphéas" at the Orangerie, highlighting a shared commitment to revealing deeper truths. As you reflect on the serene yet introspective figure of Hortense, consider how Cézanne immortalizes a nuanced moment of intimacy, akin to Monet's tranquil water gardens in the adjacent galleries.

Stop 2: Antonia



Stand before Francisco de Goya's "Antonia," and take a step back. This positioning allows you to fully appreciate the intricate details of Antonia Zárate’s attire, particularly the delicately painted black lace mantilla that frames her face, defining the composition with its stark contrast against her black dress.

Francisco de Goya crafted this oil on canvas around 1805, capturing Doña Antonia Zárate, a renowned Spanish actress of her time. Goya was at a pivotal point in his career when he created this portrait, exploring new depths in the representation of human emotion and societal roles. The choice to depict Zárate in an empire dress was a subtle nod to the changing fashion of the period, echoing broader cultural shifts amidst the Napoleonic influences in Spain. In Goya's portrayal, the contrast between the deep black of her dress and the delicate intricacies of her mantilla conveys a sense of solemnity and elegance.

This act transitioned the artwork from private ownership into the public domain, allowing a wider audience to engage with its historical and artistic significance. The painting now resides within the esteemed walls of the Musée de l'Orangerie, aligning with the vision Claude Monet had for the venue—a sanctuary for profound, transformative art. Like Monet's water lilies, Goya's portrait of Antonia Zárate invites reflection on the interplay of light and shadow, foreground and background, and the human narratives captured within. Here, the dialogue between Goya’s work and Monet’s legacy contributes to a layered artistic landscape, one that continues to inspire wonder and introspection.

Stop 3: Red Boats at Argenteuil



As you stand before "Red Boats at Argenteuil," position yourself to capture the gentle interplay of light and color that Monet so skillfully harnessed on this oil on canvas. Notice the red boats anchored tranquilly, inviting you into the serene autumn setting.

In 1963, "Red Boats at Argenteuil" entered the French national collections, marking a pivotal moment for the painting. Monet was residing in Argenteuil at the time, a period when he was deeply engrossed in understanding how light and atmosphere could transform a scene. He often worked outdoors, sometimes from a boat of his own, which inspired a series of paintings that explored the beauty of the Argenteuil Basin. Standing before this painting, one can almost sense the gentle lapping of the water against the hulls of the boats. The contrasting colors and dynamic light reflect Monet's ability to convey both stillness and movement, a skill that would later be fully realized in his water garden paintings at Giverny. The Orangerie, originally built to store citrus trees, now holds this piece as part of a broader collection that celebrates the essence of Monet's evolving style, bridging the vibrant energy of Argenteuil with the tranquil reflections of his later water lily compositions. This journey from energetic landscapes to serene contemplations of water not only transformed Monet's own work but also left an indelible legacy that continues to influence artists and art lovers alike.

This tour highlights the influence of Monet's water garden, illustrating how his exploration of light and atmosphere resonates through the works of other artists. This connection invites us to reflect on the transformative power of art and its ability to capture the essence of human experience and nature. That's 3 stops in all.
