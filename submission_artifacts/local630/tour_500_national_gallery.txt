Stop 1: The Toilet of Venus ('The Rokeby Venus')

Address:

Coordinates: 51.5081, -0.128

To truly appreciate the sensual allure of Diego Velázquez's "The Toilet of Venus ('The Rokeby Venus')," stand directly in front of the painting and let the soft interplay of light and shadow draw you into the intimate world Velázquez created. This vantage point reveals the delicate contrast between the pale skin of Venus and the rich, dark background, highlighting the artist's masterful use of oil on canvas. The sensuous curves of Venus's body are rendered with a gentle, almost tangible quality. In this timeless moment, Venus gazes into a mirror held by the winged figure of Cupid, her reflection partially obscured, offering an enigmatic glimpse into her world.

"The Rokeby Venus" is the only surviving nude by Velázquez, making it a unique specimen in his body of work. Velázquez likely painted this piece during or just before his second visit to Italy from 1648 to 1652, a period that further refined his artistic style. His time in Italy exposed him to the works of renowned Italian masters, influencing his use of color and composition. The depiction of a nude Venus was daring for a Spanish artist, reflecting Velázquez's evolving approach to capturing human beauty with both realism and grace. Its journey from Spain to England is part of its storied past, creating a bridge between artistic cultures and eras.

Since its arrival, "The Rokeby Venus" has drawn countless visitors to the National Gallery, establishing itself as a central piece in the museum's collection.

Millais, a pivotal figure in chose to delve into the tragic beauty of Shakespeare's Ophelia, capturing the moment just before her untimely demise. During the creation of "Ophelia," Millais insisted on painting the natural setting en plein air, a technique that allowed him to achieve the luminous quality for which the work is celebrated. The artist employed meticulous brushwork in oil on canvas to convey the lushness of the English countryside juxtaposed against the ethereal stillness of Ophelia's floating form. This approach not only heightened the painting's emotional impact but also influenced subsequent generations of artists to explore naturalistic settings. The narrative embodied in Millais’ "Ophelia" is one of melancholy, reminiscent of the emotional complexity found in works like Vigée Le Brun’s Self Portrait in a Straw Hat, which you have already encountered. The connection between Vigée Le Brun's calculated portrayal of emotion and Millais's haunting depiction of Ophelia invites viewers to reflect on how art captures and shapes our understanding of emotional depth. This artwork prompts contemplation on the ongoing dialogue between past artistry and contemporary interpretations. Emotional power in art has the potential to influence our perceptions of reality. John Everett Millais chose to depict the drowning of Shakespeare’s tragic character from Hamlet in two separate stages. Believing the natural setting held equal importance to the figure, he traveled outdoors to the Hogsmill River at Ewell in Surrey to paint the landscape first. Only after completing the riverbank did he return indoors to his London studio to add the figure of Ophelia.

Stop 3: The Supper at Emmaus

Address:

Coordinates: 51.5081, -0.128

Orientation: As you stand before "The Supper at Emmaus" by Michelangelo Merisi da Caravaggio, step closer and observe how the light interacts with the figures in the painting. The dramatic use of chiaroscuro — the contrast of light and shadow — draws your focus to the expressive gestures of the characters in this biblical narrative.

In 1601, the Roman nobleman and collector Ciriaco Mattei paid 150 scudi for "The Supper at Emmaus," an oil on canvas by Caravaggio, marking a significant addition to his esteemed collection. Mattei, known for his discerning taste in art, recognized the unique ability of Caravaggio to capture pivotal spiritual moments with intense realism. This transaction brought the painting into Mattei's collection, where it remained as a private treasure, reflecting his status and taste within the art world of Rome. The artwork captures the biblical scene where the resurrected Christ reveals His identity to two disciples during a meal. Christ sits serenely at the center, while the surprised disciples react with vivid gestures — one disciple leans back abruptly, while the other raises his hands in astonishment. This theatrical composition was achieved through Caravaggio's use of oil and tempera on canvas, enabling a depth of emotion and realism that was pioneering for its time. Eventually, "The Supper at Emmaus" found its way to The National Gallery, where it now serves as a public masterpiece, accessible to countless visitors and marking a shift from private ownership to public exhibition. As you look upon this remarkable work, consider the influence Caravaggio's technique of dramatic illumination has had on artists who followed, such as Vigée Le Brun, whose outdoor compositions also explored the power of light and transformation.

This tour highlights the influence and technique of Vigée Le Brun, showcasing how emotional depth and naturalistic settings resonate across various masterpieces.
