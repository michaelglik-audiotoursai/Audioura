Stop 1: Harpe by Naderman (Paris, 1780)

Address: 15 Rue Droite, 06300 Nice, France

Coordinates: 43.6984, 7.276

Orientation: You are about to explore the Palais Lascaris in Nice, a historic site now transformed into a music museum housing an extraordinary collection of musical instruments. Within these walls, you will encounter four remarkable works: a Harpe by Naderman from Paris, 1780; a Guitar by the legendary Antonio de Torres from Almeria, 1884; a Sacqueboute ténor by Anton Schnitzer crafted in Nuremberg, 1581; and a Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore, made in Milan, 1696. This collection, bequeathed to the city of Nice through a testament in 1901, reveals the fascinating evolution of musical instruments across centuries. The transformation of the Palais Lascaris into a music museum was envisioned in 2001, with its permanent exhibition opening to the public in 2011. In the stops ahead, you will encounter the harp found in the attic of the Château de Busset at Harpe by Naderman (Paris, 1780) and the 1884 guitar crafted by Antonio de Torres Jurado at Guitar by Antonio de Torres (Almeria, 1884). Your first stop is Harpe by Naderman (Paris, 1780). To best appreciate the "Harpe by Naderman (Paris, 1780)" at the Palais Lascaris, stand a few feet back from the display. This vantage point allows you to take in the harp's full height and elegant lines, highlighting its grandeur and craftsmanship, which reflect the opulence of the French aristocracy before the French Revolution.

The "Harpe by Naderman," crafted around 1780 by Jean-Henri Naderman, stands as a monument to the lavish musical tastes of the French elite during the late 18th century. This harp is part of the extensive collection of over 500 instruments housed at the Palais Lascaris, a 17th-century aristocratic building turned music museum in Nice. The harp's height, over 161 centimeters, showcases its commanding presence, while its construction from wood and other materials speaks to the artistry and skill of its maker. Jean-Henri Naderman, a renowned harp maker in Paris, was known for crafting instruments for the court of Louis XVI, possibly even herself. This connection to the French monarchy underscores the harp's cultural significance during a time when music symbolized social status and refinement. Its presence connects to the broader narrative of musical innovation and tradition within the museum's walls. This instrument stands alongside the first prototypes of the modern harp by Sébastien Érard, who revolutionized harp mechanics with his double-action harp design. The harp represents a musical and social revolution. Other instruments in this collection also witnessed and adapted to such monumental changes.

Stop 2: Guitar by Antonio de Torres (Almeria, 1884)

Address: 15 Rue Droite, 06300 Nice, France

Orientation: Display marked "Guitar by Antonio de Torres (Almeria is 1884)" within this hall dedicated to string instruments. From this vantage point, you can appreciate the craftsmanship that marks a pivotal moment in the history of the guitar, offering a bridge from the folk traditions to the concert stages of the world.

In 1884, the legendary luthier Antonio de Torres Jurado crafted a guitar in Almeria, Spain, that solidified his reputation as the father of the modern classical guitar. This instrument, now housed within the Palais Lascaris, a seventeenth-century aristocratic building in Nice, embodies Torres' groundbreaking approach to guitar making. Torres, celebrated for his innovative designs, transformed the guitar into the form recognized today, enhancing its volume and tonal richness. His craftsmanship earned him the moniker "Stradivari of guitar making," a reference to the revered Italian violin maker Antonio Stradivari, known for his exceptionally fine violins. The workshop in Almeria produced numerous guitars, as noted in an 1884 issue of the Diario de Barcelona, where the output was described as "guitars that come out of my workshops" in plural. Each guitar displayed meticulous attention to detail, from the choice of wood to the precise construction techniques that enhanced the guitar's sound and playability. Among a collection of over 500 instruments, Torres' guitar is displayed alongside celebrated creations like the harpe by Naderman from 1780. These instruments collectively illustrate the evolution of music and craftsmanship across centuries. The influence of Torres' guitar is evident, as earlier instruments such as a simple lute or mandolin similarly propelled the trajectory of musical innovation.

Stop 3: Sacqueboute ténor by Anton Schnitzer (Nuremberg, 1581)

Address: 15 Rue Droite, 06300 Nice, France

Orientation: Look closely at the tenor sackbut by Anton Schnitzer from 1581, standing in the musical instrument collection of the Palais Lascaris. By positioning yourself slightly to the side, the intricate details on its bell garland become visible, revealing the same mark as another Schnitzer instrument—a testament to the craftsmanship of this era.

The tenor sackbut, a precursor to the modern trombone, represents a fascinating period in musical history. Originating from Nuremberg in 1581, it is one of the earliest surviving sackbuts in its original state. During this time, the boundaries between church and court music began to blur, leading to new musical forms and instruments. The sackbut played a pivotal role in both settings, providing a rich, sonorous voice capable of both solemn hymns and lively dances. Anton Schnitzer, the craftsman behind this sackbut, was part of a renowned family of instrument makers in Nuremberg. His work is notable for its durability and precision—a necessity for an instrument that would be played in various contexts. The mark on the bell garland signifies not only the maker but also the era's intricate artistry. This sackbut's survival in such pristine condition allows us a rare glimpse into the past, bridging centuries of musical evolution. The Palais Lascaris, which houses over 500 instruments, is second only in France to the Musée de la Musique in Paris, part of the Cité de la Musique complex. Instruments like the guitar by Antonio de Torres from 1884, also part of this collection, illustrate the ongoing dialogue between innovation and tradition. Some instruments, such as the tenor sackbut by Anton Schnitzer, demonstrate how innovation from this era combines with the craftsmanship of later centuries, serving as a powerful reminder of music's enduring journey.

Stop 4: Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore (Milan, 1696)

Address: 15 Rue Droite, 06300 Nice, France

Orientation: Stand near the display case that houses the "Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore (Milan, 1696)." From this vantage, you can appreciate the fine craftsmanship that defines this instrument, one of the gems in the Palais Lascaris collection, which is renowned as France’s second most important collection of musical instruments.

The "Basse de violon" by Paolo Antonio Testore, crafted in 1696, is a testament to the Italian luthier tradition. Paolo Antonio, part of the renowned Testore family, continued the legacy of his father, Carlo Giuseppe Testore, who established the family workshop in Milan. This bass viol, now part of the Palais Lascaris's prestigious collection, exemplifies the meticulous artistry synonymous with the Testore name. The instrument’s presence here, amid over 500 other pieces, underscores the museum’s dedication to preserving the rich history of musical craftsmanship. This collection, transferred from the Musee Massena to the Palais Lascaris in 2001, culminated in the opening of the permanent exhibition in 2011. With instruments like the "Basse de violon" and the Sacqueboute ténor by Anton Schnitzer, the museum illustrates the cross-cultural influences and innovations in musical instrument design across Europe. How these instruments influenced each other across regions and time remains a fascinating subject for further exploration.
