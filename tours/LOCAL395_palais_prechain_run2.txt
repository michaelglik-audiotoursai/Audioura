Stop 2: Guitar by Antonio de Torres (Almeria, 1884)

Address: 15 Rue Droite, 06300 Nice, France

Antonio de Torres, born in Almería, Spain, in 1817, is widely regarded as the father of the modern guitar. His work in the latter half of the 19th century revolutionized the instrument, setting standards still revered today. Torres’s guitars, crafted with a meticulous attention to detail, introduced design elements that enhanced the guitar's resonance and tonal quality. This particular guitar, made in 1884, represents the culmination of his innovations. In his writings, notably the 1884 Diario de Barcelona, the 1884 publication discussing guitar excellence, Torres himself remarked on the unmatched excellence of the guitars emerging from his workshop. This specific guitar not only embodies his technical advancements but also his deep commitment to the craft. Torres's work transformed guitar making, similar to how Antonio Stradivari shaped violin making, earning him comparisons to the legendary luthier. The Palais Lascaris, a seventeenth-century aristocratic palace turned musical instrument museum, houses this Torres guitar among its extensive collection of over 500 instruments. This collection, second in France only to the Musée de la Musique in Paris, part of the Cité de la Musique complex, features a range of historical pieces, including violas da gamba and bass violins, showcasing the evolution of string instruments over centuries. Antonio de Torres's contributions to guitar making were pivotal, influencing musicians across Europe and beyond. His emphasis on soundboard construction and bracing patterns created a richer, more powerful sound that defined the classical guitar as we know it. As you consider the broader collection here, one can trace the lineage of innovation that connects Torres's work to other pivotal changes in the history of musical instruments, such as those seen in the Romantic era.

Stop 3: Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore (Milan, 1696)

Address: 15 Rue Droite, 06300 Nice, France

The "Basse de violon" by Paolo Antonio Testore, dated 1696, represents a pinnacle of Italian string instrument craftsmanship from Milan. The Testore family, known for their meticulous work, was a lineage of luthiers with a reputation for producing instruments of both beauty and functionality. Paolo Antonio, who learned the craft from his father, Carlo Giuseppe Testore, continued this tradition in his Milan workshop. The bass viol, a string instrument popular in the Baroque period, offers a glimpse into the musical preferences of the time. Its construction and design reflect the period's emphasis on both tonal quality and visual elegance, capturing the essence of music as both an auditory and aesthetic experience. The collection at Palais Lascaris, where this bass viol resides, was augmented in 2001 when Nice's historical musical instruments were transferred here. The bass viol by Testore stands among over 500 instruments, each telling its own story, yet together they narrate the evolution of music and instrument craftsmanship. The sounds that once resonated from these strings might have inspired the next century's musical innovations.

Stop 4: Sacqueboute ténor by Anton Schnitzer (Nuremberg, 1581)

Address: 15 Rue Droite, 06300 Nice, France

A sackbut is an early form of the trombone, with a narrower bore and less flared bell than modern versions. This particular piece is noted for its preservation, retaining original features that have often been lost in other instruments of its era. The sackbut bears the same mark as another Schnitzer instrument, sharing the date 1581 on its bell garland. This detail underscores the consistency and quality of Schnitzer's craftsmanship during the late Renaissance period in Nuremberg, a city renowned for its brass instrument production. Understanding the sackbut's role in historical contexts offers insight into its importance. During the Renaissance, such instruments were pivotal in both sacred and secular music. They featured prominently in church music and courtly events, providing the rich, sonorous tones that modern orchestras now expect from their trombone sections. As you consider the sackbut's place within the broader collection at Palais Lascaris, a seventeenth-century aristocratic building in Nice, it complements the museum's vast array of over 500 instruments, placing it as France's second most significant collection after the Musée de la Musique in Paris. This sackbut's enduring legacy is not merely an artifact of the past; it continues to influence contemporary music, echoing the profound evolution of brass instruments over the centuries. The craftsmanship of Anton Schnitzer continues to resonate, reminding us of the enduring impact of such instruments on global musical traditions.
