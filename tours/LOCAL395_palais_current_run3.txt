Stop 2: Guitar by Antonio de Torres (Almeria, 1884)

Address: 15 Rue Droite, 06300 Nice, France

Orientation: Stand before the glass display case that houses the "Guitar by Antonio de Torres (Almeria, 1884)." From this position, you can observe the craftsmanship of the guitarmaker often referred to as the "Stradivari of guitar making."

The guitar crafted by Antonio de Torres in 1884 represents a pivotal moment in musical history. Torres, born in 1817 in Almería, Spain, is celebrated as the father of the modern guitar. His innovations in design and construction reshaped the instrument, elevating its sound and playability to unprecedented levels. By 1884, Torres had achieved a reputation that rivaled the legendary luthiers of history, and his work was renowned for its excellence, as he himself noted in the Diario de Barcelona. This guitar from his Almería workshop stands as a testament to his mastery, a culmination of his years of refinement and experimentation. Torres spoke of his guitars in the plural form, acknowledging the collective excellence that emerged from his workshops. The guitar's presence in the Palais Lascaris, a seventeenth-century aristocratic building now housing a musical instrument museum, connects it to a broader narrative of musical evolution. This museum, with its significant collection of over 500 instruments, provides a fitting backdrop for Torres's work, echoing the historical journey of stringed instruments. The guitar changed the course of classical music and influenced the future of guitar making, setting a standard for craftsmanship and sound quality that persists today. The innovations introduced by Torres laid the groundwork for a century of musical exploration, leaving an indelible mark on the world of music.

The Palais Lascaris houses an exquisite bass viol crafted by Paolo Antonio Testore in Milan. This instrument represents a piece of the Testore family's legacy, a lineage beginning with Carlo Giuseppe Testore, who was a pupil of Giovanni Grancino. Dating back to 1696, this bass viol originates from a time when Milan was a hub for luthiers, or string instrument makers. The Testore family workshop was renowned for producing high-quality instruments, and this piece is a testament to their skill. The Palais Lascaris is not just any museum, a baroque palace from the 17th century,; it boasts France's second most important collection of musical instruments after the Musée de la Musique in Paris. This bass viol is part of a broader narrative, illustrating the evolution of string instruments and their role in music history. The collection also includes rare baroque guitars and violas da gamba, offering a comprehensive view of early stringed instrument craftsmanship. The innovations in plucked instruments that emerged in the 19th century set the stage for new musical expressions.

The tenor sackbut crafted by Anton Schnitzer in 1581 is a testament to the artistry and craftsmanship of Renaissance instrument makers. It bears the same mark as another of Schnitzer’s works, etched with the year 1581 on the bell garland, linking it directly to the master artisan’s hand. The sackbut's design, with its elongated slide and conical mouthpiece, reflects the evolving musical demands of the late 16th century. This period marked a transition towards more complex compositions, necessitating instruments capable of varied pitches and dynamic ranges. The inclusion of this piece in the Palais Lascaris, a seventeenth-century aristocratic building in nice, collection highlights the museum's dedication to preserving musical heritage, as it is one of over 500 instruments housed here, making it the second most significant collection in France after Paris's Musée de la Musique. Understanding the cultural context of the sackbut enriches our appreciation. During the Renaissance, music was integral to both courtly and religious life, and instruments like this sackbut played a crucial role in ensembles that performed in grand halls and cathedrals. The sackbut's sound would have complemented voices and other instruments, contributing to the rich texture of Renaissance music. This instrument, alongside others in the Palais Lascaris, offers a tangible connection to the past, showcasing the evolution of musical instruments and their impact on contemporary music.
