The "Harpe by Naderman," crafted around 1780, stands as a historical artifact within the Palais Lascaris, a 17th-century aristocratic building now transformed into a music museum. This harp, attributed to Henri Naderman, a prominent Parisian harp maker, is a testament to the evolution of musical instruments during a period when orchestral music was gaining prominence. The instrument measures an impressive 161.5 centimeters in height, crafted meticulously from wood and various materials. Its presence in the museum is not only a nod to Naderman's craftsmanship but also a reflection of the period's cultural and musical aspirations. One of the harp's notable aspects is its discovery in the attic in Allier, France. The harp's intricate design, likely commissioned for or inspired by the French court, where Jean-Henri Naderman served as a harpmaker for Queen Marie Antoinette, the last Queen of France, showcases the opulence and refinement of the era. Within the broader context of the Palais Lascaris, which houses over 500 instruments, this harp connects to the museum's narrative of musical evolution. The collection includes other significant artifacts, such as early prototypes by Sébastien Érard, a pioneer in harp development. These pieces illustrate the transformation of musical craftsmanship, showcasing how innovation in instrument design has shaped the soundscapes of future generations.

Stop 2: Guitar by Antonio de Torres (Almeria, 1884)

Address: 15 Rue Droite, 06300 Nice, France

In the year 1884, Antonio de Torres, the Stradivari of guitar making, crafted a piece that would redefine the world of string instruments. The Guitar by Antonio de Torres (Almeria, 1884) in front of you is more than an object; it is a bridge across time, connecting modern musicians with the legacy of its creator. Torres was a revolutionary, transforming the guitar from a modest folk instrument into a staple of classical composition. His innovations in design and construction, particularly the use of fan bracing, were groundbreaking. This technique involves arranging wooden struts in a fan pattern beneath the guitar's soundboard, enhancing its volume and tonal quality. The significance of this guitar extends beyond its technical advancements. Antonio de Torres often referred to his creations in the plural form—“the guitars that come out of my workshops”—indicating the prolific nature of his work and his confidence in the consistent excellence of his instruments. His 1884 statement in the Diario de Barcelona, the publication where he praised his guitars, encapsulates this pride and his dedication to quality. As you stand in Palais Lascaris, a seventeenth-century aristocratic building now home to one of France's most important musical instrument collections, consider the broader context of this guitar. The museum hosts over 500 instruments, including the Sacqueboute ténor by Anton Schnitzer, highlighting the transformative role of innovation in musical history. The guitar by Torres sits within this narrative as a pivotal chapter, challenging and expanding the role of string instruments in orchestras. Innovation played a significant role in the next leap forward for string instruments. This advancement is evident in the continued evolution and adaptation of designs, much like those pioneered by Torres himself.

Stop 3: Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore (Milan, 1696)

Address: 15 Rue Droite, 06300 Nice, France

This particular bass viol represents the skilled artistry of Paolo Antonio Testore, who followed in the footsteps of his father, Carlo Giuseppe Testore. Carlo, a pupil of Giovanni Grancino, moved to Milan to establish a workshop renowned for producing exquisite string instruments. Paolo Antonio continued this tradition, and this piece is a testament to the enduring quality of their work. The Palais Lascaris, the music museum in Nice, with its collection of over 500 instruments, houses this viol as part of its commitment to preserving musical history. The transition of Nice historical musical collections in 2001, to the Palais Lascaris, marked a significant enhancement of the museum's offerings. The viol's presence here not only highlights the craftsmanship of the Testore family but also reflects the museum's dedication to showcasing instruments that have shaped musical traditions. The viol's inclusion in this collection highlights the Testore family's significant impact on instrument making. Their work, exemplified by this viol, is renowned for its quality and rich sound. The Testore viols traveled from the bustling workshops of Milan to the serene halls of the Palais Lascaris, creating a connection between past and present through craftsmanship and history.

The Sacqueboute ténor by Anton Schnitzer, crafted in Nuremberg in 1581, is a remarkable relic of Renaissance brass instruments.
