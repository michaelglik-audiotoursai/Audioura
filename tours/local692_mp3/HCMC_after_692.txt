Step-by-Step Audio Guided Tour: Ho Chi Minh City Museum of Fine Arts, Vietnam
Tour-Category: museum

Stop 1: North Central South Spring Garden

Coordinates: 10.7761, 106.6948

The museum is open Monday to Sunday from 8:00 to 5:00.

Orientation: Delve into the artistic evolution of Vietnam showcased through varied mediums, from traditional clothing depictions to modernist paradises, embodying a rich tapestry of historical narratives within this cultural haven. Your first stop is North Central South Spring Garden. Stand before Nguyễn Gia Trí (say: ngwen zah chee)'s "North Central South Spring Garden" at the Ho Chi Minh City Museum of Fine Arts to fully appreciate the interplay of delicate lacquer panels and vibrant storytelling. From this vantage point, observe the harmonious merge of the North, Central, and South of Vietnam, as depicted by young women in traditional áo dài amid a festive spring setting.

Nguyễn Gia Trí, an iconic figure in Vietnamese art, dedicated twenty years to crafting "North Central South Spring Garden," completing it in 1989. Through his meticulous use of lacquer, a traditional Vietnamese medium that builds images through layers of resin and pigments, Tri captures the essence of unity and diversity in Vietnam. This work is not just a visual feast but a historical tapestry woven from nine separate panels, each panel celebrating a region's distinct culture. Tri's dedication to this project illustrates his commitment to transforming traditional craft into a fine art form. The narrative portrays vibrant scenes of spring, where young women from the northern, central, and southern regions of Vietnam make their way to a spring festival. These figures, rendered in delicate layers of lacquer, are enveloped by pagodas, trees, and an explosion of spring flowers, evoking a sense of renewal and hope. Tri's technique involves layering lacquer to create shimmering effects, which imbue the painting with a sense of depth and movement rare in static art forms. This piece serves as a compelling example of how an artist can bridge traditional techniques with contemporary storytelling, leaving a lasting influence on the spirit and technique of future generations. The rich interplay of regions in the artwork prompts one to consider how these diverse influences continue to inspire Vietnamese artists today.

Stop 2: The Girl with the Pearl Earring

Coordinates: 10.7761, 106.6948

Orientation: Stand before "The Girl with the Pearl Earring," allowing the subtle play of light to guide your gaze from the pearl to the enigmatic eyes of the subject. This position reveals the mastery of Vermeer's use of shadow and light, a hallmark of his work.

Created by Johannes Vermeer in 1657, this painting is often called the "Mona Lisa of the North" and continues to intrigue audiences with its skillful composition. The painting portrays a young woman turning her head over her shoulder. The large pearl earring catches the light with deftly applied strokes, holding the viewer’s gaze in its shimmer. The dark, undefined background serves to highlight her softly illuminated features, providing contrast that enhances the mystery of her expression. During its creation, Vermeer resided in Delft, immersed in a vibrant artistic culture. This era saw him transition from larger biblical and mythological scenes to more intimate portrayals of single figures, including this work. His signature technique of capturing the subtleties of light and shadow was especially advanced in this work, creating a lifelike presence that has captivated viewers for centuries. At a symposium held by the Rijksmuseum, scholars presented X-ray evidence revealing that Vermeer painted over an earlier self-portrait in another piece, "A Maid Asleep." This discovery provides insight into Vermeer’s evolving artistic process, suggesting a willingness to repurpose his canvases, driven either by resource constraints or a relentless pursuit of artistic refinement. This revelation adds depth to our understanding of Vermeer’s methodologies and his commitment to perfecting his craft. Standing before this Western masterpiece in an Eastern setting like the Ho Chi Minh City Museum of Fine Arts, one senses a dialogue between cultures, as the intricacies of Western art inspire curiosity about its manifestation in Vietnamese art forms. This unique positioning encourages reflection on the universal language of art and how Western influences might weave their nuances into the tapestry of Vietnamese art history.

Directions: Next: The Ho Chi Minh City Museum of Fine Arts building.

Stop 3: The Ho Chi Minh City Museum of Fine Arts building

Coordinates: 10.7761, 106.6948

Orientation: As you stand here, take a moment to appreciate the edifice of the Ho Chi Minh City Museum of Fine Arts. Look closely at its unique blend of French colonial architecture and Vietnamese elements. The building’s façade is a delightful mix of stately columns and intricate ironwork, melding the influences that have touched this region over time.

In 1929, the construction of this building was completed. It was initially intended as a private residence, reflecting the architectural ambitions of the time. However, its purpose shifted when it was repurposed to house the Ho Chi Minh City Museum of Fine Arts, holding an impressive collection of over 22,000 objects. This transformation turned it into a major art institution in Ho Chi Minh City, second only to the Vietnam National Museum of Fine Arts in Hanoi. The museum boasts remarkable pieces, including some of the best Champa and Óc Eo relics, highlighting the region's archaeological significance. One fascinating story concerns the inclusion of traditional Vietnamese styles like Hàng Trống and Đông Hồ woodcut paintings in its collection. These were brought to the museum by efforts to preserve cultural heritage, spearheaded by local scholars who insisted on including these historically significant works. As you continue your exploration, consider how the interplay of cultures and epochs has shaped not just this museum, but also Vietnamese art as a whole. Builders outfitted the mansion with modern Art Deco features, Eastern decorative traditions, and one of the earliest elevators in Saigon.

Stop 4: Lacquer painting collection

Coordinates: 10.7761, 106.6948

Orientation: To truly appreciate the lacquer painting collection, stand close enough to the artworks to observe the intricate play of light across their surfaces. From this vantage, the depth and iridescence unique to Vietnamese lacquer art become strikingly apparent.

Each piece in the collection is crafted using the painstaking process of applying multiple coats of natural resin, a technique that dates back centuries. This method involves sanding each layer to a smooth finish before adding the next, allowing vibrant colors and intricate patterns to emerge over time. The museum's collection highlights the works of extraordinary Vietnamese artists who have mastered this age-old technique. Among them, Tran Dinh Khuong played a crucial role in popularizing lacquer painting in modern Vietnamese art circles. His dedication to the craft, along with that of his contemporaries, led to a renaissance of lacquer techniques in the 20th century, ensuring that this traditional form remained relevant in Vietnam’s evolving art scene. The museum’s acquisition of these significant works, facilitated by dedicated curators, emphasizes the importance of weaving Vietnam’s traditional craftsmanship into the fabric of contemporary art. This fusion is palpable in the museum’s historic setting, where past and present meet.

Stop 5: Silk painting collection

Coordinates: 10.7761, 106.6948

Orientation: This collection is best appreciated from the center of the room, where you can fully immerse yourself in the delicate intricacies of the silk paintings that wind through Vietnamese history like a soft, enduring thread.

Through these acquisitions, the museum emphasizes the cultural importance and diversity of Vietnamese art, drawing visitors who seek to explore these unique techniques and themes. The medium involves applying watercolor directly onto silk, a technique that requires precision and an understanding of how the paint interacts with the fine threads. This method allows for a translucency and depth of color that are unparalleled, offering viewers a glimpse into the subtleties of Vietnamese life and landscape. Each painting reveals a story, whether it be of rural scenes, historical events, or everyday life, rendered in soft, flowing lines and luminous colors. This addition not only expanded the museum's scope but also solidified its position as a leading institution for Vietnamese art. Standing before these paintings, one may feel a sense of connection to the past, to the artisans who painstakingly created these works, and to the cultural heritage they represent. As you move forward, consider what stories these silk threads have to tell and what lessons they may impart about resilience and beauty.

This tour highlights the rich interplay of traditional techniques and contemporary storytelling in Vietnamese art, as seen in works like lacquer paintings and silk paintings.
