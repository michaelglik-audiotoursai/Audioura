Orientation: You are about to explore Palais Lascaris in Nice, home to a fascinating collection of historical musical instruments that span centuries of craftsmanship and innovation. Within these walls, you will encounter four captivating works: a harp by François Joseph Naderman, a guitar by Antonio de Torres, a basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore, and a sacqueboute ténor by Anton Schnitzer. Each of these instruments reflects a pivotal moment in the evolution of their respective musical forms. Notably, the Naderman harp was among the instruments transferred to Palais Lascaris from the Musee Massena in 2001, culminating in the opening of the permanent exhibition in 2011. Your first stop is Harpe by Naderman (Paris, 1780).

The Harpe by Naderman is a striking example of 18th-century French musical craftsmanship. Created around 1780, this harp, attributed to Jean-Henri Naderman, showcases the elegant design typical of instruments used in the court of Louis XVI. Naderman, known for making harps for notable figures, likely crafted instruments for Marie Antoinette, the last queen before the revolution, Queen of France, highlighting the harp's connection to the opulence of the French court. This harp is part of a larger collection at the Palais Lascaris, which was transformed into a music museum in 2011 after its collections were moved from the Musee Massena, the former music museum in Nice. The Palais Lascaris now houses over 500 instruments, making it one of France's most significant collections, second only to the Musée de la Musique in Paris, part of the Cité de la Musique. The museum is housed in a 17th-century aristocratic building, which provides a fitting backdrop for these historical treasures. The harp stands at an impressive height of 161.5 cm, crafted from wood and adorned with materials typical of the period. The selection of materials enhances its visual appeal and contributes to its acoustic qualities. The presence of this harp in the collection at Palais Lascaris offers a glimpse into the world of 18th-century music, where such instruments played a crucial role in the cultural life of the French aristocracy. The innovations of this era set the stage for the development of string instruments that would follow, paving the way for future advancements in musical expression.

Stop 2: Guitar by Antonio de Torres (Almeria, 1884)

Address: 15 Rue Droite, 06300 Nice, France

His work in Almeria marked the culmination of innovations that forever changed the landscape of classical guitar making. Torres's influence is often likened to that of Antonio Stradivari in violin making, underscoring the profound impact his designs had on the instrument's evolution. In the Diario de Barcelona, Torres himself extolled the excellence of his guitars, referring to them in the plural form, highlighting the collective craftsmanship emerging from his workshops. The Palais Lascaris, where this guitar is displayed, serves as a fitting backdrop. A seventeenth-century aristocratic residence, it now houses a vast collection of over 500 musical instruments, making it France's second-most significant collection after the Musée de la Musique in Paris, part of the cité de la musique complex. Within this rich tapestry of musical history, the guitar by Torres stands as a testament to the transformative power of innovation in the arts. The guitar's presence here connects to the broader narrative of musical evolution represented within the museum. This guitar represents the ongoing transformations in the era of the classical guitar, inspired by the enduring legacy of Antonio de Torres.

Stop 3: Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore (Milan, 1696)

Address: 15 Rue Droite, 06300 Nice, France

This bass viol is a testament to Paolo Antonio Testore's skill, part of a lineage of renowned luthiers. Paolo, along with his brother Carlo Antonio, continued the family tradition established by their father, Carlo Giuseppe Testore, who had moved to Milan to establish his workshop. The Testore family became known for their exquisite string instruments, and this viol represents both their artistry and the era's musical evolution. The transfer of Nice's historical musical instrument collection to Palais Lascaris in 2001 was a pivotal moment, transforming the venue into a premier music museum. With over 500 instruments, it ranks as France second most significant collection after. The collection includes varied treasures, from violas da gamba to rare baroque guitars, each piece offering a glimpse into different periods and styles. The bass viol stands among 500 instruments, connecting the past to the present. The broader collection illustrates the evolution of music and craftsmanship over centuries. Looking forward, consider how future innovations, like those of the Industrial Revolution, further transformed musical craftsmanship. The viol's place in history marks the beginning of a shift in music that would continue to evolve in the centuries to come.

Stop 4: Sacqueboute ténor by Anton Schnitzer (Nuremberg, 1581)

Address: 15 Rue Droite, 06300 Nice, France

This piece is not just a relic; it's a window into the musical past, showcasing the technological and artistic achievements of the Renaissance era. Schnitzer, a renowned instrument maker of his time, crafted this sackbut with a precision that has allowed it to endure through centuries. Its preservation in such an original state is rare, making it a significant artifact within the Palais Lascaris, a seventeenth-century aristocratic building in Nice, collection. Sackbuts are the ancestors of modern trombones, and their design reflects the musical needs and aesthetic preferences of the late 16th century. The slide mechanism of the sackbut, which allows for a range of notes to be played smoothly, was revolutionary for its time. This design enabled musicians to explore new musical expressions, paving the way for the versatility seen in modern brass instruments. The Palais Lascaris, with its extensive collection of over 500 instruments, provides the perfect backdrop for this historical piece. As the second most important collection of its kind in France, it offers a comprehensive view of the evolution of musical instruments. The tenor sackbut by Anton Schnitzer not only complements the museum's collection but also underscores the rich history of musical innovation showcased here. The presence of this sackbut highlights the enduring impact of early instrument makers like Schnitzer. His work laid a foundation for future innovations in musical instrument design, influencing how music would be played and heard for generations.
