Before we look at anything on the walls, here is the story of Boston Athenæum in Boston, Massachusetts itself — who created it, why it exists, and what it is known for. The Boston Athenæum is one of the oldest independent libraries in the United States. It is a membership library, for which patrons pay a yearly subscription fee to use Athenæum services. The institution was founded in 1807 by the Anthology Club of Boston, Massachusetts. It is located at 10½ Beacon Street on Beacon Hill in Boston. In 1809, it moved to the Rufus Amory House, adjacent to the King’s Chapel Burial Ground. Construction on the Athenaeum’s present home on Beacon Street began in 1847. Designed by Edward Clarke Cabot, the building opened in 1849, with a sculpture gallery on the first floor, the book collection on the second, and a painting gallery on the skylit third floor. In 1868, Charles Ammi Cutter assumed the role of Librarian at the Boston Athenaeum. During his tenure, he developed the Expansive Classification system, an ambitious undertaking aimed at creating a comprehensive yet user-friendly way to organize knowledge. This system featured alphanumeric codes, offering an innovative method for cataloging diverse materials with precision. In 1891, Cutter published the first edition of his classification system, laying the groundwork for future library science innovations. However, many smaller libraries found his system too intricate, prompting requests for adaptations. Orientation: Stand near the central display of Annie Adams Fields' contributions, where light falls upon her groundbreaking book covers. This position allows you to see the subtle departures in design that redefined literary aesthetics in Boston.

Annie Adams Fields, born in Boston in 1834, was an influential figure in literary and social circles. Educated at the School for Young Ladies, she developed a lifelong passion for literature and self-expression. In the 1880s, Fields began designing book covers for the Boston publisher Houghton, Mifflin. Her designs embraced simplicity over the lavish Victorian norms, reshaping the visual language of published works. This shift had a tangible impact on how books were perceived and valued by their covers. Fields was married to James Thomas Fields, a well-known publisher. Together, they created a salon environment in their Beacon Hill home, nurturing connections among literary luminaries. Charles Ammi Cutter, often referred to simply as He for his pioneering efforts, created his own classification system, known as the Expansive Classification. This was an attempt to complete and organize the five-volume set left unfinished by his predecessors, offering a systematic approach to cataloging books. This action mirrored the structured and accessible approach that Fields used in her book designs, promoting a broader accessibility in literature. The collaborative nature of these efforts among Fields, Houghton, Mifflin, and Commonwealth exemplified a flourishing period of literary and artistic advancement.
