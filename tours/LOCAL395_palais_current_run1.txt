In this illustrious collection, the Harpe by Naderman occupies a significant place. Crafted in Paris around 1780 by Henri Naderman, this harp exemplifies the artistry and sophistication of its era. At the time, Paris was a hub for musical innovation, and Naderman was at the forefront of this movement, designing instruments that not only produced beautiful music but also stood as works of art. The harp's wooden frame and intricate design speak to the craftsmanship of the late 18th century. Its construction involved various materials, each chosen for its ability to contribute to the instrument's resonant sound. The harp was discovered in the attic in Allier, France. Its long period of concealment highlights the transient nature of art and culture, where objects of beauty can be lost and found across generations. The Palais Lascaris, transformed into a music museum, home to this harp, boasts over 500 instruments, making it the second most important collection in France after the Musée de la Musique de la Philharmonie in Paris. This harp, along with the many others, forms a bridge between the past and the present, inviting us to explore how musical instruments have evolved and what future innovations might await in the world of sound.

Stop 2: Guitar by Antonio de Torres (Almeria, 1884)

Address: 15 Rue Droite, 06300 Nice, France

Orientation: Stand in the central gallery of Palais Lascaris, where the collection of string instruments showcases a transformative period in music history. Direct your gaze toward the Guitar by Antonio de Torres, made in Almeria in 1884. This position allows you to appreciate the craftsmanship of an instrument that redefined its class.

His innovations in design and construction are legendary, earning him the moniker "the Stradivari of guitar making." At this time, he confidently declared in the Diario de Barcelona, "The guitars that come out of my workshops" — a testament to the excellence and precision that defined his work. Torres revolutionized guitar construction through his inventive approach, which would come to shape the classical guitar as we recognize it today. By refining the body shape and employing innovative bracing techniques, Torres enhanced both the sound and resonance of the instrument, setting a new standard that others would follow. The guitar's presence at Palais Lascaris, a seventeenth-century aristocratic building turned musical instrument museum, underscores its significance within a broader narrative of musical evolution. While the guitar's precise visual details remain unverified here, its historical impact is indisputable. The Palais Lascaris offers a window into the evolution of musical instruments, illustrating how craftsmanship and innovation can redefine an entire genre.

Stop 3: Sacqueboute ténor by Anton Schnitzer (Nuremberg, 1581)

Address: 15 Rue Droite, 06300 Nice, France

Orientation: As you stand near the tenor sackbut by Anton Schnitzer, positioned carefully within the Palais Lascaris, notice the sheer age and craftsmanship echoing through the centuries from 1581.

The tenor sackbut is a brass instrument that predates the modern trombone, known for its distinct slide mechanism allowing for a range of pitches. Crafted by Anton Schnitzer of Nuremberg, a renowned 16th-century instrument maker, this sackbut is one of the earliest surviving examples in its original form. Its presence here highlights the evolution of brass instruments during the Renaissance. It is part of a collection formed around the bequest. The tenor sackbut stands as an important piece of music history and a testament to craftsmanship that has weathered centuries.

Stop 4: Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore (Milan, 1696)

Address: 15 Rue Droite, 06300 Nice, France

Orientation: Stand slightly to the right of the Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore, crafted in Milan in 1696. From this angle, the intricate craftsmanship of the instrument's body catches the light, revealing the careful handiwork that went into its creation.

Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore, dated 1696, is a testament to the enduring legacy of the Testore family's craftsmanship. The instrument, housed within the Palais Lascaris, the music museum established in 2001, represents an era when Milan was a hub of musical innovation. Paolo Antonio Testore, continuing the tradition of his father Carlo Giuseppe Testore, contributed to this legacy with instruments renowned for their rich sound and exquisite detail. The Palais Lascaris itself has become a custodian of such historical treasures since 2001, when the musical instrument collections of Nice were transferred here. In this context, Testore's work stands alongside other remarkable pieces, like the Harpe by Naderman from 1780, highlighting the diverse narratives of musical history. Such innovations laid the groundwork for music's global influence, echoing through centuries of artistry and craftsmanship.
