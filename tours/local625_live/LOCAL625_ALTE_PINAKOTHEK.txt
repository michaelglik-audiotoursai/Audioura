Stop 1: The Holy Family

Address: Alte Pinakothek, Barer Str. 27, 80333 München, Germany

Coordinates: 48.1494, 11.5705

Museum Information: 10.00–18.00

Orientation: You are about to explore the Alte Pinakothek in Munich. The two works, spanning different periods and styles, come together to explore the themes of identity and self-reflection through religious and allegorical art. The Canigiani Holy Family, restored in 1983, invites contemplation beyond the frames, while Andrea del Sarto's profound meditation in "The Holy Family" offers a deeper understanding of the subject matter. Your first stop is The Holy Family. As you approach The Holy Family by Raphael in the Alte Pinakothek, stand slightly to the right to appreciate the way light interacts with the painting's surface, bringing out the luminescence of the colors and the subtleties in the characters' expressions that might otherwise remain unnoticed.

The Holy Family, a masterpiece by Raphael, was commissioned by Florentine merchant Domenico Canigiani around 1505–1507. Canigiani, eager to celebrate his marriage, intended the painting, crafted with oil on canvas, for a private domestic altar. This commission marked a pivotal moment for Raphael, aligning him with Florence's influential patrons and providing a platform to showcase his exquisite skill in a domestic religious setting. In 1983, The Holy Family was restored as a tribute to Raphael's enduring legacy, commemorating the 500th anniversary of his birth. Initially created for private devotion, the painting now captivates a broader audience at the Alte Pinakothek, a transformation that allows countless visitors to appreciate its impact within the context of art history. Raphael, active during the Italian High Renaissance, found himself in a flourishing artistic environment of Florence when he created this work. His talent in capturing both the divine and the human intimacy in his compositions distinguished him from his contemporaries. This painting illustrates his mastery of depicting serene spirituality intertwined with tender human interaction, a signature of his artistic exploration at the time. The current exhibition, intriguingly, draws attention more to the frames than the paintings themselves, inviting viewers to consider the artwork's boundary, designed to enhance its narrative and visual impact. The interplay of the art and its frame in The Holy Family emphasizes the carefully curated harmony Raphael strived to achieve, leaving a lasting impression of peace and reverence.

Directions: Continue through Alte Pinakothek — next is Allegorische Frauengestalt mit Spiegel.

Stop 2: Allegorische Frauengestalt mit Spiegel

Address: Alte Pinakothek, Barer Str. 27, 80333 München, Germany

Coordinates: 48.1494, 11.5705

Orientation: To fully appreciate Allegorische Frauengestalt mit Spiegel, stand directly in front of the painting to capture the luminosity of Hans Baldung Grien’s oil on panel work. From this vantage point, the mirror in the woman's hand becomes a portal, reflecting not only light but deeper allegorical truths.

In 1529, Hans Baldung Grien, a german painter of the northern renaissance, created this enigmatic piece, titled Allegorische Frauengestalt mit Spiegel, Schlange, Hirsch und Hündin, during a pivotal moment in his career. Known for his symbolic complexity, Baldung painted a woman holding a mirror, surrounded by a serpent, a stag, and a hind. Baldung, who primarily worked in Strasbourg and Freiburg, often incorporated fantastical elements into his art, as seen here. His creative choices bridged the sacred and the secular, offering viewers a chance for self-examination through the allegorical use of the mirror. The painting’s acquisition by reflects its enduring importance. By including this work in its collection, the Alte Pinakothek, emphasizing older artworks, underscores its commitment to showcasing pivotal moments in art history. Standing before this piece, the viewer sees not just paint on panel but a window into the interplay of human nature and its reflections — both real and metaphorical. The mirror reveals reflections in art.

Across these stops, one thread runs through: religious devotion in the sixteenth century. Seen together, the works show how differently that subject could be imagined. That's 2 stops in all.
