Stop 1: Violes gambe by William Turner (Londres, 1652)

Coordinates: 43.6954, 7.2657

Orientation: You are about to explore the Palais Lascaris in Nice, where a remarkable collection awaits. Encounter the Violes gambe by William Turner, the Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore, and the Harpe by Naderman. Each work showcases the transformation of musical expression in Europe through craftsmanship and innovation. Testore's 1696 bass viol, part of the historical musical instrument collection transferred to the Palais Lascaris in 2001, sets the stage for a journey through time and musical evolution. Your first stop is Violes gambe by William Turner (Londres, 1652). As you stand in the Palais Lascaris, let your gaze fall upon the viola da gamba crafted by William Turner in 1652. This position allows you to see the delicate artistry that reflects not just Turner's skill but also the turbulent times of its creation.

Turner, primarily known as a painter in London, extended his artistry to the intricate design of violas da gamba during a period of English Civil War uncertainty. His violas, distinguished by their heart-shaped contours, are not merely instruments; they symbolize the resilience and cultural continuity of England during tumultuous times. The Palais Lascaris now stands as the second most important historical musical instrument collection, surpassed only by the Musée de la Musique in Paris. This viol, among other pieces, continues to reflect the aesthetic aspirations and historical richness of its era. The presence of this instrument within the Palais connects its audience not only to the annals music but also to a shared European cultural heritage, emphasizing the palace's mission to preserve and celebrate these ancient sounds.

Directions: Continue through Palais Lascaris — next is Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore (Milan, 1696).

Stop 2: Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore (Milan, 1696)

Coordinates: 43.6954, 7.2657

Orientation: To fully appreciate the Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore, crafted in Milan in 1696, stand slightly to the right of the display. This vantage point reveals the exquisite grain of the wood and the original varnish, which has remarkably survived the passage of centuries.

Paolo Antonio Testore, active from around 1700 to 1767, was a master violin maker whose work exemplifies the height of Italian craftsmanship. The Basse de violon in front of you remains in an exceptional state of preservation, allowing us to glimpse the authentic techniques of the late 17th century. Unlike many instruments that have undergone restorations, its original varnish remains largely intact, offering a rare insight into Testore's meticulous artistry. The Testore family, with its roots in Milan, was integral to the evolution of string instrument making during the Baroque era. Paolo Antonio's father, Carlo Giuseppe Testore, founded the family's workshop after learning his craft from Giovanni Grancino. This lineage of expertise ensured that the Testore instruments were not only functional but also works of art in themselves, cherished by musicians and collectors alike. In 2001, this Basse de violon became part of the Palais Lascaris collection, following the transfer of historical musical instruments from the Musee Massena. This transfer was part of a broader project to establish a dedicated music museum, which opened to the public in 2011. Within the museum's wider collection, this instrument stands alongside pieces like the violes gambe by William Turner, which you admired earlier. Each piece contributes to a rich tapestry of European musical history. Consider how each crafted element of the Basse de violon, from its robust body to the elegant scrollwork, was designed to balance aesthetic beauty with acoustic performance. As you continue through the museum, ponder how the innovations and traditions embodied in these instruments influenced French musical developments in subsequent centuries.

Directions: Your final stop in Palais Lascaris: Harpe by Naderman (Paris, 1780).

Stop 3: Harpe by Naderman (Paris, 1780)

Coordinates: 43.6954, 7.2657

Orientation: As you stand before the Harpe by Jean-Henri Naderman, make sure to observe the intricate gilded woodwork and its remarkable size, towering over five feet. This harp is not just a musical instrument but a window into the elegance of the late 18th century—a time of opulence and sophistication in France.

 His donation, driven by his passion for music, included a remarkable collection of instruments. Jean-Henri Naderman, active as a celebrated harp maker in Paris from at least 1735 to 1799, specialized in crafting harps that were both aesthetically beautiful and functionally advanced. The harp is made from painted and gilded wood and metal, reflecting the grandeur of pre-Revolutionary France. The instrument, once belonging to the Viscountess of Beaumont, was more than just a beautiful artifact. It played a part in aristocratic circles, serving not only as an object of musical expression but as a testimony to social status. In December 2008, this same harp was brought to a studio for gold restoration, preserving its historical splendor. This work not only highlighted the ongoing appreciation and care required for historical instruments but also underscored the harp's legacy in musical history.
