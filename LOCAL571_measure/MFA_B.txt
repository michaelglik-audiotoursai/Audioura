The hushed echo of footsteps on polished stone mingles with the soft, diffused light in the Sharf Visitor Gallery—a space devoted to Ancient Nubia Now in the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston. If you pause at the gallery’s threshold, the scale and diversity of the display become instantly clear: monumental stone sculptures and the intricate brilliance of gold jewelry draw your gaze along a carefully orchestrated path through over 400 objects.

Between 1913 and 1916, George A. Conducted excavations in southern Egypt and northern Sudan for the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston. These excavations yielded over 400 objects that became part of the museum’s collection, one of the largest and most significant outside Khartoum. In 2019, the museum's curators presented the Ancient Nubia Now exhibition, showcasing these artifacts that had long been overshadowed by Egyptian works. Moving physically through the exhibition, what unfolds is a timeline of innovation and resilience: colossal statues kings carved from sandstone, luminous pieces of jewelry crafted from gold and semi-precious stones, and Kerma pottery shaped with a precision that tells of advanced ancient techniques. The objects include imports from Greece and Rome, visual evidence that Nubian trade networks once stretched across the Mediterranean and deep into Africa. These physical properties—stone, gold, clay, and imported glass—bear silent witness to Nubia’s reach and ambition. The gallery’s placement within the wider Americas wing also signals the museum’s ongoing commitment to presenting interwoven global cultures, with other spaces named for key supporters like Americas. Nearly every artifact on view in Ancient Nubia Now survived centuries of burial and the fragility of archaeological extraction. This act of preservation was itself at stake—many items might never have come to Boston without Reisner’s intervention and the support of patrons like Sharf Visitor, the supporter of museum exhibitions. The result is a collection that expands the museum’s narrative and makes visible histories that risked erasure. The generosity of past benefactors continues to unlock new perspectives, just as Francis Bartlett’s earlier donations enriched other corners of the collection.

Stop 2: Sons of Liberty Bowl

Address: 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3398, -71.0942

Orientation: Stand directly before the Sons of Liberty Bowl and look closely at the upper rim, where a ring of names is engraved. From this vantage, the light reveals the sharpness of each letter and the clarity of the year—1768.

In 1949, the Sons of Liberty Bowl entered the collection of the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, through the combined efforts of seven hundred donors, many of them Boston schoolchildren, whose contributions brought this historic silver vessel into public stewardship. Crafted in 1768 by the silversmith Paul Revere, the bowl honors ninety-two members of the Massachusetts House of Representatives who refused to rescind a letter sent across the colonies in protest of the Townshend Acts—British taxes on tea, glass, and paper. Revere engraved each legislator’s name himself, creating a visible record of those willing to risk their standing for colonial unity. Sargent authored the text that was illustrated in related documents that help interpret the bowl’s story, making the context and meaning accessible to visitors and scholars. The bowl’s material presence—hammered silver with a broad, plain body and a band of inscription along the rim—testifies to the deliberate restraint of Revere’s technique, which focused attention on the act of naming and commemoration rather than ornate decoration. Its acquisition on December 7, 1948, collection day, was chosen to coincide with the anniversary of Pearl Harbor, adding an additional layer of civic symbolism to its journey into public hands. This bowl’s story runs parallel to what you encountered at the Ancient Nubia Now stop: the Museum’s founding in 1870 and its early role as a guardian of shared memory. The Sons of Liberty Bowl, purchased with the small change of hundreds of schoolchildren, draws a direct line to later collection-building efforts—like those funded by Francis Bartlett and other transformative donors—where collective action shapes the evolution of the museum’s galleries. The spirit of protest and shared civic purpose embodied in Revere’s silver continues to resonate through the physical expansion of these halls, setting a precedent for how modern art and ideas might enter the collection in years to come.

Stop 3: John Singer Sargent’s  The Daughters of Edward Darley Boit

Address: 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3398, -71.0942

Orientation: As you stand here, look directly ahead—John Singer Sargent’s The Daughters of Edward Darley Boit (1882) is hung so that the gaze of the four girls seems almost to drift past you, dissolving into the hush of the gallery. Step back until both oversized, blue-and-white Japanese porcelain vases flank your peripheral vision: from this vantage, the unusual square canvas and the matching vases—once in the Boit family’s Paris apartment—form an axis of symmetry that Sargent surely intended, but that only becomes fully legible in this precise alignment. This is the angle at which Art, a regular gallery patron, once confided to Portrait that the painting seems to breathe with the museum’s shifting light.

The painting of the Boit sisters by Sargent is not a typical family portrait. The girls are depicted in their Paris home, with two in the light and two in the shadows, creating a unique composition. The large canvas size breaks from 19th-century norms, reflecting the room's spaciousness. Edward Darley Boit commissioned the portrait, which critics found psychologically complex due to the unconventional grouping of the children. The painting, along with original Japanese vases, was gifted to the museum in 1919, allowing for a display that merges time and geography. Each acquisition, including the recent addition of Loïs Mailou Jones’s work, builds on a tradition of patronage valued by visitors. The Daughters of Edward Darley Boit painting remains a powerful and thought-provoking piece in the museum's collection.
