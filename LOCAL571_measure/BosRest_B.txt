Stop 1: Union Oyster House

Address: 41 Union St, Boston, MA 02108

Coordinates: 42.3601, -71.0573

Type/Specialty: Seafood

Specific Examples: Fresh oysters, clam chowder, lobster rolls

Orientation: You are about to embark on a walking journey through Boston, MA. Starting at the historic Union Oyster House and ending at The Parker House Restaurant, your route weaves through the heart of Boston's culinary heritage. From the 1826-established Union Oyster House, where history and oysters converge, to The Parker House Restaurant, a family-run gem since 1927, each step reveals a tapestry of tradition and reinvention. At Union Oyster House, opened in 1826, you'll learn about America’s oldest continuously operating restaurant. Cheers Boston, where NBC producers found the Bull & Finch in the early 1980s, is another stop on the tour. Your first stop is Union Oyster House. From Park Street T station, walk towards Tremont St, then turn left onto Tremont St. Continue until you reach Union St, then turn right. The restaurant will be on your left. You might notice the historic architecture of the surrounding buildings as you approach. Stand just outside 41 Union Street, where the brickwork catches the light and the windows tilt at angles set by old Boston streets—a patchwork of red and gray that’s held fast for centuries.

Inside Union Oyster House, the air is thick with steam from the oyster bar and the low murmur of diners—noisy in the way that only a place with two centuries of stories can be. Here’s the first secret: this isn’t just another seafood spot. The Union Oyster House, opened in 1826 as Atwood’s Oyster House, is America’s oldest continuously operating restaurant. That means every shucked oyster and every bowl of chowder comes with a side of real, documentable history—no embellishment needed. The building itself, constructed around 1716, predates the United States. Its angled facade follows the bends of Union and Marshall Streets, a quirk left from the original 18th-century deeds. Let’s share a story over coffee—or, more fittingly, over a bowl of chowder. If you slip inside and scan the booths, you’ll find one marked “The Kennedy Booth.” John F. Kennedy, before and during his presidency, was a regular here. Staff have dedicated his favorite seat to his memory, and that simple plaque means you’re sitting where he once did, eating the same local oysters and New England specialties, perhaps thumbing through the news or planning the next move. It’s a slice of Boston’s political history, concrete and close. Another piece that regulars like to whisper about: since its founding in 1826, Union Oyster House has changed hands only three times. For a business surviving wars, depressions, and changing tastes, that’s nearly miraculous. Joseph A. Milano, Jr. has carried on those traditions since 1970, ensuring the menu and atmosphere remain deeply tied to the city’s roots. The Union Oyster House is open Sunday through Thursday from 11 in the morning until 9 at night, and until 10 on Fridays and Saturdays. Reservations are needed—this isn’t a place for walk-ins on a whim. Expect to spend under $30 for a meal of seafood and New England classics. The clam chowder is a must: it’s thick, creamy, and somehow manages to capture not just the flavor, but the very soul of Boston.


Directions: As you exit Union Oyster House, head west on Union Street. Continue walking until you reach Hanover Street, then turn left and stroll down towards Faneuil Hall Marketplace. Cheers Boston will be on your right, just past the marketplace. Enjoy your walk and maybe grab a drink where everybody knows your name!



Stop 2: Cheers Boston

Orientation: Stand on Beacon Street, just across from the Boston Public Garden, and look for the modest brick building with the familiar black-and-gold sign reading “Cheers.” The entrance sits slightly below street level, tucked beneath the grand Hampshire House, just steps from the bustle of Beacon Hill. Pause at the wrought-iron railing and take in the busy hum drifting out the door—the pulse of lunchtime laughter, the clink of pint glasses, and the savory tang of classic pub fare rising into the air.

Here at 84 Beacon Street, the story begins in 1969 when Thomas Kershaw and his partner found a new purpose for the basement of Hampshire House, which had been little more than storage. They opened the Bull & carving out a cozy, wood-paneled refuge for locals and newcomers alike. For more than a decade, it was a neighborhood fixture—until an unexpected twist changed its fate. In the early 1980s, NBC producers, searching for the perfect bar exterior for a new sitcom, spotted the Bull & Finch. Its brick-and-glass entry—modest, inviting, unmistakably Boston—became the face of “Cheers,” the show that would run from 1982 to 1993 and turn this tucked-away pub into a landmark recognized on screens around the world. The bar’s transformation didn’t end there. In 2002, after closing a deal with CBS Studios—successors to the show original producers— changed the pub name officially to Cheers. Today, visitors from across the globe pass through these doors, drawn by the promise of community—where, as the show’s theme insisted, everybody knows your name. Inside, the bar serves American classics: hearty sandwiches, burgers, small plates, and a wide selection of beers and cocktails. The camaraderie at the tables is as thick as the scent of sizzling fries. Cheers Boston welcomes guests every day of the week from 11:00 in the morning until 10:00 at night, with the kitchen closing an hour earlier. No booking is needed—just the luck of finding a seat, and the comfort of joining in a ritual that has become a part of Boston’s living pulse. The Union Oyster House you stopped at a moment ago was all about the taste of Boston’s past. Here, the flavor is memory itself—served fresh, with a side of laughter and the echo of a television jingle. The next aroma will transport you to a place where literary legends dined.


Directions: As you leave Cheers Boston, stroll down Beacon Street and continue towards Tremont Street. Walk past the Boston Common and take a left onto School Street. The Parker House Restaurant will be on your right, just a short walk away.



Stop 3: The Parker House Restaurant

Orientation: You’ve already felt the wood-paneled comfort of Cheers Boston, but this stop is where the city’s culinary history gets sly, layered, and just a little mischievous.

Step in and catch the scent—there’s butter, of course, from rolls that have outlived generations, and a whiff of vanilla from the dessert that made this place famous. The Parker House Restaurant, founded in 1855 and rebuilt into its current form in 1927, is a registered historic landmark—listed on the National Register of Historic Places and a member of Historic Hotels of America. The marble underfoot could tell you about the politicians who schemed here, but today, it’s the kitchen that does most of the talking. In 1856, Harvey D. Parker hired a French chef named Augustine Francois. Layers of cake, chocolate, and custard were combined in a creative improvisation that became the Boston Cream Pie—a cake, not a pie, and the name stuck. Now, it's the official dessert of Massachusetts, the official state dessert, the Boston cream pie, all starting because one Frenchman couldn’t resist making something new with what he had. But let me share a secret that’s rarely whispered over the mahogany tables: In the 1910s, a young man named Ho Chi Minh—yes, the same Ho Chi Minh who would later become the leader—worked in the Parker House kitchen. He washed dishes and learned the rhythms of a Boston hotel, long before history would write his name in textbooks. The kitchen where he worked still turns out Parker House rolls, soft and golden, the same ones that once caught Eleanor Roosevelt’s notice when she dined here with Franklin. The menu leans classic American—Boston Cream Pie, those famous rolls, and comforting standards. The restaurant opens its doors from 7:00 each morning, so whether you want breakfast or something more decadent, the timing’s on your side. They don’t post a required booking or price at the door, but the real draw is the taste of history itself—layered, unexpected, and just a little bit subversive, the way Boston likes it.

That's 3 stops — The Parker House Restaurant, birthplace of the Boston Cream Pie and Union Oyster House, America’s oldest continuously operating restaurant since 1826.
