Stop 1: Violes gambe by William Turner (Londres, 1652)

Orientation: You are about to explore the Palais Lascaris in Nice, home to a remarkable collection of musical instruments. The three stops on this tour include the Violes gambe by William Turner, the Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore, and the Harpe by Naderman. These instruments showcase the evolution of European musical culture through the ages. At this work, you'll find the Violes gambe by William Turner (Londres, 1652), showcasing English craftsmanship. Moving on to this work, the Harpe by Naderman (Paris, 1780) stands as a material witness to the opulence of the French court. Your first stop is Violes gambe by William Turner (Londres, 1652). A hush hangs in the air as you step close to the vitrine, where the warm, honeyed wood of the viola da gamba by William Turner (Londres, 1652) quietly catches the filtered light from the Palais Lascaris’s tall windows. Stand to one side to glimpse the elegant curve of its body and the delicate outline of the F-holes—details that reveal both the hand of its maker and the purpose of its voice.

 Gautier assembled this collection over decades, pursuing rare and historic pieces that reflected his lifelong devotion to music. By donating his instruments, Gautier ensured that the viol by William Turner, made in London in 1652, became part of the Palais Lascaris, forever connecting English craftsmanship with the grandeur of a seventeenth-century French palace. Turner, primarily known as a painter, was also active as a maker of viols with labels dating from 1647 to 1656. During those years in Aldgate, London, Turner produced several fine viols, and his instruments became recognized for their characteristic heart-shaped sound holes—an English signature from that era. This viol, carefully constructed in 1652, belongs to the rare body of his surviving work, and its presence here is a physical record of that brief but productive chapter in Turner’s life as a luthier. The viol itself is a treble model, its slender body and gently arched back showing the handwork of a mid-seventeenth-century English workshop. The F-holes, carved with precision, are not merely decorative: their shape and placement guide the resonance of each note, producing the rich, melancholic tone that English composers cherished during a period of political and social upheaval. Within the context of the Palais Lascaris’s collection—France’s second most significant trove of musical instruments after Paris’s Musée de la Musique—Turner’s viol stands as a bridge between English and continental tastes. When Gautier’s bequest entered this former aristocratic residence, the viol by Turner became more than an English relic; it joined a living ensemble of instruments that are still played in the museum’s salons. Here, the deeply introspective sound Turner crafted in 1652 meets the vibrant spirit of Italian and French music, opening new dialogues each time a bow is drawn across its strings.

Stop 2: Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore (Milan, 1696)

Coordinates: 43.6951, 7.2652

Orientation: Stand before the Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore (Milan, 1696), and allow your gaze to follow the sweep of its body, where the light of this chamber catches the gentle arching. From just left of center, the deep curves and broad shoulders become unmistakable, letting you compare its Italian form with the English violes gambe by William Turner you have already seen.

In 1696, Testore crafted this Basse de violon in Milan, establishing a lineage that would carry the prestige of the Testore name into the heart of Italian lutherie. Decades before, Carlo Giuseppe Testore founded the family workshop after departing the studio of Grancino, securing a new center of instrument making in Milan and laying the groundwork for his sons’ careers. In the early 2000s, the city of Nice transferred its historical musical instrument collections—including this instrument—from the Musée Masséna to the Palais Lascaris, turning a once-playing bass viol into a cornerstone of the museum’s music collection and shifting its daily fate from performance to preservation. The instrument itself exudes scale and gravity. Its wide bouts and robust ribs set it apart within this room, signaling the Milanese focus on resonance and projection—physical qualities that mattered not just for sound but for the role such an instrument played at the foundation of ensembles. Paolo Antonio Testore’s hand is legible here: his blend of invention and reliable strength, favored in the Testore workshop, gave performers a tool for both clarity and power. That practical, expressive intent shaped how Italian music resounded across Europe at the turn of the eighteenth century. Context ripples outward from the object. Directly beside the violes gambe by Turner you saw earlier, this Basse de violon stands as a counterweight—an emblem of Milan’s assertive musical identity during years of Italian influence. Even as the Palais Lascaris itself was altered in the eighteenth century to suit changing needs, instruments like this bear witness to a moment of transformation, when the flair of Italian craftsmanship reached outward toward France and beyond.

Stop 3: Harpe by Naderman (Paris, 1780)

Coordinates: 43.6951, 7.2652

Orientation: Position yourself so you can see the side profile—this reveals the single-action pedal mechanism at its base, a key technical feature of harps from this era. From here, the delicate gilding becomes legible, and the craftsmanship that set this instrument apart in late eighteenth-century Paris is unmistakable.

In 1780, Jean-Henri Naderman crafted this single-action pedal harp in Paris. The harp’s journey did not end there. By 2011, the permanent exhibition was opened to the public. This decision meant that the Naderman harp, once reserved for the salons of nobility, now stood alongside pieces like the basse de violon you stopped at earlier, forming a narrative of musical innovation and taste spanning centuries. The harp’s painted and gilded wood is not just for show; in the France of 1780, such decoration signaled not only technical mastery but also aristocratic status. The single-action pedal mechanism allowed musicians to change the pitch of each string by a semitone, a technical marvel before the advent of the double-action harp. The cost of producing such an elaborate instrument—both in material and craftsmanship—meant it was an object of privilege, accessible only to the upper echelons of society. The harp’s presence here is a quiet echo of that world, preserved through the ambitions and choices of collectors and curators. The fate of such a majestic instrument, once the pride of a private salon, is now entwined with the evolving story of this museum.
