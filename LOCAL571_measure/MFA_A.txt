Your first stop is Ancient Nubia Now. Stand at the entrance of the Ancient Nubia Now exhibit at the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, where the gallery is named for patrons like Americas and Pace, who played crucial roles in bringing this momentous collection to life. From this spot, the expanse of over 400 artifacts becomes apparent, offering a vivid tapestry of Nubian history that challenges historical narratives.

The Museum of Fine Arts, Boston organized the exhibition "Ancient Nubia Now" from October 13, 2019, to January 20, 2020, with the ambition of reshaping public perception about Nubian history. The exhibition featured over 400 objects, illustrating the richness of Nubian civilization through items like ornate jewelry and monumental sculptures. Notably, this exhibition was driven by a commitment to tell a more accurate story, countering the overlooked narratives of Nubia's influence and achievements. The gallery itself pays homage to its patrons. Americas, a key benefactor, supported the acquisition and display of many Nubian artifacts. Their contributions have facilitated a deeper understanding and appreciation of ancient Nubian culture, resonating within the halls they helped fill with history. By exploring the exhibition, visitors encounter artifacts like towering royal statues and delicate Kerma pottery. Each object, carefully curated, is part of a broader narrative that underscores Nubia's pivotal role in ancient trade networks that extended from the Mediterranean into central Africa.

Stop 2: Sons of Liberty Bowl

Address: 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3392, -71.0942

Orientation: As you stand before the Sons of Liberty Bowl, crafted in 1768 by the illustrious silversmith Paul Revere, position yourself where the light catches the bowl’s gleaming surface. This vantage point not only reveals the exquisite craftsmanship but also connects you to a pivotal moment in American history—the defiance of British rule by a group of Massachusetts rebels.

In 1768, the Sons of Liberty, a group of Massachusetts rebels, commissioned this silver bowl from Paul Revere, Jr. This act was a bold statement of defiance against British rule, as the bowl was meant to honor the group's efforts toward American independence. Over time, the bowl became an iconic piece of American protest art, symbolizing the spirit of rebellion that would eventually lead to the Revolution. In 1949, during the tense period of the Cold War, the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, acquired the Sons of Liberty Bowl. This acquisition at such a pivotal moment in history underscored the museum's commitment to preserving artifacts that embody the fight for freedom and democracy. Today, the bowl holds a place of honor at the museum, connecting the revolutionary spirit of the 18th century with the ongoing struggle for liberty and justice. The polished surface reflects the light, much like the hope it represented during its creation. Paul Revere’s choice of silver was deliberate, symbolizing purity and permanence, qualities that aligned with the Sons of Liberty's ideals. The detailed engraving on the bowl commemorates ninety-two members of the Massachusetts House of Representatives who courageously refused to rescind a letter protesting the Townshend Acts, which imposed taxes on essential goods like tea and paper. The acquisition of this bowl by the museum was made possible through the collective efforts of the community, including seven hundred donations from Boston schoolchildren and the public. This act of community involvement not only highlights the historical significance of the bowl but also reflects the museum's broader mission to engage and involve the public in its collection. This legacy of strategic funding, reminiscent of the Francis Bartlett Donation which facilitated the acquisition of other significant works like the bronze statue of Mantiklos 'Apollo', continues to enrich the museum's collection. As you ponder the significance of this bowl, consider how such community-driven efforts have paved the way for the museum's ongoing mission to preserve and present art that tells diverse and meaningful stories.

Stop 3: John Singer Sargent’s  The Daughters of Edward Darley Boit

Address: 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3392, -71.0942

Orientation: ** As you stand before John Singer Sargent’s "The Daughters of Edward Darley Boit," position yourself slightly to the left of the frame. This angle allows you to fully appreciate the interplay of shadow and light that defines the room's depth, a technique Sargent mastered to imbue the piece with a haunting realism.

In 1919, the Museum of Fine Arts in Boston acquired this painting, forever altering its collection with Sargent's evocative portrayal of Edward Darley Boit's daughters. The painting has since become a staple of the museum's offerings, illustrating a pivotal moment in art history when American artists began to make their mark on the global stage. Painted in 1882, the work features four young girls, captured in a Parisian apartment. Their arrangement within the composition is anything but traditional. Two of the sisters stand in shadow, their features obscured, while the others are bathed in soft light, emphasizing their differing personalities and the sense of mystery surrounding them. Sargent’s use of light and shadow, along with his choice to depict the girls in such an informal setting, was a bold departure from the formal portraiture of the time. This decision highlights Sargent’s innovative approach, which has influenced generations of portrait artists. Interestingly, the room housing this artwork is part of the Linde Family Wing for Contemporary Art, named after gallery patron Linde. By housing Sargent's work here, the museum creates a dialogue between the past and the present, showcasing how historical pieces continue to resonate with contemporary audiences. The painting’s acquisition underscores the museum's commitment to preserving significant works of art, a mission that echoes the sentiments of patrons like Francis Bartlett, whose donations have funded acquisitions seen earlier in the tour, such as the two-handled jar. These stories of acquisition and patronage illustrate the ongoing evolution of the museum's collection, shaping its identity and offerings for future generations.
