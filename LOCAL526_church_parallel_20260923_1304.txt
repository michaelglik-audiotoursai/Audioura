Orientation: As you approach Sacred Heart Parish, the sturdy brick facade stands as a testament to its enduring presence since its establishment in 1922 by the Stigmatine Fathers.

In the early 20th century, Sacred Heart Parish became a beacon of faith for the waves of Irish immigrants who arrived seeking solace and community. They poured their labor and love into building a magnificent church that reflected their resilience and devotion. Later, Italian immigrants enriched the parish with their customs and traditions, adding layers of cultural vibrancy to the spiritual life here. The year 1958 marked a notable moment in the parish's narrative. Father Stephen Dalla Via, then pastor of Sacred Heart, sought the help of Aldo A to assist with church matters. This collaboration underscored the close-knit nature of the parish, where mutual support and shared responsibilities were integral to its functioning. Such episodes encapsulate the essence of Sacred Heart Parish as a place where the community came together in faith and action. The devotion to the Sacred Heart of Jesus, a cornerstone of Catholic spirituality, is palpable here. This particular devotion, which views the heart of Jesus Christ as a symbol of God's boundless and passionate love for mankind, has roots tracing back to medieval Catholic mysticism. It is celebrated liturgically in the Latin Church on the third Friday after Pentecost, reflecting the deep spiritual traditions that continue to thrive within these walls.

Stop 3: Newton Cemetery

Coordinates: 42.3242, -71.1917

Orientation: Position yourself to best view this location.

Here, amid the winding paths and serene vistas of this garden-style cemetery, lies James F.C. Hyde. In 1855, when Newton Cemetery was organized, Hyde became the first of Newton's founding families to choose this new space over the traditional East Parish Burying Ground. His decision marked a pivotal shift, as the city began to embrace this modern resting place for its revered citizens. Hyde’s choice signaled a moment of transition for Newton, one that embraced a future of growth and community. Further along these paths, you might pause at the tombstone of Herman James "Jim" Shea Jr., a name that echoes with the activism and progressive ideals of the 1960s. Born in 1939, Shea was a lifelong resident of Newton and a Tufts University graduate. Although he briefly attended the University of Virginia School of Law, his true calling lay in serving his community. As a civil engineer, real estate broker, and university instructor, Shea wore many hats, but it was his political career that left a lasting imprint. As a freshman legislator elected to the Massachusetts House of Representatives in 1968, Shea was an active voice against the Vietnam War and a staunch supporter of the modern environmental movement. His work on the Newton Board of Aldermen and later in the state legislature reflected a deep commitment to progressive change. Newton Cemetery is more than a collection of gravestones; it’s a narrative of lives intertwined with the city's evolution. Among these silent markers, the stories of those like Hyde and Shea are an enduring testament to the civic spirit and historical layers that make Newton what it is today.

Stop 4: Our Lady Help of Christians Catholic Church

Orientation: As you arrive at Our Lady Help of Christians Catholic Church, the towering spire greets you, a testament to the church's historical and architectural significance. The intricate stained glass windows catch the morning light, casting colored patterns on the pathway.

Our Lady Help of Christians Catholic Church in Newton, Massachusetts, stands not merely as a physical structure but as a beacon of faith and community life since its foundation in 1873. This church, deeply rooted in the traditions brought by Irish immigrants, continues to serve as a spiritual anchor for the neighborhood. Its past intertwines with significant events, one of which was a visit from Mother Teresa of Calcutta on June 15, 1988. Her presence here was not just a ceremonial gesture; it was a moment of spiritual encouragement, reflecting her lifelong commitment to serving the marginalized. During her visit, she addressed the congregation, inspiring many to deepen their involvement in community service, a legacy still honored by parishioners today. The church's story is further enriched by the cultural tapestry woven by Italian immigrants who joined the parish community, bringing their customs and religious traditions. This blend of customs is evident in the church's celebrations and rituals, where Italian feasts and Irish hymns find their place. The church's design itself marries old and new, symbolizing this fusion of traditions. The architecture, with its Gothic Revival style, features pointed arches and ribbed vaults, echoing the timelessness of its mission. In the broader context of the area's Catholic history, this church represents the growth and resilience of a community that has weathered many changes. Just as you observed at the Newton Cemetery, where the passage of life is marked by somber graves, this church marks the passage of time with lively gatherings and celebrations, ensuring that both the past and the present are honored.
