Stop 1: War Remnants Museum

Address:

Coordinates: 10.7769, 106.6934

Type/Specialty: Museum

Specific Examples: Exhibits on the Vietnam War, military vehicles, and photography collections.

Orientation: As you traverse this urban landscape, you will encounter significant historical sites and modern architecture, where the remnants of a tumultuous past coexist with thriving commercial activity, reflecting the city’s journey through survival and renewal. Your first stop is War Remnants Museum. From the nearest main street, Võ Văn Tần, walk south towards the museum entrance. The shade of tamarind trees along Võ Văn Tần Street offers some respite as the heavy air carries the faint tang of rust and oil drifting from the museum’s outdoor courtyard. Here, battered tanks and helicopters stand silent, their chipped metal surfaces tracing the outline of decades-old scars.

Step beyond the gates of the War Remnants Museum and the city’s cacophony drops away, replaced by the muffled shuffling of feet on tiled floors and the low murmur of voices reading placards. Established in 1975, the museum stands as a witness to the Vietnam War, repurposing the former USIS offices at this address into a site that catalogs the impact of conflict through photographs and objects. In September of that year, the new government opened these doors to the public, calling the institution the ‘Exhibition House for US and Puppet Crimes.’ This was more than an administrative act. Months after the war’s end, Vietnamese citizens—many still in mourning, some returning for the first time since the fighting stopped—entered these rooms to confront the visible evidence of occupation and resistance. The choice of name declared purpose and perspective, and the building’s halls bore witness as survivors, families, and onlookers searched for their own faces or stories in the images on the walls.

Years later, another chapter unfolded. In 1990, the museum changed its name to the ‘Exhibition House for Crimes of War and Aggression,’ reflecting a shift in tone as Vietnam emerged from decades of isolation. By 1995, after President Bill Clinton lifted the United States trade embargo and diplomatic relations were normalized, the institution became the ‘War Remnants Museum.’ Each renaming echoed the evolving rhythms of Vietnamese society: from revolutionary certainty to reconciliation and redefinition.

Through these changes, the ordinary flows of life—schoolchildren on field trips, elders tracing the names of lost relatives, international visitors listening for echoes of their own histories—have continued to pulse through these halls. Just beyond the entrance, the courtyard’s rusting armor and warplanes now serve as a silent gathering place. Here, the daily rituals of commemoration and learning persist, woven into the pattern of Ho Chi Minh City’s ever-shifting day.

Directions: From the War Remnants Museum, stroll down Vo Van Tan Street, taking in the sights of the vibrant local life around you. Just keep an eye out for the grand entrance, which is hard to miss! Continue to Independence Palace

In 1962, the old presidential palace lay in ruins behind its battered walls. President Ngô Đình Diệm, who governed the Republic of Vietnam, ordered the construction of a new seat of power. The architect, Ngô Viết Thụ, was chosen to realize this vision, and the building that rose here became the president’s home and workplace. Its walls would soon witness the final chapters of a divided nation. On February 27, 1962, two South Vietnamese Air Force pilots, Nguyễn Văn Cử and Phạm Phú Quốc, turned their planes against their own government. They dropped napalm and bombs onto the palace, targeting President Ngô Đình Diệm. Amid the chaos, Madame Nhu, Diệm’s sister-in-law, fell through a hole blasted open in the floor by the explosions. She survived, suffering burns and cuts, but the attack left the old palace so damaged that it was abandoned. This act of defiance not only scarred the building but set in motion the creation of the very structure you see before you. With the air still heavy with tension, another act of insurgency marked these grounds. On April 8, 1975, Nguyễn Thành Trung, a Viet Cong spy who had infiltrated the South Vietnamese Air Force, piloted a captured F-5E fighter jet over the palace. He dropped bombs on the roof, this time aiming at President Nguyễn Văn Thiệu. The palace again shook with the force of civil conflict, the impact echoing far beyond its walls. These events—each with their named actors and violent consequences—are woven into the marble floors and the wide, open halls, where the sound of footfalls mingles with the memory of sirens and shouted orders. The palace stands as both a monument and a scar, its very existence a ledger of the ambitions and failures of those who once ruled here.
Directions: From Independence Palace, stroll down Nam Ky Khoi Nghia Street, taking in the vibrant sights of the city. As you walk, keep an eye out for the beautiful Saigon Notre-Dame Basilica on your left. Continue straight until you reach the bustling Ben Thanh Market, where the lively atmosphere and colorful stalls await you!

Stop 3: Ben Thanh Market

Address:

Coordinates: 10.7761, 106.6953

Type/Specialty: Market

Specific Examples: Local crafts, street food stalls, and fresh produce.

Orientation: The crowds swell and break like tides against the old entrance, and the clocktower rises overhead—a fixed point in the restless movement of District 1.

Step inside, and the first thing that strikes is scale: Ben Thanh Market draws more than 10,000 visitors each day, each one weaving between nearly 1,500 booths and over 6,000 small businesses. These numbers are not mere statistics—they reflect a living architecture of commerce, where stalls are stacked with rice, fabric, lanterns, and lacquerware. The foundation stones beneath your feet have seen fire and reinvention. Between 1912 and 1914, the French colonial administration rebuilt Ben Thanh Market using iron-frame construction, replacing wood with iron. That change allowed the market to withstand the heat, bustle, and rain of Saigon for the next century, where once a stray ember could erase a city block.

Look up at the walls: in 1952, local architect Lê Văn Mậu oversaw the installation of ceramic relief sculptures as part of a nationalist redesign. These are not just decoration—they are a deliberate gesture, placing Vietnamese motifs at the heart of a colonial structure. The cool surface of each ceramic tile holds the fingerprints of artisans and the vision of Lê Văn Mậu, who wanted a public space that could speak in the local tongue even as it echoed with foreign footsteps.

The market’s history is not only in stone and tile but also in the lives shaped here. On August 25, 1963, Quách Thị Trang, a 15-year-old Buddhist student, was killed during a protest in front of this market. The event brought international attention to the repressive measures of President Ngô Đình Diệm’s regime, and her name became known beyond the city.

Ben Thanh Market is a living index of the city’s collisions and continuities. Its iron bones, nationalist ceramics, and echoing shouts link the colonial ambitions you glimpsed at Independence Palace to the present-day energy of Saigon. As you leave the market, the lively buzz of Nguyen Hue Walking Street awaits, carrying the city’s stories forward.

Directions: From Ben Thanh Market, stroll towards Le Loi Street, where you’ll see the vibrant shops and street vendors along the way. Continue walking straight until you reach the intersection with Nguyen Hue Walking Street, where you can enjoy the lively atmosphere and beautiful views of the area. Don't forget to take in the stunning Saigon Opera House as you approach! It is about a minute's walk away.

Stop 4: Nguyen Hue Walking Street

Address:

Coordinates: 10.7765, 106.6958

Type/Specialty: Public Square

Specific Examples: Fountains, street performances, and cafes.

Orientation: Nguyen Hue Walking Street is where the smooth granite underfoot divides the wide pedestrian promenade from the lanes of traffic on either side.

This stretch of open pavement, alive with evening strollers and the smells of roasted corn and iced coffee, was once something entirely different. French engineers decided in 1887 to fill it in, paving over centuries of muddy commerce to create the boulevard now known as Nguyễn Huệ. The city’s pulse shifted upward from canal boats to café terraces, and the transformation left a mark that is still visible in the grand trees and stately buildings along the route. Nguyen Hue has always been a stage for public life, but in the 1950s, that stage turned deadly when Chavigny de Lachevrotière’s assassination sent ripples through Saigon’s foreign community—a stark reminder that beneath the routines of café gatherings and market strolls, political tensions simmered close to the surface. In the 1960s and 1970s, as the Vietnam War intensified, the apartment block at 42 Nguyễn Huệ housed top American advisors, CIA operatives, and high-ranking military officials. Today, the entire street thrums with a different energy. Beneath the paving stones, an underground mall signals Saigon’s rapid modernization; at street level, families pose for photos and food vendors call out in quick Vietnamese.

Directions: As you stroll down the street, keep an eye out for the impressive Saigon Opera House on your left. Continue walking straight, and soon you'll see the towering Bitexco Financial Tower rising ahead of you, a striking feature of the skyline. It is roughly 400 meters away.

Stop 5: Bitexco Financial Tower

Address:

Coordinates: 10.7728, 106.6955

Type/Specialty: Skyscraper

Specific Examples: Observation deck views, shopping center, and restaurants.

Orientation: The best vantage is across the busy traffic circle, where the full silhouette of the tower can be seen against the shifting sky.

Local children say the building looks like a spaceship that landed overnight, or a lotus bud pausing before it opens. But this is no fable: Bitexco Financial Tower was completed in 2010, and for a brief moment, it stood as the tallest building in Vietnam. With 68 floors above ground and three basements, its full height measures 262.5 metres—an exact number that once crowned Saigon’s ambitions before the title passed north to Keangnam Hanoi Landmark Tower in 2011.

The architect, Carlos Zapata, shaped the tower to echo Vietnam's national flower, the unfurling lotus. That motif—deliberate and unmistakable—is embodied in the curving, petal-like wings and, most spectacularly, in the helipad that juts out from the structure’s side. Yet the helipad, despite its prominence, is rarely touched by helicopter skids. Vietnamese military airspace restrictions, combined with the fierce down-draft caused by the tower’s glass arc, have kept the platform almost untouched by flight. Instead, the helipad has served as a VIP champagne lounge during fashion shows, as the finish line for the Ho Chi Minh City Skyrun, and as a set for photographers chasing the city’s highest vantage.

Inside, the city’s fortunes have shifted as quickly as its skyline. For a time, the mall stood nearly empty—a “ghost mall” beneath the city’s most recognizable crown. The centuries seem to converge in the glass: old ambition reflected in new light, the city’s story continuing upward and outward, as you prepare to leave its shadow for the next chapter.

This tour reveals the enduring impact of conflict and transformation on the fabric of a city, illustrating how history shapes public spaces and collective memory. As you reflect on these narratives, consider how the echoes of the past continue to resonate in the vibrant life of the present. That's 5 stops in all.
