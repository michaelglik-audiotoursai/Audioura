Stop 1: Cours Saleya Market

Address: Cours Saleya, 06300 Nice, France

Coordinates: 43.6953, 7.2655

Type/Specialty: Market

Specific Examples: Fresh produce, local flowers, artisanal goods

Orientation: You are about to embark on a walking journey through Nice, France. Your first stop is Cours Saleya Market. From Nice Ville train station, head south on Avenue Thiers, turn left onto Avenue Jean Médecin, then right onto Cours Saleya. You'll be greeted by the vibrant colors and sounds of the market. Stand beneath the striped market awnings, their fabric casting a shifting shadow over the worn stone underfoot. The air here is thick with the fragrance of fresh-cut flowers and the low hum of vendors calling out to passersby. Look toward the center of the square: the stone fountain’s surface glistens, worn smooth by centuries of hands and water.

Cours Saleya Market, pressed between the Mediterranean’s blue and the pastel facades at the foot of the French Alps, is not simply a place of commerce—it’s the living pulse of Nice’s Old Town. The market’s story is shaped by rhythms and rituals that have persisted for generations. Step closer to the fountain, and you’ll notice the constant movement: hands exchanging coins, baskets brimming with local produce, and the soundscape of voices in French, Niçard, and the accents of travelers.

One episode, still retold among local families, begins in 1897, when Nice opened the first wholesale cut flower market in the world. Growers from the hills surrounding Nice would rise before dawn, loading their carts with blooms—roses, mimosa, and lilies—then descend into Cours Saleya. By early morning, the square was transformed: stems sorted by color and scent, buyers negotiating directly with producers. This practice turned the market into a daily ritual and a point of pride for the city. The flower market’s reputation soon spread throughout Europe, and the tradition of local growers bringing their harvest to this square remains visible today in the bursts of color on every stall.

Another layer of the market’s identity unfolded between 1921 and 1938, when Henri Matisse lived at 1 Place Charles Félix, just at the eastern end of Cours Saleya. Matisse, a leading figure in modern art, often painted the view from his window, capturing the movement of umbrellas, the flicker of sunlight on stone, and the market’s ever-changing crowd. The connection between the daily rhythms of the market and the artistic currents of Nice is reflected in his work.

Cours Saleya’s daily market continues to draw both locals and visitors, echoing Nice’s growth from a Greek settlement called Nikaia around 350 BC to a thriving Riviera city of nearly one million people. The footsteps that circle the market’s fountain today carry forward the traditions, stories, and exchanges that have defined this square for generations.

Directions: From Place Masséna, head east on Rue Masséna, turn right onto Rue de la Préfecture, then left onto Rue Rossetti. You might catch the sweet scent of nearby pastries wafting through the streets. Continue to Nice Cathedral (Cathédrale Sainte-Réparate de Nice) — it is roughly 750 meters away.

Stop 2: Nice Cathedral (Cathédrale Sainte-Réparate de Nice)

Address: Place Rossetti, 06300 Nice, France

Coordinates: 43.6961, 7.2750

Type/Specialty: Cathedral

Specific Examples: Baroque architecture, beautiful frescoes, serene atmosphere

Orientation: Standing across the checkerboard pavement of Place Rossetti, the façade of Nice Cathedral rises before you, its Baroque lines catching the Mediterranean light. The square’s chatter and echoes drift around you, mingling with the scent of citrus and stone.

The cathedral’s story begins in 1650, when construction of this main body commenced and continued for decades, culminating in its consecration in 1699. The building stands as a significant example of 17th-century architecture. Here, the drama of the style comes alive: the façade, completed in 1830, is adorned with a statue of Saint Reparata, a recognized Christian martyr and patron saint, at its center, flanked by Syagre, Bassus, Pontius, and Valérien—saints revered in Nice. The bell-tower, added in 1757, marks time above the square, its chimes blending into daily life. The story goes that Reparata was a young woman martyred in Caesarea, Palestine, during the persecutions of Emperor Decius, known for persecuting Christians, around 250 AD. According to tradition, after her death, angels carried her relics across the Mediterranean and brought them to Nice. Her likeness presides over the entrance, symbolizing the city's spiritual identity as it grows and shifts. Inside, the cathedral’s ten chapels shimmer with artwork, but its presence is just as much about what has happened outside its walls. In 2010, a French court ruled to hand over ownership of Nice Cathedral to Russia, a decision that drew attention to the building’s longstanding ties to the Russian community in the region. Ahead, the cathedral’s spires point skyward, marking a place where faith and fortitude have shaped the city’s story.

Directions: As you continue, keep an eye out for the colorful shops and cafés lining the street. Soon, you’ll reach the stunning Palais Lascaris on your right, with its beautiful façade waiting to be explored. It is about a minute's walk away.

Stop 3: Palais Lascaris

Address: 15 Rue Droite, 06300 Nice, France

Coordinates: 43.6964, 7.2762

Type/Specialty: Historic Palace

Specific Examples: Baroque interiors, musical instrument collection, historical exhibits

Orientation: As you stand here on rue Droite, the stone façade of Palais Lascaris rises before you—its worn steps and carved portal facing the street’s daily thrum. Pause across from the entrance: this is the best vantage to see the monumental staircase just inside, its painted ceilings glimpsed between comings and goings.

Step past the threshold and enter the world of the seventeenth century, when the Lascaris-Vintimille family, named by Charles Emmanuel II, Duke of Savoy, as the “principalissima” among Nice’s nobility, made this their home. The palace was built for them in the middle of that century, anchoring their prestige in stone and plaster. For generations, the rhythms of aristocratic life played out in these rooms—receiving visitors, marking feast days, and, in quieter corners, negotiating marriages and alliances that would shape the Old Town’s future. Their tenure ended with the upheaval of the Revolution, and in 1802, the house passed from their hands. The walls still bear the layered traces of those years: baroque frescoes, marble floors, and the echo of footsteps once laden with ceremony.

By the early twentieth century, the palace had suffered neglect. In 1942, the city of Nice purchased it, a decision born of local resolve to preserve what remained of the Old Town’s civil grandeur. Restorers soon found themselves contending with more than simple disrepair. In 1946, Palais Lascaris was officially classified as a Monument Historique, which required the painstaking conservation of its carved balustrades and painted vaults.

Just beyond the cathedral you’ve already seen, this hidden palace reveals a softer side of history: a place where aristocratic ceremony met the evolving rhythms of community life.

Directions: From Palais Lascaris, head south on Rue Droite, turn left onto Rue de la Préfecture, then follow the signs to the stairs leading up to Castle Hill. The ascent offers stunning views of the city below. Continue to Castle Hill (Colline du Château) — it is roughly 900 meters away.

Stop 4: Castle Hill (Colline du Château)

Address: Promenade des Anglais, 06300 Nice, France

Coordinates: 43.6950, 7.2650

Type/Specialty: Scenic Park

Specific Examples: Panoramic views, waterfall, historical ruins

Orientation: Pause where the fragmentary stone walls catch the sun and the wind carries the faint brine of the Baie des Anges; this is the vantage the ancient founders chose, and where later generations built, fought, and finally, opened the land to all.

Stand here and the transformation is stark. Today’s Castle Hill is a public park, crowned with trees and the calls of swifts, but for centuries it bristled with fortifications. The stones underfoot and the remnants of walls around you are what remains of the military citadel that once dominated Nice, standing guard from the 11th century into the 18th.

Centuries ago, the hill was already a stage for siege and resistance. In 1543, Castle Hill became the focus of the Siege of Nice, when Franco-Ottoman forces pressed hard against the Duchy of Savoy. The defenders held out atop these ramparts, with the city below battered and burning. The siege is remembered not only for its violence, but for the endurance of the people who survived beneath the shadow of these walls.

One episode stands out in the hill’s long tenure as Nice’s shield: in 1706, King Louis XIV ordered the fortress destroyed. His army arrived and systematically dismantled the defenses, erasing the stronghold that had withstood so many threats from the skyline. The city’s defenses were lost, and the shape of Nice changed permanently.

The hill’s purpose shifted again in the 19th century. By 1828, what had been a military site was recast as the first public garden in Nice, a deliberate gesture that opened these heights to the townspeople and, soon, to travelers from afar. This act marked the beginning of Nice’s transformation into a destination for visitors, a pattern you have already glimpsed in the market and cathedral you passed earlier.

Here, stone and sky, ruin and garden: the weight of centuries is visible in every view. Each step forward brings another perspective—of conquest, of loss, and of the city’s persistent renewal.

Directions: From Castle Hill, stroll down the path towards the Old Town, enjoying the stunning views of Nice as you go. As you reach the bottom, head toward Rue Rossetti, where you'll find the charming cathedral, Cathédrale Sainte-Réparate. Continue to Place Rossetti — it is about a minute's walk away.

Stop 5: Place Rossetti

Coordinates: 43.6952, 7.2653

Orientation: The best view opens up from here: the facades of ochre and pale pink enclosing the square, the domed roof of the cathedral you stepped inside earlier rising on one side, and the tangle of narrow streets radiating outward.

“Ice cream in the square tastes different,” locals sometimes say, and they mean this spot. For decades, the Fenocchio family—whose gelateria now sits on the north edge of Place Rossetti—has been a reference point in Nice. When the shop first opened in 1966, the founder, Jean-Baptiste Fenocchio, would greet children and grandparents alike, offering dozens of flavors from rose to olive to violet. He insisted on open doors and tables spilling onto the flagstones, so that the aromas of fruit and cream would blend with the salty air drifting up from the sea. Today, the chatter at Fenocchio’s tables mixes with the sound of footsteps on stone, echoing the rhythms that shape daily life in Old Town.

The square itself took its present form in the 19th century, when city authorities cleared a cluster of medieval houses to open up space around the cathedral. Each surface here tells of that layering. The worn stone benches are cool in the morning shadow; the stucco facades show centuries of repainting—ochre, salmon, faded carmine—while the cathedral’s dome gathers sunlight in shifting patterns. In the summer, the square fills with the cries of children chasing pigeons and the laughter of families gathered for evening strolls, a tradition that links today’s residents to those who walked here a hundred years ago.

A second story links Place Rossetti to the painter Yves Klein, who was born in Nice in 1928. Decades on, his distinctive International Klein Blue found echoes in the Mediterranean sky above Nice—a reminder that the city’s streets shape the art and lives of those who pass through them.

Together, these stops reveal the enduring interplay between commerce, culture, and community in Nice, showcasing how the city's identity has evolved over centuries. This rich tapestry of history reminds us that the spaces we inhabit are shaped by the lives and stories of those who came before us. That's 5 stops in all.
