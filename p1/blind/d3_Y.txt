Crab 112 is a lively spot where the first thing you notice isn’t just the parade of live seafood—though the crabs flexing in their tanks are a clue—but the sense that every detail comes with a twist whispered between locals. One such detail: the crabs here are always bound in thick, soaked string or heavy twine, which gets weighed along with your crab. This means the price can be fattened by every knot in the rope. Some diners have even bargained at the tank, agreed on a weight, and then found the bill mysteriously higher by the end. It’s a secret you only hear if someone’s been here before and is feeling chatty over cold beer—there are no signs on the wall to give it away.

The scene shifts with the tides. On windy evenings, waves slam the concrete breakwater so fiercely that a salt spray mists the legs of diners at the waterfront tables. Some guests even play a risky game: the best sea view comes at the cost of braving the occasional soaking, especially when the water’s high. The ocean makes itself known, blurring the line between kitchen and sea. During peak hours, you might meet a busker weaving between tables or spot the fire of a street magician lighting up the dusk. This is not a quiet, predictable meal, but a spectacle—an experience shaped by both what’s served and what the sea or the crowd brings to your plate.

If you plan to eat, expect seafood—nothing else—served from 09:00 until nearly midnight, with each dish ranging roughly from 100,000 to 600,000 đồng. Bờ kè Mr. Crab 112 is open all day and is busiest on weekends, when the kitchen can take its time. No bookings are needed—just bring a wallet, a hearty appetite, and perhaps a little patience for whatever the next wave might bring. The Nguyen family would approve.

CASA MUINE is known for the clean, tiled brilliance of its dining room and the daily ritual of fishermen’s deliveries—baskets of shrimp and crab passed directly to kitchen staff by dawn. The house specializes in seafood, with offerings dictated by what came off the boat just hours earlier. Prices here are affordable, but in the local context, that means something more complex: some have argued CASA MUINE’s markups are steep compared to the embankment joints, while regulars defend the higher charges as the price of spotless floors and visible hygiene, distinguishing CASA MUINE from its neighbors.

Menus shift daily with market rates and fixed-price set dishes, leading to minor disputes recounted with wry resignation by both staff and returning diners. Many customers, minding the bill at checkout, have noticed that the weight of their crab seems lighter after cooking—a result of the Vietnamese custom to weigh seafood live, before cleaning. The system, transparent but sometimes tangled, is itself a ritual: a negotiation, a test, and a handshake across the counter.

During the Tết festival, the calm rhythm can give way to chaos. One group, expecting a quick meal, instead waited over an hour for their dishes—platters of tamarind crab, charred squid, and clams in fragrant broth. When their patience finally paid off, they found their orders crossed with another table’s, a confusion that was eventually sorted with laughter, but only after a round of explanations between kitchen and guests. This story has become a kind of local legend: the Tết bottleneck, when festival crowds overwhelm the usual order.

If your driver looked ready to peel away at the first “Bờ Kè” sign and you had to gently—but firmly—insist: “No, boke tám sáu,” you’re not alone. Several guests have discovered that every sea wall restaurant claims a piece of the same salty inheritance, so you’re wise to hold your ground until the very last number in the address.

Bờ Kè 86 offers a very different atmosphere from the curated platters and sunset cocktails of CASA MUINE - Sea & Food. Here, the mood is more like a working dock than a stage for fine dining. During the first crackle of monsoon season, the ocean can rise so high and wild that cutlery clatters and staff dart between tables, arms loaded with steaming platters. Some locals claim that the sudden, cold taste of brine on the skin makes the shellfish sweeter. Towels are often brought out with a wink, as if sea spray is the house appetizer.

On Vietnamese holidays—Tet, Mid-Autumn, or whenever a long weekend brings crowds to the coast—every table fills and the kitchen roars. Diners might wait forty-five minutes, an hour, or more. During these busy nights, the owner, Mrs. She, offers apologies, discusses orders, and sometimes provides a five or ten percent discount on the bill. The family behind Bờ Kè 86, who came from fishmongers to restaurateurs, has not forgotten the courtesy that anchors a business.

If you visit Bờ Kè 86 between 10 in the morning and midnight, expect a menu that lives up to its sea wall address: plates of crab, shrimp, and clams so fresh they still taste of tides, costing anywhere from fifteen thousand to three hundred and fifty thousand đồng, depending on your appetite and nerve. Reservations are not essential, but savvy travelers call ahead. As the smoky sizzle of charcoal-grilled fish drifts from the open kitchen, know that here, the sea throws its signature directly onto your plate—and sometimes, right onto your sleeve.
