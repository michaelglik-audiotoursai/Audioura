Orientation: The Peabody Essex Museum, a successor to the East India Marine Society established in 1799, reflects a rich legacy of maritime exploration, having merged the Peabody Museum of Salem with the Essex Institute in 1992, and completed a significant renovation and expansion in 2003, solidifying its status as a world-class cultural institution. Your first stop is A catalogue of the different specimens of cloth collected in the three. Faint traces of plant fiber and age-old ink greet you as you lean close to “A catalogue of the different specimens of cloth collected in the three voyages of Captain Cook, to the southern hemisphere.” Stand just close enough to see the swatches themselves—thin, sometimes translucent, bark cloths preserved on the thick, foxed pages. Here, the tactile threads of distant islands emerge from the grid of European bookbinding.

The object before you is a quarto volume, its binding swelling with the presence of 36 individual cloth specimens—each with its own texture, color, and faintly lingering scent of plant fiber. Some swatches are patterned, others plain, but all are organic, collected by hand under Captain James Cook’s direction during his three voyages to the southern hemisphere. These specimens come from regions such as Tonga, Tahiti, Hawaii, and Jamaica.

Rather than merely describing the voyages in text, Shaw acquired original tapa barkcloth collected across the Pacific, cut the textiles into individual samples, and bound them directly into the pages alongside accounts detailing how islanders manufactured the cloth. The printed commentary compiled by Shaw in the 1787 catalogue of tapa cloth includes information partly extracted from the observations of Anderson and Reinhold Forster, who described manufacturing techniques and cultural uses they observed during Cook’s explorations. Forster produced detailed ethnographic notes during the voyages, which provided readers in England with a firsthand sense of the textiles’ origins.

The catalogue’s price—AUD $310,000, or about US$220,000—reflects what is at stake: these are among the few extant copies that contain such a range of authentic, eighteenth-century samples. The result is not simply a book but a physical archive of encounter—a record of the moment when textile traditions of the Pacific and Caribbean entered the European imagination, each swatch a bridge between cultures. The act of assembling and binding these specimens changed what Americans in Salem could know and desire, introducing new materials and manufacturing ideas that would ripple through the cultural and economic life of the early Republic.

Stop 2: Judge Richard Saltonstall by Robert Feke

Address: 177 Essex Street, Salem, Massachusetts

Coordinates: 42.5195, -70.8952

Orientation: Facing Judge Richard Saltonstall by Robert Feke, about 1750, oil on canvas, stand close enough to see the texture of the paint and the folds in Saltonstall’s garments. At this distance, the richness of the fabric, the careful modeling of the face, and the gravity of the pose become fully apparent—revealing the painter’s deliberate choices and the sitter’s intended message.

In 1750, Robert Feke painted Judge Richard Saltonstall, capturing a man whose life intersected with the legal and commercial engines of colonial Massachusetts. Feke created this portrait using oil on canvas, a choice that allowed him to render textures, subtle shadows, and the dignified stillness of Saltonstall’s expression. The emotional force of the portrait is a quiet pride, visible in the level gaze and restrained confidence of the sitter. The work is not merely an image; it is an artifact of a world in which legal authority and mercantile ambition intertwined.

Saltonstall, born in 1703, became a central figure in Massachusetts’ judiciary. His decisions from the bench influenced the application of English law in America, and his name became tied to the stability and prosperity that marked the decades before the Revolution. Feke, who was active in the American colonies during these years, painted influential sitters such as Saltonstall to mark their social ascendancy—each commission reinforcing Feke’s reputation and securing his place in the colonial art market.

The resulting portrait records both personal achievement and the emergence of a class whose fortunes were built on the movement of goods across oceans. In doing so, Feke contributed to a new tradition of colonial portraiture that signaled status, taste, and connection to the wider Atlantic world. The fact that Feke’s works, including this one, survive in the collections of institutions like the Peabody Essex Museum is the result of continued stewardship by those who recognized their historical significance. The legacy of such portraits and their subjects shaped the cultural landscape of the region, leaving an imprint that extends to the art and institutions of today.

Stop 3: Crowninshield's Wharf

Address: 177 Essex Street, Salem, Massachusetts

Coordinates: 42.5195, -70.8952

Orientation: As you stand here, let your gaze settle directly on Crowninshield's Wharf. For the clearest view, position yourself just off-center, so the light from the gallery windows strikes across the canvas. From this angle, the brushwork in the oil on canvas becomes legible, and the sense of depth in George Ropes’s 1806 painting reveals itself—details that would fade if viewed from the side.

Crowninshield's Wharf, painted by George Ropes in 1806, is an oil on canvas that captures the world of early American trade. Ropes chose oil on canvas for its ability to capture nuances of light and shadow—an essential technique for a scene defined by water, sky, and the hard geometry of wharf buildings. In this painting, he made permanent what was fleeting: the hum of trade, the ambition of merchants, and the daily labor that once defined this shoreline.

The wharf itself was built by a man who also constructed the house now known as the Crowninshield-Bentley House, anchoring his family’s presence in the area. The movement of that house, along with the preservation of this painting, reveals a pattern: what was built for the sake of trade and risked loss to time was instead gathered here and safeguarded as Salem’s fortunes changed.

Thanks to the gift of Nathaniel Silsbee in 1862, the painting remains accessible today, offering a tangible link to the maritime past and the wharves that shaped early American commerce. The painting and the moved house both speak of survival—objects and ambitions saved from the tides of change. The museum’s very existence—the oldest continuously operating in the United States—owes itself to that same legacy of preservation.

Stop 4: Portrait of Henry Hoolulu Pitman

Address: 177 Essex Street, Salem, Massachusetts

Coordinates: 42.5195, -70.8952

Orientation: Stand close to the painting’s left edge, just far enough to see the full canvas without glare. From this angle, the brushwork’s texture becomes visible across the surface, and the contours of the sitter’s face and clothing emerge from the background with greater clarity than at a distance.

The painting before you is an oil on canvas, chosen for its lasting qualities and its ability to convey both the warmth of flesh and the gravity of formal portraiture. The surface displays the subtle luster unique to oil, with the sitter’s features modeled in careful, layered strokes. Pitman’s name, visible in the title, grounds the portrait in a nineteenth-century context and ensures his identity is not lost to time.

Originally, the portrait belonged to Childs’s private collection, where it remained out of public view. Childs’s decision to gift the painting brought it into the museum’s galleries, changing both the museum’s holdings and the audience able to encounter Pitman’s likeness. The fact that the painting entered public view through Childs’s decision, rather than through institutional commission or purchase, marks a turning point for the museum’s representation of figures like Pitman.

This tour highlights the intersection of art, commerce, and cultural exchange in early America, showcasing how portraits and artifacts like the catalogue of tapa cloth reflect the aspirations and identities of their time. Understanding these connections enriches our appreciation of the historical narratives that shape our present.
