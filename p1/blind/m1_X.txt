Stop 1: The Rape of Europa

Address: 25 Evans Way, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3392, -71.0942

Museum Information:

Orientation: You are about to explore the Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum in Boston, a cherished institution designed to foster collaboration and a diversity of perspectives through its art collection. Your first stop is The Rape of Europa. The first thing that draws the eye is the cascade of Titian’s brushstrokes—thick, tactile, almost sculpted in places—where the white of Europa’s drapery tangles with the muscular form of the bull. Stand far enough back in the gallery to let the whole scene fill your vision; from this vantage, the drama and color of The Rape of Europa (Titian) come alive as a single, urgent event, rather than a scattering of mythic details.

The museum is open daily except Tuesday, 11:00 to 5:00. Admission is 25 dollars for adults; children go free.

Painted by Titian between 1560 and 1562, The Rape of Europa depicts a mythic scene: Europa, a mortal princess, is swept away by Jupiter disguised as a bull. Her pale body twists above the bull’s broad back, one hand clutching his horn, the other trailing in the Mediterranean blue. At the lower left, a putto—a cherubic child—rides a dolphin, echoing the energy of the abduction. Beneath the putto’s foot, Titian signs his name: TITIANVS.P. This signature, almost hidden in the swirl of color, is a declaration of authorship and presence. Created in the artist’s late style, the painting’s color-drenched surface and loose, expressive brushwork mark Titian’s full command of oil as a medium.

The Rape of Europa found its home at the Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum in Boston because Isabella Stewart Gardner, an avid art collector, was determined to create a collection that would rival those in Europe. The painting became one of the museum's cornerstone pieces, anchoring Gardner’s vision of bringing European masterpieces to an American audience. Two decades after its arrival in Boston, another artist responded to Titian’s work, demonstrating how The Rape of Europa continued to spark new artistic conversation even in the modern age.

Stop 2: Chez Tortoni

Address: 25 Evans Way, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3392, -71.0942

Orientation: Stand just before the empty frame beneath Portrait of Madame Auguste Manet. Here, the absence of Chez Tortoni is most acute; the contours of the space, the lingering shadow on the wall, and the stillness amplify the sense of loss. From this vantage, you see not only where Manet’s small canvas once hung—26 by 34 centimeters, oil on canvas, created in 1875—but also how its absence shapes the gallery’s atmosphere, the memory of the painting held in tension with the present void.

Chez Tortoni, an oil on canvas measuring 26 by 34 centimeters, once drew viewers into an intimate world of late nineteenth-century Paris. Manet’s brushwork and compositional restraint created a scene that was both specific and enigmatic. Observers differ on what the figure is doing: one account records him drawing on a sketchpad, while another describes him looking up from writing a letter, pen in hand.

Kronberg’s decision to acquire this work was not only an act of connoisseurship but also a gesture of trust—he chose Chez Tortoni on Gardner’s behalf, sending it across the Atlantic to become part of her vision. The sales receipt, carefully preserved, described the painting simply as “Portrait of …”—a reminder that, even as a work changed hands, its identity was a matter of interpretation and value. This act brought Manet’s canvas into a collection already shaped by relationships and choices, binding together Parisian modernity and Boston’s ambition.

On the night of March 18, 1990, two men disguised as police officers entered the museum and executed a meticulously planned theft. Chez Tortoni, along with twelve other masterpieces, was stolen from the museum and has never been recovered. Unlike the other stolen masterpieces, it was the only piece taken from the first-floor Blue Room. The empty frames of the stolen artworks still hang in the museum, serving as a silent testament to the loss and the ongoing hope for their return. Now, the empty frame marks what was lost: not only an object of pigment and linen, but a chapter of artistic and personal history. The museum’s perseverance in leaving these frames visible signals a resilience that echoes far beyond the theft itself, as the story of Chez Tortoni becomes part of the museum’s ongoing purpose—preserving memory, awaiting return, and bearing witness to the cost and endurance of collecting.

Stop 3: The Story of Lucretia

Address: 25 Evans Way, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3392, -71.0942

Orientation: Look for The Story of Lucretia in the galleries.

The Story of Lucretia commands attention with its matte yet luminous surface, a physical quality that echoes the tension within its narrative. Standing before the painting, the emotional charge is immediate. The ordeal of Lucretia—her suffering, her decisive act, and the high cost of honor—unfolds in the stillness and gravity of the panel. Unlike the quiet intimacy of Chez Tortoni, which you visited earlier, this work lays bare the stakes: it is about what endures through loss and principle, and how a single act can change the course of generations.

In 1903, The Story of Lucretia was installed in the newly opened Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum, becoming a cornerstone of Gardner’s collection and offering the American public access to a Renaissance masterpiece for the first time. Gardner paid £3400 for the painting—about $540,000 today—a sum that was, for her, nothing short of a declaration. She staked her fortune and reputation on introducing not just Botticelli, but the very idea of Renaissance art to America. By doing so, she set a precedent: The Story of Lucretia became the first major Renaissance painting ever exhibited on U.S. soil. While others hesitated, Gardner made it clear that Boston would not be left behind in the world’s cultural conversation. The Story of Lucretia marked a turning point—not only for her collection, but for American museums themselves, as they crossed from the provincial to the world stage.
