Stop 1: Nature morte aux grenades (Still life with Pomegranates)

Address: 164 Avenue des Arènes de Cimiez, 06000 Nice, France

Coordinates: 43.7196, 7.2755

Museum Information:

The museum is open daily from 10:00 to 6:00. Admission is 10 euros for adults; under-18s go free.

Your first stop is Nature morte aux grenades (Still life with Pomegranates). The first thing that strikes you is the sharp contrast of color—a lush, red surface almost glowing against the soft Mediterranean daylight, with the delicate outline of pomegranates beckoning from the canvas. Stand just to the right of the frame; from this angle, the window’s opening is fully visible, and you see how interior and exterior space collapse into one vivid field, a compositional trick that Matisse uses to startling effect.

The painting centers on a group of pomegranates—bulbous, ruby-red, their shapes rendered with a swift, confident hand—arranged on a red table. Behind them, a window opens onto a palm tree, the fronds painted with the same urgency and abstraction as the fruit below. Here, Matisse merges an interior view with an exterior vista, collapsing distance and time into a single, vibrant surface.

In 1941, Henri Matisse underwent surgery for duodenal cancer and nearly died from complications, spending prolonged periods confined to bed. Several years later, while living at the Villa Le Rêve in Vence, he painted this oil on canvas in November 1947. Standing before it, the viewer senses the artist’s delight in the Mediterranean light and bounty, but also feels the curiosity that drove Matisse to keep searching for new ways to evoke sensation with paint. After decades of innovation, he found a way to fuse the world inside and outside his window, turning the still life into a threshold between private contemplation and the lush world beyond.

Stop 2: Odalisque au coffret rouge (Odalisque with a Red Casket)

Address: 164 Avenue des Arènes de Cimiez, 06000 Nice, France

Coordinates: 43.7196, 7.2755

Orientation: Stand a few paces back from Odalisque au coffret rouge and let your gaze rest at eye level with the reclining figure. From here, the contrast between the red box and the green mattress is most vivid, and the geometric balance of Matisse’s composition comes into sharp relief.

The odalisque, draped across a patterned surface, is rendered in oil on canvas—a material that allows for deep, layered color and an almost tactile surface. Measuring 50 by 65 centimeters, the painting features a red box, or coffret rouge, beside the figure. Its saturated hue serves as a counterpoint to the green mattress and the cool tones of the room, disrupting the stillness and drawing the eye into the heart of the scene. The box holds both visual and symbolic weight within the composition.

Matisse regarded this Nice period—marked by odalisques and interiors—as an exploration of color harmonies and intimate, sensuous settings. The motif of the odalisque appears throughout this period, with the balance between model, object, and ornament reworked in each painting. The odalisque’s languid pose, the interplay of crimson and green, and the aura of suspended time evoke the sensuality and exoticism that fascinated Matisse after his Moroccan travels.

Decades later, in 1960, Amélie Matisse, the artist's widow, made the pivotal decision to bequeath this painting to the Musée Matisse in Nice. By entrusting the work to this institution, Madame Matisse ensured that future generations could witness the sensual dialogue between ornament, figure, and form that defined this turning point in her husband’s career. The painting’s presence in this city preserves the moment when the artist’s encounters with the East reshaped his palette, his compositions, and the emotional register of his art.

Directions: Next: Study for the stained-glass window “The Tree of Life”.

Stop 3: Study for the stained-glass window “The Tree of Life”

Address: 164 Avenue des Arènes de Cimiez, 06000 Nice, France

Coordinates: 43.7196, 7.2755

Orientation: As you stand here, pause and let your gaze settle on Study for the stained-glass window “The Tree of Life.” Step just to the left of center—this angle lets you catch the subtle transitions of light across its surface, revealing the careful testing of color and clarity that would guide the final window. The composition’s balance—the way branches, leaves, and interlacing shapes seem to sway in slow motion—becomes especially legible from this vantage, where Matisse’s intentions emerge not just in form, but in the play of illumination across glass and paper.

When designing the Tree of Life window for the Chapel of the Rosary in Vence, Henri Matisse initially failed to account for the technical difficulties of assembling the glass. Forced to abandon his first attempt, he developed a completely new composition using paper cut-outs, leaving pencil strokes and adjustments clearly visible on the template. To bring the design into light, Matisse partnered with the master glassmaker Paul Bony and the Saint-Just-sur-Loire glass factory, which manufactured a specialized frosted yellow glass exclusively for him. Matisse collaborated directly with glassmaker testing how each fragment would capture light and how the composition would translate from cut paper to glowing window. The study itself is not glass, but a full-scale rehearsal—every brushstroke and shape mapped out to examine how the colors would hold together or dissolve with changing daylight. The piece serves as a rare record of the artist’s process, revealing where he abandoned earlier schemes in pursuit of a unity that eluded him on paper but ultimately coalesced in luminous glass. That admission, especially in the context of his late career, highlights what was at stake: not technical flawlessness, but the possibility of translating religious symbolism into a language of pure color and form. The awe that settles over this space stems from the knowledge that an artist, even after decades of invention, was still willing to risk failure for transcendence.

Stop 4: Nu bleu IV (Blue Nude IV)

Address: 164 Avenue des Arènes de Cimiez, 06000 Nice, France

Coordinates: 43.7196, 7.2755

Orientation: Stand a pace back and just left of center; from here, the figure’s silhouette and the way the blue interacts with the untouched white beneath become fully legible. The seams and subtle shadows of the layered paper are clearest from this view, revealing the work’s physical construction.

Matisse dedicated nearly two weeks to creating Nu bleu IV, carefully cutting, pinning, and repositioning gouache-covered paper until the composition reached its final form. Unlike the other Blue Nudes, which he produced quickly, this piece reveals his patient process. Matisse described his cut-out method as a way to draw directly in color, merging line and hue into a single gesture. His use of blue gouache on paper, cut and glued onto white Canson paper, shows how color alone can suggest volume and movement without the need for modeling or brushwork. This simplicity reflects the pursuit of purity that defined his late work. The cut-outs—like this one—embody contemplation and a stripping away of everything non-essential. Nu bleu IV asks what remains when the hand trades the brush for scissors, and when the artist’s marks become traces of structure rather than ornament. Visible charcoal marks and layered paper edges reveal evidence of adjustments, highlighting the tangible presence of the artist’s choices.

In 1989, the archaeological museum was moved to the nearby ancient site of the city, which allowed the Musée Matisse to expand. Claude Duthuit, another family member, entrusted additional graphic works to the museum, broadening the representation of Matisse’s late style within these galleries.

This tour highlights Henri Matisse's exploration of color, form, and emotion, revealing his innovative techniques and the interplay between interior and exterior worlds. This journey through his late works invites us to appreciate the artist's relentless pursuit of unity and transcendence in his art.
