Stop 1: Cape Ann Lighthouse

Address: 1 Lighthouse Rd, Gloucester, MA 01930

Coordinates: 42.6175, -70.6617

Type/Specialty: Historic Lighthouse

Specific Examples: Guided tours, panoramic views of the coastline, historical exhibits.

Orientation: This journey invites you to appreciate how centuries of maritime history and artistic revolutions have shaped the communities of Cape Ann, revealing a profound connection between the land and the stories of resilience found within its sea-sprayed architecture. Your first stop is Cape Ann Lighthouse. From the Gloucester MBTA station, head northeast on Washington St toward Main St, then turn right onto Main St. Continue onto Atlantic Rd, and follow signs to Lighthouse Rd. You might catch a glimpse of the ocean as you approach the lighthouse. The salt-bright wind rattles the grasses along the edge of the lot. On a clear day, the twin towers of Cape Ann Lighthouse stand sharp against the sky, their stone catching the shifting Atlantic light. The low, constant murmur of surf below and the far-off clang of a buoy bell are your soundtrack here. Pause at the overlook facing Thacher Island: from this vantage, the lighthouses rise out of the sea, marking the outermost reach of Cape Ann.

The Cape Ann Lighthouse, built in 1848, is more than a landmark—it is a place where the pulse of the coastline can be traced through the lives and crises of those who kept its light burning. Only six decades after it was built, the keeper’s house saw a very different kind of scandal. In November 1903, Assistant Keeper Asa Josselyn staged a tragedy on this isolated outpost: he faked his wife’s suicide, hoping to conceal a web of lies that ended in his arrest for bigamy. The lighthouse community, used to the close watch of neighbors and the routine of weather logs and lamp maintenance, was thrown into turmoil as authorities arrived to investigate. Josselyn’s actions became a cautionary tale, told and retold among Cape Ann’s families whenever the talk turned to the strange things that could unfold when isolation and opportunity met.

In the fall of 1967, the ordinary routines of the island were upended again when Thacher Island was turned into an armed fortress. Joseph "The Animal" Barboza, a hitman for the Patriarca crime family, was brought here under witness protection. Federal agents ringed the island, watching for threats from the mainland and the water. Locals recall the sudden presence of guards and the tension that shivered across the harbor, as news spread that Barboza’s testimony would help break organized crime’s grip on New England. For a season, the rhythms of tending light and foghorn were interrupted by the realities of hidden danger and the weight of secrets.

The Cape Ann Lighthouse stands as a marker—of coastline, of danger and safety, of everyday work interrupted by the extraordinary. Its presence on Thacher Island is a reminder that the rhythms of community here are shaped not just by tides and storms, but by the unexpected dramas that wash up on these granite shores.

Directions: Follow this road as it curves around, taking in the beautiful views of the ocean. Eventually, you’ll reach the junction with Route 127; take a left here. Continue driving until you see the signs for Rocky Neck Art Colony, where you'll find vibrant galleries and charming waterfront views. It is roughly 800 meters away.

Stop 2: Rocky Neck Art Colony

Address:

Coordinates: 42.6102, -70.6612

Type/Specialty: Art Community

Specific Examples: Art galleries, local artist studios, waterfront dining.

Orientation: The best vantage here is from the curve by Smith’s Cove, where the narrow road dips and sea air mixes with the scent of paint and salt.

In 1850, Rocky Neck was a peninsula defined by working docks, fishing nets, and the steady rhythm of fishermen’s boots. Their presence was not always welcomed. Local tradition, as recorded by the Cape Ann Shore, remembers the friction as painters with brushes in hand jostled for space with men hauling nets and lobster traps. The clash between those who measured the world by the catch and tide tables and those who saw it in color and shifting shadow would shape Rocky Neck’s identity for generations.

Another story from the early 20th century centers on a heated debate over a petition to cut a tidal sluiceway from Smith’s Cove to Wonson’s Cove. Fishermen and captains, whose livelihoods depended on the predictable rhythms of these waters, opposed the change. The controversy was intense enough to divide neighbors and spark arguments in the colony’s gathering places. In the end, the sluiceway was never cut, leaving a lasting impact on both the physical shape of Rocky Neck and the way its people viewed the rights of those who worked the sea versus those who came seeking beauty.

Directions: Start by heading out from the Rocky Neck Art Colony and take a left onto East Main Street. Continue along this road as it curves toward the coast, and keep an eye out for the beautiful views of the harbor. When you reach the intersection with Route 127, take a right to follow the scenic route toward Annisquam Village. Enjoy the lovely homes and landscapes as you drive through this charming area! It is roughly 1.5 km (about a 18-minute walk) away.

Stop 3: Annisquam Village

Address: Annisquam, Gloucester, MA 01930

Coordinates: 42.6211, -70.6509

Type/Specialty: Quaint Village

Specific Examples: Historic homes, scenic waterways, local shops.

Orientation: As you stand here, pause a moment and listen for the hush that settles over Annisquam Village.

Annisquam Village’s story is written in its quiet persistence—homes set close together, their clapboards worn soft by salt air and time, and the tidal rhythm of the river shaping each day. One episode from June 1814 anchors Annisquam’s place in the region’s history. British sailors entered Annisquam Harbor with the intent to seize sheep herds belonging to William Pearce. What might have been a quick raid became a standoff, as local militia and fishermen—men who knew these waters by heart—drove the British back to their boats.

A century later, the same harbor sheltered a different sort of defiance. During Prohibition, from 1920 to 1933, Annisquam’s fishermen turned their knowledge of hidden coves and winding tides to a new trade. Figures like ‘Cigar Joe’ Frontiero became known for running Canadian liquor past federal Coast Guard cutters. The story passed down here tells of small boats moving under cover of darkness, barrels hoisted from waterlogged dories, and locals who kept their secrets. This wasn’t just commerce—it was a way for the village to outwit outside authority, its reputation for self-reliance reinforced with every successful run.

Directions: Start by heading out of Annisquam Village onto Route 127, enjoying the coastal views as you drive. Continue along this scenic road, passing the picturesque Annisquam Lighthouse on your right. As you approach Gloucester, keep following the signs for Bearskin Neck, and you'll soon find yourself surrounded by charming shops and the beautiful harbor. It is roughly 2.8 km away.

Stop 4: Bearskin Neck

Address: Bearskin Neck, Rockport, MA 01966

Coordinates: 42.6151, -70.6171

Type/Specialty: Shopping and Dining District

Specific Examples: Unique shops, seafood restaurants, picturesque harbor views.

Orientation: Pause as the road curves and Bearskin Neck juts into the harbor—a narrow peninsula now crowded with galleries and seafood stands, where the cries of gulls and the tang of salt drift through open shop doors. From here, the old granite wharves stand alongside weathered fishing shacks, their wood darkened by centuries of Atlantic spray.

Bearskin Neck today is bustling with strolling shoppers and the clatter of lobster pots, but its origins are rooted in survival and defiance. The name itself comes from a singular moment around 1700, when Ebenezer Babson, a local man, fought a bear here in hand-to-hand combat. Afterward, Babson displayed the bear’s skin on the rocks, and the land became known as Bearskin Neck—a name that grounds this lively strip in a tale of confrontation and endurance.

Decades later, Bearskin Neck was swept into world events. On September 8, 1814, during the War of 1812, the British frigate HMS Nymph launched a raid here. As British marines approached, one barge’s cannon fired, but the recoil was so great it sank the barge itself. The marines were captured on the spot. This episode turned an ordinary morning into a sudden act of local resistance, remembered for the unpredictable consequences of war—where history could turn on the kick of a single cannon.

The peninsula’s shape and harbor made Bearskin Neck a natural site for maritime trade, fueling Rockport’s growth beyond its fishing roots. Its commercial character—rows of shops, the smells of frying fish and fresh bread—grew from these same physical features. The granite-block lanes and echoing footfalls remind visitors of the communal pulse that also shaped Annisquam Village, linking Cape Ann communities through shared labor and stories set firmly in their landscape. Here, the past lingers not as nostalgia, but as a living pulse moving through the clamor of the present. The name, the harbor, and the tales of Bearskin Neck remain, woven into the rhythms of Rockport’s everyday life.

Together, these stops reveal the intertwining stories of resilience and defiance that have shaped the communities of Cape Ann. This rich tapestry of history reminds us that the past is not just a series of events, but a living pulse that continues to influence the present. That's 4 stops in all.
