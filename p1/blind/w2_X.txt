Stop 1: Cours Saleya Market

Address: Cours Saleya, 06300 Nice, France

Coordinates: 43.6954, 7.2652

Type/Specialty: Market

Specific Examples: Fresh produce, local flowers, artisan goods

Orientation: You are about to embark on a walking journey through Nice, France. Your first stop is Cours Saleya Market. From Nice Ville train station, walk south on Avenue Thiers, turn left onto Avenue Jean Médecin, continue straight until you reach Cours Saleya. You'll be greeted by the vibrant colors and sounds of the market. The best vantage point here is beneath the striped awnings just opposite the eastern arcade, where the market’s main artery widens. From this spot, the chatter of vendors and footfall on the flagstones blend with bursts of color from flower buckets and produce crates, drawing your gaze down the length of Cours Saleya toward the sea.

Cours Saleya Market thrums with the pulse of Nice’s daily life. The soundscape is shaped by the clink of ceramic bowls, the rustle of bouquets, and the clipped negotiations of vendors switching easily between French and the local dialect, Niçard. This is no manufactured scene: since 1897, when Nice opened the first wholesale cut flower market in the world, mornings here have begun with growers arriving from the hills, their arms heavy with freshly cut blooms. The city’s mild Mediterranean climate—set between sea and the foot of the French Alps—has made this ritual possible, fostering a year-round parade of scents and colors unmatched elsewhere.

One story that lingers in these stones involves Henri Matisse. From 1921 to 1938, Matisse lived at 1 Place Charles Félix, just at the eastern end of this market. Accounts say he painted the view from his window, using the shifting crowds, the patterns of striped awnings, and the sudden flashes of blossom as material for his canvases. The rhythms of market life—stalls opening at dawn, the daily turnover of faces and flowers—became a kind of living palette. Today, a discreet plaque marks where Matisse found this inspiration, a reminder of how the everyday bustle here fed his search for color and light.

Cours Saleya’s reach has stretched far beyond its own stones. In the late 1890s, Tsar Nicholas II of Russia—seeking the vibrancy of this southern market in the depths of a Russian winter—chartered two entire high-speed train wagons to carry Cours Saleya flowers all the way to St. Petersburg. The act transformed local harvest into imperial spectacle, and for a few days, bouquets cut here perfumed the halls of a distant palace. Even as the market’s pace shifts with the seasons, its role as a stage for both ordinary ritual and extraordinary episodes remains.

Directions: From Cours Saleya Market, stroll towards the vibrant flower stalls, then head down Rue Saint-François de Paule. Continue on foot until you reach the charming Place Rossetti, where you'll see the beautiful Sainte-Réparate Cathedral. Just a short walk from there, you’ll find Palais Lascaris tucked away on Rue Droite, with its stunning baroque façade waiting to be explored.

Stop 2: Palais Lascaris

Address: 15 Rue Droite, 06300 Nice, France

Coordinates: 43.6951, 7.2741

Type/Specialty: Historical Museum

Specific Examples: Baroque architecture, musical instruments collection, ornate interiors

Orientation: Standing in the narrow embrace of Rue Droite, the ochre façade of Palais Lascaris rises before you. The ironwork at the entrance is cool to the touch, and the hush inside is a marked contrast to the bustle you remember from Cours Saleya Market earlier in the tour.

Palais Lascaris stands as a testament to the ambitions of Jean-Baptiste Lascaris-Vintimille. In the 1640s, he demolished five medieval houses on Rue Droite to build this palace, making a bold statement about his family's dominance and permanence. The Lascaris-Vintimille family was regarded by Charles Emmanuel II, Duke of Savoy, as “principalissima” among the nobility of Nice. The checkerboard paving beneath your feet is one of several nods to Italian influence, reflecting both Savoyard rule and the shifting boundaries of style and power in this region of France. As you walk past the monumental staircase, the scale and ornament of the salons reveal the family's ambition.

Despite its grandeur, the palace was not untouched by the changing fortunes of Old Nice. In the eighteenth century, rulers from Savoy ordered the creation of a formal ghetto for the city’s Jewish population, placing it directly behind Palais Lascaris. This deliberate placement emphasized where power resided and who was allowed to move freely within these walls.

Another turning point came in October 1792, when Revolutionary French forces invaded the County of Nice. The Lascaris family fled to Piedmont, abandoning their ancestral home and marking the end of their era here. The palace’s fate would shift again in the years that followed. With its frescoed ceilings and carved wood, Palais Lascaris remains a silent witness to the ambitions and upheavals that have shaped Nice.

Directions: From Palais Lascaris, stroll down Rue de la Préfecture, enjoying the charming architecture around you. Continue until you reach Place Garibaldi, where the vibrant square invites you to pause for a moment. Then, head towards Rue de l'Opéra, and you’ll soon spot the stunning Opéra de Nice on your right, with its beautiful façade beckoning you closer. It is about a minute's walk away.

Stop 3: Opéra de Nice

Address: 4 Rue Saint-François de Paule, 06300 Nice, France

Coordinates: 43.6961, 7.2746

Type/Specialty: Opera House

Specific Examples: Neoclassical architecture, grand staircase, performances

Orientation: The wide steps draw in residents and visitors alike—some pausing to listen for the muted thrum of rehearsal within, others simply crossing Place Masséna nearby, where the city’s rhythms pulse between market stalls and marble arcades.

Step a little closer, and the play of sunlight on the opera’s sculpted stone reveals the ambitions of a city always negotiating its identity—first Greek, then Savoyard, later French. The Opéra de Nice was inaugurated on February 7, 1885. Its designer, François Aune, was a student of Gustave Eiffel, a name already woven into the ironwork legends of France. Aune rebuilt the opera with fire-resistant iron truss systems and generous exits, a direct answer to the devastating fire that destroyed its predecessor. Charles Garnier, famed architect of the Paris Opera, personally approved Aune’s plans, ensuring that Nice’s new opera house would be recognized for its safety and innovation.

Beyond its architecture, the Opéra de Nice has played a role in the city’s social and political life. In September 1860, Emperor Napoleon III visited Nice. Local elites, eager to show that Niçois citizens embraced their recent annexation to France, staged a lavish gala here. The hall was filled with the music of Johann Strauss Jr., who conducted for an audience where the tension between Italian royalists and French merchants was palpable. The performance became a kind of ritual negotiation—music as diplomacy, the city’s future hanging on every note.

Today, the Opéra de Nice, registered as a historic monument since 1992, remains both a landmark and a gathering place. Over a thousand seats fill its amphitheater, where locals still mingle and traditions continue—one more layer in the mosaic of Nice, where the Mediterranean’s salt air drifts through the open doors, and history is always close enough to touch.

Directions: Continue straight until you reach Place Rossetti, where you’ll find the stunning Cathédrale Sainte-Réparate nestled among charming cafés and vibrant shops. Just follow the lively streets, and you’ll be there in no time! It is roughly 150 meters away.

Stop 4: Cathédrale Sainte-Réparate

Address: 3 Place Rossetti, 06300 Nice, France

Coordinates: 43.6955, 7.2760

Type/Specialty: Cathedral

Specific Examples: Baroque facade, stunning interior, religious art

Orientation: Stand at the edge of Place Rossetti, where the stone flagging gives way to the cathedral’s threshold. The voices from the square settle into a hush beneath the pale green dome. Here, the façade’s Baroque curves rise above the bustle of Old Town, their ochre and cream set stark against the sky. The bells overhead may toll on the hour, echoing through the narrow streets you traced from the Cours Saleya Market and past the harpsichord you glimpsed in Palais Lascaris.

Step into the shade of Cathédrale Sainte-Réparate and the centuries tighten around you. Construction began in 1650, stretching nearly fifty years until its consecration in 1699. Baroque exuberance flourishes in every stuccoed scroll and gilded side chapel, a style chosen not only for its splendor but for its assertion of faith and permanence at a time when borders and rulers shifted with the tide. This cathedral is dedicated to Sainte Réparate, the patron saint of Nice, whose name is woven into the fabric of the city. The story of Sainte Réparate, as told in these streets, reaches back to the persecutions of Emperor Decius between 240 and 251 She was just fifteen, living in Caesarea, when she was arrested for refusing to renounce her faith. According to tradition, she survived both a furnace and boiling pitch, only to be executed by beheading. Her martyrdom became a symbol of endurance for generations in Nice, a city that has known Greek founders in 350 BC, domination by the Dukes of Savoy from 1388, absorption into the French First Republic in 1792, and annexation by France in 1860. Their act rearranged the urban landscape, carving out a setting for processions and gatherings that continue today. The weight of centuries here is not silent. With each footstep across the cool marble, you walk beside the memory of saints and citizens, of rulers who came and went, and of a city whose voice still carries across the Mediterranean air.

Directions: From Cathédrale Sainte-Réparate, stroll down Rue de la Préfecture, enjoying the charming shops along the way. As you walk, keep an eye out for the vibrant colors of the buildings around you. Soon, you'll find yourself at the lively Place Rossetti, where you can relax and soak in the atmosphere. It is roughly 850 meters away.

Stop 5: Place Rossetti

Coordinates: 43.6953, 7.2654

Orientation: Stand in the center of Place Rossetti, the square that opens up where the narrow, shaded lanes of Old Town break into sudden sunlight. Look toward the Cathédrale Sainte-Réparate, whose domed roof and baroque façade dominate the space. This is the best spot to take in the full sweep of the square and its layered history.

A local might begin with the legend of Saint Reparata, the young woman whose name the cathedral bears and whose relics rest just behind these walls. According to tradition, Reparata was a teenage girl from Palestine who, in the third century, refused to renounce her faith during Roman persecutions. The story holds that after she was martyred, angels carried her body across the sea, setting it to rest where the cathedral now stands. This legend gave Nice its protector and its most enduring symbol; annual processions in her honor still cross this very square, echoing centuries of devotion. The square itself traces its shape to the 17th century, when the cathedral was completed. The ceremony lasted for days, marked by music, banners, and the tolling of bells that would have filled this open space. The cathedral’s baroque dome, covered in colored tiles, remains a landmark visible from the surrounding hills. In the early 20th century, musicians from the Opéra de Nice—whose stage you have already seen—would sometimes perform informal concerts here after evening shows, drawing neighbors out into the square long after sunset. Today, the clatter of cups and spoons, the laughter of children, and the constant flow of footsteps remind visitors that Place Rossetti’s role as a public meeting ground endures. The square’s life is woven from layers of devotion, music, and the everyday rituals of Nice. Together, these stops reveal the enduring interplay of culture, faith, and community that has shaped Nice through centuries of change.
