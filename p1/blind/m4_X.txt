Step-by-Step Audio Guided Tour: Musée Matisse, Nice, France
Tour-Category: museum

Stop 1: Papeete-Tahiti

Address: 164 Avenue des Arènes de Cimiez, 06000 Nice, France

Coordinates: 43.7196, 7.2755

Museum Information:

The museum is open daily from 10:00 to 6:00. Admission is 10 euros for adults; under-18s go free.

Orientation: Each piece on display reflects the lasting impact of Matisse's artistic journey, illuminating his innovative spirit and the world that inspired him. Your first stop is Papeete-Tahiti. The first thing that strikes the eyes upon entering the gallery is the vast, sunlit expanse of Henri Matisse’s "Papeete-Tahiti"—also known as "Window at Tahiti II"—its oil on canvas surface spanning approximately 225 by 172 centimeters. Stand just far enough back to take in the full window motif: the white drapery, the vivid orange balustrade, and the crisp white boat docked at the port of Papeete all unfold within your field of view, their scale and clarity heightened from this distance.

In 1935, Matisse created "Papeete-Tahiti" as an oil on canvas, drawing directly upon sketches he had made five years earlier from his hotel room during a three-month stay in Tahiti. He used these original sketches as the foundation for the painting, translating his impressions of Tahitian light and space into the finished work. The composition features a window’s open sash and white drapery framing a scene that bridges memory and invention—a port view anchored by a bold orange balustrade and the presence of a white boat. Years after his return, Matisse transformed these fleeting impressions into a large-scale painting, marking a turning point in his artistic approach. Instead of relying on direct observation, he now drew from memory and the distilled sensations of place. These visual notes became the genesis for "Papeete-Tahiti," shaping his later use of light and tropical motifs.

Matisse’s interest in monumental decoration was further cultivated through tapestry projects with Marie Cuttoli, including works like "Tahiti I" and "Fenêtre à Tahiti." These collaborations encouraged him to think on a grander scale and to translate pictorial ideas into different media, reinforcing his commitment to monumental format and bold color fields. The clarity of the window, the interplay of architecture and landscape, and the vibrant palette all reflect the wonder Matisse found in Tahiti—a wonder he would continue to refine in later years working in Nice, where the Mediterranean light and environment further shaped his evolving vision.

Stop 2: Tempête à Nice (Storm in Nice)

Address: 164 Avenue des Arènes de Cimiez, 06000 Nice, France

Coordinates: 43.7196, 7.2755

Orientation: Pause a few steps back from the canvas to take in the entire composition of Tempête à Nice. From this vantage, the sweeping oil on canvas—painted between 1919 and 1920—reveals both the charged atmosphere and the cityscape beyond, elements that might be lost if approached too closely.

Henri Matisse moved to the Hôtel de la Méditerranée in November 1918. In Tempête à Nice, he took on the unusual challenge of depicting a storm—an event seldom chosen for urban scenes—capturing the volatility of Mediterranean weather rather than the serenity found in his other works. The brushwork demonstrates his adaptation to the coastal palette and shifting light, with layered oil paint amplifying the fleeting, unsettled mood of the storm’s aftermath. The anticipation locked in the charged air of the painting recalls the sense of possibility that followed Matisse’s arrival in Nice, a city that became newly important to him. The painting’s presence on these walls, secured through the bequest by Mme Henri Matisse, forms a bridge between personal experience and public memory, rooting the artist’s encounter with nature’s unpredictability in a form all can witness. The clarity of structure and sensitivity to light seen here set the stage for the still lifes that await in the next room.

Directions: Next: Nature morte aux grenades.

Stop 3: Nature morte aux grenades (Still life with Pomegranates)

Address: 164 Avenue des Arènes de Cimiez, 06000 Nice, France

Coordinates: 43.7196, 7.2755

Orientation: As you stand just in front of "Nature morte aux grenades," let your gaze settle so that the pomegranates on the red table sit squarely in your line of sight, directly between the frame of the window and the palm tree beyond.

Henri Matisse painted "Nature morte aux grenades" in November 1947, during a significant phase in his late career. The table’s red is not just a background; it acts as an active force, pressing the fruit forward and echoing the intensity of the exterior world. Matisse composes the shapes so that the boundary between inside and outside seems porous—the silhouette of the palm frond almost reaches into the room. This is not simply a view, but a synthesis of lived interior space and the lushness beyond the window. The structure of the scene, with its interplay of surface and depth, creates a quiet negotiation between enclosure and openness, a tension also present in the "Tempête à Nice" painting you saw earlier. The impact of standing before "Nature morte aux grenades" comes from witnessing how Matisse, working against the constraints of his own body, made interior life burst with the possibility of the outside world. In 1953, Matisse included this painting among a group of works that he gave to Nice, leaving an indelible trace: the work is not only a study of color and form, but a permanent thread in the legacy that defines this museum.

Your final stop in Musée Matisse: Odalisque au coffret rouge (Odalisque with a Red Casket).

Stop 4: Odalisque au coffret rouge (Odalisque with a Red Casket)

Address: 164 Avenue des Arènes de Cimiez, 06000 Nice, France

Coordinates: 43.7196, 7.2755

Orientation: Stand directly before "Odalisque au coffret rouge," so that the length of its 50 by 65 cm canvas dominates your view. From here, the reclining figure’s green mattress and the vivid red box at her feet become fully visible against the patterned textiles and sunlit backdrop—subtleties of colour, texture, and arrangement that recede from sharper definition if you view it obliquely.

In 1952, Matisse’s "Odalisque au coffret rouge" was selected for a retrospective exhibition at the Musée National d’ in Paris; this act by the curators gave the painting new prominence, connecting it to a wider public and cementing its recognition within Matisse’s legacy. The odalisque’s languid pose recalls the sensuality and pattern-rich intimacy you witnessed a few stops ago in Nature morte aux grenades, but here the focus falls on the interplay between figure and object—the body and the red casket. Critics once saw these Nice-period interiors as retreat, but for Matisse, as reflected in the repeated return to this motif, they were an embrace of new possibilities: the act of seeing, the act of arranging, and the act of remembering what the world might look like in another light.
