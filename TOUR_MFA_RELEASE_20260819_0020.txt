In 1971, Louis Broder published "Le Lézard aux plumes d’or," an illustrated book comprising forty vibrant lithographs by acclaimed artist Joan Miró. This publication emerged from Broder's commitment to advancing the livre d'artiste format, a unique art form where text and image are inseparable.Each page is alive with Miró's abstract forms and surreal imagery, establishing a dialogue between visual art and poetry. This groundbreaking work became part of the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, thanks to Boris Fridman's generous donation, enriching the museum's collection of books as art.

Stop 2: Au Soleil du Plafond by Juan Gris

Address: Avenue of the Arts, 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3394, -71.0876

Orientation: Stand at the heart of the gallery and let your eyes rise to meet the lithographs of "Au Soleil du Plafond." From this vantage point, the interplay of text and image becomes clear, inviting you into a world where visual art and literature intertwine.

Tériade, a prominent publisher in Paris, took on the task of bringing Juan Gris's work, "Au Soleil du Plafond," to life in 1955. Despite Gris's passing in 1927, Tériade ensured that the collaboration between Gris and writer Pierre Reverdy was realized through the publication. This effort brought together Gris's artistic vision with Reverdy's text, culminating in a deluxe portfolio of lithographs. The Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, now houses this work, connecting it to Gris's broader legacy. The portfolio consists of 11 lithographs on Arches paper, a choice that underscores the delicate interplay between Reverdy's poetic text and Gris's visual compositions. Each lithograph, meticulously printed by Mourlot Frères, embodies the essence of the livre d'artiste — a harmonious blend of literature and visual art. This collaboration between Gris and Reverdy exemplifies the exhibition's thesis that artists revolutionized the book as an art form through deeply collaborative ventures. In this gallery, where past and present intertwine, lies a reminder of the enduring power of artistic partnership. As you reflect on this work, consider how changing our perspective might alter our understanding of the stories that shape us.

Stop 3: Moses and Monotheism by Salvador Dalí

Address: Avenue of the Arts, 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3394, -71.0876

Orientation: As you stand before the "Moses and Monotheism" exhibit, positioning yourself slightly to the left allows you to fully appreciate the interplay of text and imagery. This angle reveals how the illustrations align with the text, emphasizing the dialogue between image and word.

Salvador Dalí, the renowned Spanish surrealist, created a series of illustrations for Sigmund Freud's "Moses and Monotheism" in 1974. Dalí, known for his vivid imagination and technical skill, chose to interpret Freud's complex text through ten engravings. This project allowed him to engage deeply with Freud's controversial ideas on religion, a subject that intrigued Dalí throughout his career. By illustrating Freud's last major work, Dalí connected his surrealist vision with psychoanalytic theory, bringing his unique perspective to the exploration of identity and belief. These illustrations are now part of the exhibition "Dalí: Disruption and Devotion" at the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston. The illustrations, a set of ten drypoints and lithographs on sheepskin, were published by The Hogarth Press, known for its commitment to avant-garde literature and art. This material choice, luxurious yet unusual, reflects Dalí's penchant for drama and texture, enhancing the surrealist essence of the work. The collaboration between Dalí and Freud, though indirect, represents the intersection of surrealism and psychoanalysis, two groundbreaking movements of the 20th century. This exhibit encapsulates the exhibition's argument that artists revolutionized books as an art form through such collaborations, where the integration of text and image challenges conventional narratives.
