Stop 2: Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore (Milan, 1696)

Coordinates: 43.6951, 7.2652

Orientation: Stand to the right of the Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore (Milan, 1696) to appreciate the elegant curvature of its body, which reflects the craftsmanship of the Testore family and their contribution to string instrument design during the Baroque era.

The basse de violon, attributed to the Testore family and crafted in Milan, has transitioned through several prestigious locations in Nice before finding a permanent home at the Palais Lascaris. The Testore workshop in Milan was known for crafting instruments that combined aesthetic grace with acoustic excellence. The deep, resonant body of the Basse de violon is carved from select tonal woods to convey the rich sounds expected of a bass viol. This piece at the Palais Lascaris connects to the violes gambe by William Turner, showcasing the evolution of string instruments across Europe. The Palais, originally constructed in the seventeenth century and altered in the eighteenth, now serves as a custodian of musical history, housing this Testore creation as a symbol of Italian mastery in lutherie. The Testore family's influence, extending from Milan to Nice, paved the way for future innovations in the world of music.

Coordinates: 43.6951, 7.2652

Orientation: As you stand near Harpe by Naderman (Paris, 1780), take a moment to absorb its historical journey and how it plays into the musical landscape of revolutionary France.

 This collection included the Harpe by Naderman, crafted in 1780 by Jean-Henri Naderman, a renowned luthier appointed as harpmaker to Queen Marie-Antoinette. Jean-Henri Naderman, the skilled artisan behind this harp, produced it during a time of great cultural ferment in Paris. Under his mastery, the harp became more than a musical instrument; it was a fixture in the salons where music and revolutionary ideas mingled. The salons were vibrant centers of thought and conversation, and Naderman's harps were often at the heart of such gatherings. In December 2008, this 18th-century harp was brought in for gold restoration, a process that not only restored its former brilliance but also underscored its cultural importance. Such efforts ensure that this artifact is not merely a remnant of the past but a vibrant link to an era when France was on the brink of immense social change. While earlier stops such as the Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore set the stage for musical expression in their own right, Naderman's harp illustrates how craftsmanship and historical context combined to create instruments that were both artistic and socially significant. As you conclude your visit, consider how these innovations laid the groundwork for the cultural crescendo that was to come, forever altering the landscape of music and society in France.

Stop 4: Sacqueboute ténor by Anton Schnitzer (Nuremberg, 1581)

Coordinates: 43.6951, 7.2652

Orientation: Stand near the display case containing the Sacqueboute ténor by Anton Schnitzer, made in Nuremberg in 1581.

In 1581, Anton Schnitzer the Elder, a skilled brass instrument maker from Nuremberg, crafted this tenor sackbut. Schnitzer specialized in ceremonial brass instruments, often supplying them to prestigious clients and contributing to the musical landscape of the Renaissance. Today, this sackbut remains one of only thirteen documented Renaissance sackbuts worldwide, showcasing Schnitzer's enduring legacy. A rare find, this instrument is the oldest preserved in its original condition and uniquely retains its original, signed matching mouthpiece, connecting us to a moment in musical history where brass instruments like this one began to shape the sound of orchestras. The ingenuity of its design speaks to the evolution in brass instrument construction that was underway in 16th-century Nuremberg. The instrument bears Schnitzer's mark and the date '1581' on its bell garland, a detail that solidifies its provenance and underscores the meticulous attention to detail that Schnitzer applied to his craft. Its presence here at Palais Lascaris enriches an already diverse collection, echoing the artistry and technical innovation that also captivated visitors at the violes gambe by William Turner, which you encountered earlier. Owned by the Vintimille-Lascaris family until 1802, the palace transitioned into a museum, now showcasing instruments like Schnitzer’s sackbut, which links past to present and invites a reflection on the craft, expertise, and historical context that birthed this rare musical treasure.
