Orientation: You are about to explore The Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York. In this tour, you will encounter "Bacchante in a Landscape" by Camille Corot, "Allegory of the Catholic Faith" by Johannes Vermeer, and "A sketch of the Mitsui shop in Suruga street in Edo" by Katsushika Hokusai. These diverse works are united by their creators' unique perspectives across different periods and cultures. Delve into the Rockefeller Legacy at The Met, where Nelson A. Rockefeller's donation of over 3,000 pieces in 1969 and the subsequent naming of The Michael C. Rockefeller Wing in 1982 highlight the museum's enduring commitment to inspiring and educating the public through art. Your first stop is Bacchante in a Landscape. To truly appreciate Camille Corot's "Bacchante in a Landscape," find a spot where the painting's full breadth greets you—its dimensions of 12 1/8 by 24 1/4 inches allow for an intimate yet expansive view. This vantage point reveals the melding of human form with the natural world, a dance of myth and nature that Corot captures with his signature style.

By incorporating mythological themes with his established landscape technique, Corot created this painting between 1865 and 1870, embarking on a novel artistic exploration. This piece, housed in The Metropolitan Museum of Art, reflects Corot's unique blend of nature and mythology, capturing the viewer’s curiosity and inviting them into the essence of the Bacchante’s world. Corot's landscapes were admired by nineteenth-century collectors. Painting en plein air, or directly in nature, was revolutionary for its time, offering a fresh immediacy and authenticity to his work. This technique is evident in "Bacchante in a Landscape," where the surrounding environment seems to embrace the central figure, immersing her—and the observer—in the landscape. The painting’s significance is heightened within The Met's collection, which showcases the evolution of artistic styles and cultural exchanges that shaped European art. The Met has long been a treasure trove of cultural history, enriched by various contributions.

Orientation: Stand just a few steps back from the painting "Allegory of the Catholic Faith" by Johannes Vermeer, ensuring you can take in its full scale and intricate details. This vantage point allows the symbolic elements to unfold in their intended grandeur, revealing the complex interplay of faith and iconography.

In the painting "Allegory of the Catholic Faith," crafted between 1670 and 1672, Vermeer offers a rich tapestry of Catholic symbolism, unusual for Protestant Holland at the time. The medium of oil on canvas allows for exquisite detail in elements such as the globe, which represents the reach of the Catholic Church, and the serpent crushed under a woman's feet, symbolizing the triumph of faith over heresy. The painting itself resides within the Friedsam Collection, bequeathed to the museum in 1931 by Michael Friedsam. Friedsam, a notable art collector and philanthropist, played a crucial role in enriching The Met's holdings with works that spanned a wide array of cultural and historical contexts. His bequest, much like the earlier donation from Nelson A. Rockefeller, whom you encountered through the Bacchante in a Landscape, underscores the importance of private patronage in shaping the museum's diverse collection. The gallery where this work is displayed bears the name of the Rockefeller Tang, whose contributions have furthered the museum's mission to educate and inspire through the arts. As you stand before Vermeer's work, consider how it reflects the intricate dance between art, religion, and personal conviction during a time of significant religious and political tension. The Metropolitan Museum of Art, through the convergence of such works, invites an exploration of the global influences that have shaped its extensive collections. As you continue your journey, the impact of these diverse contributions on the museum's narrative becomes ever more apparent.

Stop 3: A sketch of the Mitsui shop in Suruga street in Edo

Address: 1000 5th Ave, New York, NY 10028

Coordinates: 40.7794, -73.9623

Orientation: Stand to the right of "A sketch of the Mitsui shop in Suruga street in Edo" by Katsushika Hokusai to catch the full breadth of its bustling scene. From this angle, the depth of the street and the majestic silhouette of Mount Fuji in the background become more vivid, offering insight into both commerce and culture in Edo-period Japan.

This woodblock print, crafted by Hokusai around 1830, depicts the Mitsui Store at Suruga Street in Edo, a vibrant commercial hub of its time, with the iconic Mount Fuji looming in the distance. The piece is part of Hokusai's acclaimed series "Thirty-Six Views of Mount Fuji," where each image features the mountain as a focal point, yet each scene varies dramatically in its depiction of everyday life. The technique of woodblock printing allowed for the mass production of art, making such scenes accessible to a broader audience, a democratization of art that resonated with the Edo period's rising merchant class. Hokusai, renowned for his ability to capture the essence of Japanese life and landscape, utilized fine lines and vivid contrasts in this print to emphasize the bustling activity of Suruga Street and the serene permanence of Mount Fuji. The layering technique employed in this print was not merely an artistic choice but a cultural one — it highlighted the coexistence of nature and commerce, an integral aspect of Edo society. The Rockefeller family's influence on The Met is echoed in this piece's presence within the museum's collection. The Rockefeller legacy has long been intertwined with The Met's global outreach, enhancing its role as a beacon for artistic exchange. In this gallery, named in part after the Qatar family, the Mitsui shop scene also connects to earlier stops like the "Allegory of the Catholic Faith," showcasing how The Met's collection spans diverse cultures and histories.
