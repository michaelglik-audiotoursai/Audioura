Stop 1: Violes gambe by William Turner (Londres, 1652)

Coordinates: 43.6953, 7.2655

Orientation: You are about to explore the Palais Lascaris in Nice, home to a remarkable collection of string instruments. Encounter the Violes gambe by William Turner, Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore, and Harpe by Naderman. These works showcase the evolution of musical expression through strings, reflecting broader cultural shifts. Witness the craftsmanship of renowned makers and delve into the history of music's transformation within these hallowed walls. The Palais Lascaris stands as a testament to the city's rich musical heritage, a treasure trove bequeathed for all to appreciate. Your first stop is Violes gambe by William Turner (Londres, 1652). Stand in the opulent salon of the Palais Lascaris, where the elegance of the room envelops you, and let your gaze fall upon the Violes gambe crafted by William Turner in London in 1652. From this vantage point, the intricate craftsmanship and historical aura of the instrument can truly be appreciated.

Crafted from fine wood, the viol's body reflects the skill and artistry of Turner. As a maker working in Aldgate, London, Turner was renowned for the quality and precision of his instruments, which often featured heart-shaped sound holes that contributed to their rich tonal qualities. The viol’s structure, with its flat back and carved scroll, is designed to resonate with deep, rich tones—a feature coveted by 17th-century musicians seeking expressive depth in their performances. During its time at the Palais Lascaris, this viol has been an integral part of concerts held in the palace's salon, connecting modern audiences with the musical traditions of the past. As string instruments like this viol crossed borders into Europe, they evolved, influencing and being influenced by local musical practices. The viol's history is not just one of craftsmanship, but of the diffusion and transformation of musical culture.

Stop 2: Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore (Milan, 1696)

Coordinates: 43.6953, 7.2655

Orientation: Stand before the Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore, crafted in Milan in 1696. Observe the rich, aged wood and the intricate craftsmanship that defines this instrument, inviting you to reflect on the artistry of the Baroque period.

 Among these treasures is the Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore, a testament to the craftsmanship of the Testore family, whose legacy in instrument making began with Carlo Giuseppe Testore, a student of Giovanni Grancino. This instrument now resides in the grandeur of the Palais Lascaris, a palace that was originally the residence of the Lascaris-Vintimille family before being transformed into a museum. This transformation, following its designation as a historical monument in 1946, provided a fitting home for Gautier's collection. The Basse de violon embodies the ornate and expressive qualities of the Baroque era, evident in its design and acoustic potential. The Testore workshop, established in Milan, was renowned for producing instruments that were both visually appealing and capable of producing the deep, resonant tones integral to the music of the time. Paolo Antonio Testore, active from around 1700 to 1767, was pivotal in continuing this family tradition, crafting violins and basses that were sought after across Europe. The Palais Lascaris itself underwent significant alterations in the eighteenth century, aligning its architectural transformation with the evolving tastes of its era. This backdrop enhances the historical narrative of the Basse de violon, allowing visitors to appreciate not just the instrument's aesthetic and functional qualities, but also its place within the broader tapestry of musical evolution. As you consider the Basse de violon, contemplate how the pursuit of elegance and expression in music evolved as the world approached the 18th century.

Stop 3: Harpe by Naderman (Paris, 1780)

Coordinates: 43.6953, 7.2655

Orientation: As you stand before "Harpe by Naderman (Paris, 1780)," position yourself to appreciate the full height of this elegant instrument. From this vantage, the intricate woodwork and craftsmanship become apparent, revealing details you might otherwise miss.

Consequently, the Palais Lascaris, a former aristocratic residence, became the home to this remarkable collection, including the 1780 single-action pedal harp crafted by Jean-Henri Naderman in Paris. The harp stands at 161.5 cm, its wood structure adorned with delicate gilded accents that catch the light. This single-action pedal harp, a precursor to today's complex double-action mechanisms, allowed musicians to change pitches using a pedal system. Jean-Henri Naderman's craftsmanship is evident in the harp's elegant design and precise mechanism. This connection between the instrument and its surroundings offers a glimpse into the opulent lifestyle of the past, before the sweeping changes brought about by revolution and modernization. As Europe transitioned into a new era, the fate of such exquisite instruments hung in the balance.
