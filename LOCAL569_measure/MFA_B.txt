Stop 2: Sons of Liberty Bowl

Address: 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3399, -71.0942

Orientation: Ns of Liberty Bowl is crafted by Paul Revere in 1768, and observe its gleaming silver surface, which captures the light and the spirit of rebellion it was designed to commemorate. From this vantage point, you can appreciate the intricate engravings that honor the courage of those who defied British rule.

In 1768, a pivotal moment in American resistance history unfolded when members of the Sons of Liberty, a group of Massachusetts insurgents, commissioned Paul Revere, the silversmith and midnight rider of 1768, Jr. to create a silver bowl. The bowl commemorates ninety-two members of Representatives who courageously stood by their protest letter against the Townshend Acts, which levied taxes on essential goods like tea and paper. In 1949, the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston acquired this emblematic piece through the contributions of seven hundred donations, a communal effort involving schoolchildren and citizens alike. The Sons of Liberty Bowl is crafted from silver, a choice material that was not only precious but also practical for engraving. Its surface bears the engraved names of the defiant lawmakers, serving as an eternal testament to their bravery. The use of silver, known for its luster and durability, was a deliberate decision to ensure the bowl's message of resistance would be as enduring as the metal itself. The acquisition of the bowl by the Museum of Fine Arts forms a part of its broader mission to preserve and present the rich tapestry of American history and culture. As you reflect on the Sons of Liberty Bowl, consider how personal identity and collective action shape artistic legacies, a theme that resonates through the museum's diverse collection, from Ancient Nubia to the birth of a nation.

Stop 3: John Singer Sargent’s  The Daughters of Edward Darley Boit

Address: 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3399, -71.0942

Orientation: As you stand before John Singer Sargent’s "The Daughters of Edward Darley Boit," position yourself to the left of the gallery for a perspective that draws you into the depth of the spacious room, accentuating the scale and atmosphere of this evocative scene.

In 1882, Edward Darley Boit commissioned Sargent to paint a portrait of his daughters, capturing a moment in their young lives. This collaboration initiated the creation of Sargent's renowned work, "The Daughters of Edward Darley Boit," which became a significant piece in the artist's oeuvre. This act of generosity ensured the artwork's preservation and public display, allowing it to become an enduring part of the museum's collection, accessible to countless visitors over the decades. The painting itself is a large oil on canvas, depicting four young girls dressed in white, their delicate figures contrasting with the shadowy interior of the Boit family's Paris apartment. Sargent's decision to employ a composition that merges portraiture with a sense of narrative space was both bold and innovative for its time. The girls are arranged in a way that suggests a casual spontaneity, yet each one is captured with precise individuality, reflecting their distinct personalities. As you observe the painting, consider the role of the Horticultural gallery patron in making this viewing possible; their contributions have shaped the space in which these narratives thrive. Furthermore, the story of the Boit sisters donating the work adds an additional layer to the museum's focus on philanthropy and artistic enrichment. This commitment resonates throughout the gallery, much like the Bartlett Donation enabled the acquisition of key works such as the amphora and Mantiklos 'Apollo' you've encountered earlier. In embracing these narratives, the museum not only preserves art but also enriches its history and identity, affirming its dedication to cultural storytelling.
