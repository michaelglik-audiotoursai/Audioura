Stop 2: Sons of Liberty Bowl

Address: 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3392, -71.0942

Orientation: Stand close to the Sons of Liberty Bowl, allowing your eyes to trace the intricate engravings that adorn its polished silver surface. From this vantage point, the craftsmanship of Paul Revere becomes palpable, a testament to a time of fervent resistance and artistry.

Crafted in 1768 by the renowned silversmith Paul Revere, the Sons of Liberty Bowl embodies more than just exquisite craftsmanship; it encapsulates a pivotal moment in American history. This silver vessel was created to honor ninety-two members of the Massachusetts House of Representatives who, in defiance of British authority, refused to rescind a protest letter against the oppressive Townshend Acts. These acts imposed taxes on essential goods like tea and paper, stirring unrest across the colonies. This deliberate act of creation marked a harbinger of the revolutionary spirit that would sweep through the American colonies. The bowl's journey to the Museum of Fine Arts in 1949 exemplifies community engagement and collective effort. The acquisition was made possible by contributions from seven hundred donors, including Boston schoolchildren, a gesture that underscores the shared ownership of history. Linde, Art, and Newland House Drawing galleries, named after significant patrons, host this piece, epitomizing the museum's commitment to preserving artifacts with profound historical significance. In 1949, the Museum of Fine Arts in Boston sought to acquire the Sons of Liberty Bowl, a significant piece created by Paul Revere, Jr. The museum initiated a campaign to gather funds for this acquisition, relying on the support of the community. As a result, seven hundred small donations came together to make the purchase possible. This collective effort allowed the bowl to become a permanent part of the museum's collection, where it remains an icon of American history. The narrative of the Sons of Liberty Bowl connects to the museum's broader mission, which is further highlighted by the Modern collections housed within the same institution. This revolutionary spirit resonates with the echoes of change and defiance captured in works across the gallery, reminding visitors of art's enduring power to reflect and inspire societal transformation.

Address: 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Orientation: As you stand before John Singer Sargent's "The Daughters of Edward Darley Boit," notice how the dark, expansive background contrasts with the ethereal whites of the girls' dresses. Their positioning within the foreground of this nearly square canvas reveals a tableau that is both intimate and mysterious, drawing you into their world.

The Museum of Fine Arts in Boston acquired this extraordinary piece as a gift from Mary Louisa Boit. Her generous donation brought Sargent's 1882 painting from the private realm into the public domain, allowing countless visitors to experience its enigmatic charm. Edward Darley Boit, an American collector and the girls’ father, commissioned Sargent to capture his daughters, resulting in a significant piece that blurs the lines between portraiture and genre painting. Boit himself was deeply embedded in the expatriate community in Paris during the 19th century, a milieu that influenced both the aesthetics and subjects of Sargent’s work. The painting's transfer was more than just an addition to the museum’s collection. It exemplified the profound impact that donations can have on cultural institutions, echoing the contributions of others like Jessie, who also played a vital role in enriching the museum’s offerings. The painting resides in the Art of the Americas galleries, surrounded by works that span 3,000 years, from Mesoamerican civilizations to modern American art, reflecting the museum's dedication to displaying a diverse tapestry of artistic narratives. Each of the Boit daughters is captured in a moment of introspection, their expressions ranging from contemplative to distant. This nuanced portrayal became possible through Sargent’s masterful manipulation of light and shadow, which creates a compelling tension between visibility and obscurity. The painting's placement in this gallery underscores its connection to both American identity and artistic evolution. Ultimately, the legacy of Edward Darley Boit's commission and Mary Louisa's donation demonstrates the ongoing influence of individual patrons in shaping the museum's collection, a theme that reverberates throughout the institution's history.
