Step-by-Step Audio Guided Tour: Palais Lascaris, Nice, France - Museum Tour
Tour-Category: museum

Stop 1: Violes gambe by William Turner (Londres, 1652)

Coordinates: 43.6954, 7.2652

Orientation: You are about to explore the historical collection at Palais Lascaris in Nice. Encounter the Violes gambe by William Turner, Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore, and the Harpe by Naderman. These three works showcase the evolution of European string instruments and their impact on musical expression. The transfer of the historical musical instrument collections to Palais Lascaris in 2001 led to the opening of the music museum in 2011, offering a glimpse into the rich cultural heritage of the instruments displayed here. Your first stop is Violes gambe by William Turner (Londres, 1652). As you stand before the exquisite violes gambe by William Turner, crafted in London in 1652, its elegance and craftsmanship call to the soul of music lovers gathered within the historic walls of Palais Lascaris. Positioned in a serene alcove, the instrument invites quiet reflection on its deep history and significance.

Turner, primarily lauded for his painting, ventured into crafting musical instruments, creating this violes gambe during his career from 1647 to 1656. This particular instrument found its home at the Palais Lascaris due to the dedication of Antoine Gautier, a passionate collector and amateur musician born in Nice in 1825. Gautier, who played the violin and the viola, bequeathed his entire collection of ancient musical instruments to the museum in 1901. This generous act included Turner's violes gambe, transforming the museum into a celebrated repository of musical heritage. The donation ensured that the Palais Lascaris became home to France's second most important collection of historic musical instruments, only surpassed by the Musée de la Musique de la Philharmonie in Paris. The violes gambe's presence in the museum’s collection echoes the cultural and historical significance that these instruments once held in European music. Enthusiasts and scholars visiting the museum are drawn to this particular piece for its rarity and connection to the artistic soul of the Baroque era. Turner's foray into instrument making highlights a unique intersection of his visual artistry and musical craftsmanship, offering a glimpse into the versatility that characterized 17th-century artistic endeavors. In the tranquil environs of the Palais Lascaris, the violes gambe stands as a bridge connecting us to the rich, melodic past that shaped musical traditions for generations.

Directions: Continue through Palais Lascaris — next is Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore (Milan, 1696).

Stop 2: Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore (Milan, 1696)

Coordinates: 43.6954, 7.2652

Orientation: Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore is crafted in 1696 in Milan. From here, the subtle details of its form and the precise craftsmanship become discernible, revealing not only the instrument's physical beauty but its story across the centuries.

The Basse de violon, crafted by Paolo Antonio Testore, represents a significant chapter in the Testore family's legacy—a lineage steeped in the art of violin making since the late 17th century. This particular instrument, with its back composed of two pieces of tightly waved maple, showcases the meticulous attention to detail that distinguished the Testore family's creations. The Testore workshop, originally established by Carlo Giuseppe, the first violin maker in the family, was renowned for producing instruments of exceptional quality, attracting musicians who sought instruments that could produce deeper, more resonant tones. As a result, the Basse de violon remains a key piece in understanding the innovations that pushed the boundaries of sound, setting the stage for the orchestral music of later centuries. In the late 18th century, a pivotal change in string instrument construction would further revolutionize how strings were played and heard, but it was the groundwork laid by craftsmen like Paolo Antonio Testore that made such advancements possible.

Directions: Your final stop in Palais Lascaris: Harpe by Naderman (Paris, 1780).

Stop 3: Harpe by Naderman (Paris, 1780)

Coordinates: 43.6954, 7.2652

Orientation: As you stand here, let your eyes rest upon the Harpe by Naderman (Paris, 1780), situated gracefully in the intimate setting of the Palais Lascaris.

This harp, crafted in Paris around 1780, is part of the museum's collection in Nice, France. It is a single-action pedal harp, a design innovation that allowed musicians to change keys more fluidly during performances. This technical advancement was particularly significant for composers and performers of the time, providing them with new creative possibilities. Standing 161.5 cm tall, the harp’s intricate details come alive in the dim light of the room, echoing the sophistication and opulence of Parisian society during the Enlightenment. Culturally, this harp links directly back to the Viscountess of Beaumont, to whom it once belonged. Imagine the salons of Paris in the late 1700s, where such an instrument would have been a centerpiece, providing both auditory and visual pleasure. The harp's presence here at Palais Lascaris not only enriches the collection but also serves as a bridge to the past, connecting visitors to a time when music was a central pillar of cultural and social life. The harp studio at the Paris Conservatory, associated with François Joseph Naderman, Jean-Henri's son, underscores the family’s enduring legacy in the world of music. This initiative at the Conservatory marked a shift toward formalized musical education, influencing generations of harpists and ensuring the instrument's continued evolution. As you reflect on this harp, consider the legacy it left for future musical generations, as both an object of beauty and a catalyst for change in the musical landscape.

That's 3 stops — Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore, a key piece in sound innovation and Harpe by Naderman, significant for composers with new creative possibilities.
