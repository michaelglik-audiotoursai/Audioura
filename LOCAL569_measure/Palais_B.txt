Tour-Category: museum

Stop 1: Violes gambe by William Turner (Londres, 1652)

Coordinates: 43.6954, 7.2692

Orientation: You are about to explore the Palais Lascaris in Nice, home to a remarkable collection of musical instruments. Encounter the Violes gambe by William Turner, Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore, and Harpe by Naderman. These masterpieces showcase the evolution of musical craftsmanship across different eras. Witness the impact of these exquisite instruments on European society and culture, as the museum's transformation from a palace to a music museum highlights the fusion of artistry and history within its walls. Your first stop is Violes gambe by William Turner (Londres, 1652). Stand near the intricately carved wooden frame of the Violes gambe by William Turner, crafted in London in 1652. From this vantage point, the delicate craftsmanship and heart-shaped motifs on the viol come vividly to life, revealing the skill and artistry of its creator.

In the grand setting of the Palais Lascaris, a historical monument restored by the city of Nice after its purchase in 1942, the Violes gambe crafted by William Turner in 1652 holds a remarkable place. This instrument is part of an extensive collection of over 500 musical instruments, second only to the Musée de la Musique de la Philharmonie in Paris. Turner's viol, known for its distinctive heart-shaped motifs, represents a blend of artistry and musical innovation that characterized 17th-century English luthiery. The violas da gamba, including Turner's, were pivotal in shaping the soundscape of intimate chamber music, resonating with the rich acoustic heritage of the period. Turner, primarily celebrated for his painting prowess, is an intriguing figure in the world of musical instrument making. His viols, crafted during a time when he worked in Aldgate, London, are rare and demonstrate his multifaceted talent. These instruments reflect his deep understanding of harmony and structure, qualities that also permeated his visual art. The viol's presence in the Palais Lascaris, once the home of the Lascaris-Vintimille family until 1802, adds layers of cultural and historical context to its significance. The viol's journey to the Palais Lascaris is intertwined with the legacy of Antoine Gautier, a 19th-century collector born in Nice in 1825. Gautier, an amateur musician who founded a quartet with his brother, bequeathed his collection of historical instruments to the museum. This bequest, fueled by his passion for music, laid the foundation for the Palais Lascaris's transformation into a museum that celebrates musical heritage. As music transitioned from chambers to grander halls, the violas da gamba stand as a testament to the evolving demands on string instruments. This evolution prompts us to ponder what future innovations in string instruments might arise to meet the challenges of modern musical settings.

Directions: Continue through Palais Lascaris — next is Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore (Milan, 1696).

Stop 2: Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore (Milan, 1696)

Coordinates: 43.6954, 7.2692

Orientation: Stand back slightly to take in the full elegance of the Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore, crafted in 1696. From this vantage, you can appreciate its intricate contours and imagine the rich, resonant sounds that have echoed through centuries.

The Basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore, created in Milan in 1696, marks the emergence of Testore as a notable violin maker. Though primarily active from around 1700, Testore's early craftsmanship is evident here, demonstrating his skill and potential at the cusp of a distinguished career. His father, Carlo Giuseppe Testore, founded the Testore workshop in Milan, having learnt from Giovanni Grancino, a renowned craftsman of the time. This family tradition of excellence in instrument making, passed from father to son, significantly shaped the Italian craftsmanship that became influential across Europe. A bass viol by Paolo Antonio Testore, similar to the violes gambe by William Turner you saw earlier, enriches the collection at Palais Lascaris with its historical significance and intricate design. In 2001, the city's historical musical instrument collections, including this Basse de violon, were transferred to the Palais Lascaris as part of a project to transform the venue into a music museum. This transition to Palais Lascaris, open to the public since 2011, provides a tangible link to Testore's craftsmanship and ensures its appreciation by future generations. As public concerts gained popularity in the late 18th century, the demand for robust and resonant instruments like Testore's Basse de violon increased, influencing subsequent designs and ensuring a lasting impact on music history. This instrument, alongside others in the collection, offers insight into the evolution of baroque music and the pivotal role of Italian craftsmen in shaping the soundscapes of their time.

Directions: Your final stop in Palais Lascaris: Harpe by Naderman (Paris, 1780).

Stop 3: Harpe by Naderman (Paris, 1780)

Coordinates: 43.6954, 7.2692

Orientation: As you stand here, notice the exquisite craftsmanship of the Harpe by Naderman (Paris, 1780), taking in the intricate details that speak to a bygone era of elegance and musical sophistication.

The harp now enriches the museum's cultural heritage, offering visitors a glimpse into the Louis XVI period. Jean-Henri Naderman, a distinguished harp maker of his time, crafted this single-action pedal harp with a precision that highlights the delicate artistry of the late 18th century. The instrument is constructed from painted and gilded wood and metal, details that underscore Naderman’s reputation for combining aesthetic beauty with functional innovation. The harp was once owned by the Viscountess of Beaumont, linking it to the sophisticated salons of French aristocracy, where music was not just entertainment but a status symbol. What sets this harp apart is Naderman's ingenuity in design. At a time when musical instruments were evolving, his craftsmanship embodied both the technical advancements and the artistic flair desired by the Parisian elite. The harp’s presence in the refined salons of Paris symbolized cultural refinement, much like the basse de violon by Paolo Antonio Testore you observed earlier, which spoke to the rich traditions of Italian craftsmanship. As you reflect on the journey of this harp, consider how the grandeur woven into its strings resonates with the elegance of 18th-century France, setting the stage for its lasting legacy.
