In the Linde Family Gallery at the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, stand directly in front of Joan Miró's "Le Lézard aux plumes d’or," and let your eyes trace the contours of the lithographs. From this vantage, the vivid interplay of colors and textures on the Louis Broder's vellum becomes strikingly apparent, revealing the fusion of artistic and literary elements.

Published in 1971 by the renowned Louis Broder, this work contains 40 color lithographs, each printed on Japan paper, which adds a tactile richness to the visual experience. Broder, known for his intimate editions, commissioned Miró to explore the boundaries of visual storytelling, resulting in a piece that seamlessly integrates image and narrative. This collaboration with Broder highlights the artist's dedication to crafting books as unified art forms, where the text and images dance across the pages in harmony. The book's presence at the museum, gifted by Boris Fridman, underscores the importance of such works in the broader collection of printed art. Miró's ability to transform a mythical creature into a surreal narrative invites viewers to consider the ancient stories that might have inspired its transformation.

Orientation: "Moses and Monotheism" and allow your gaze to fall on the sheepskin is where Salvador Dalí's illustrations breathe life into Sigmund Freud's controversial text. From this vantage point, observe how the visual narratives and typographic elements merge, capturing the seismic shift from polytheism to monotheism.

In 1939, Sigmund Freud, the father of psychoanalysis, authored "Moses and Monotheism," a daring exploration of religious origins. Decades later, in 1974, Salvador Dalí brought Freud's provocative ideas to life through a series of ten illustrations. The consequence of Dalí's innovative approach is vividly apparent as his surreal interpretations dance across the pages, each stroke imbuing Freud’s words with a renewed, haunting resonance. Dalí, who once sketched Freud in London, channels his admiration for the psychoanalyst into these illustrations, bridging the worlds of art and psychology. This work joins the broader collection here, echoing the transformative journey of Le Lézard aux plumes d’or, another milestone in the evolution of art. As the story of Moses unfolds, one is left to ponder how such transformations continue to ripple through to the present day.

Stop 3: Au Soleil du Plafond by Juan Gris

Address: 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3396, -71.0897

Orientation: "Au Soleil du Plafond," a captivating work crafted by the Spanish artist Juan Gris in collaboration with French poet Pierre Reverdy, was published in 1955 by Éditions Verve. This livre d'artiste positions itself as a poetic exploration of visual and textual interplay, having survived as a distinct legacy of their creative partnership.

Juan Gris, a key figure in the Cubist movement, collaborated closely with Pierre Reverdy, a french poet associated with surrealism, to merge visual art with poetic expression. Together, they created a series of eleven lithographs printed on Arches paper, known for its quality and durability. Tériade, a respected publisher dedicated to collaborative art books, commissioned Gris for this project, emphasizing the potential of such works to connect artistic disciplines. The lithographs, meticulously printed by Mourlot Frères, a renowned atelier in Paris, demonstrate how Gris and Reverdy’s collaboration elevated the book form to an artistic masterpiece. Reverdy's lyrical texts, lithographed in harmony with Gris's visuals, create a seamless fusion of word and image, supporting the exhibition’s thesis that the livre d'artiste model revolutionized traditional roles for artists and writers. This collaboration heralded a new era for books as integrated artistic entities. By bringing "Au Soleil du Plafond" to life, Gris and Reverdy established a synthesis that transformed the way we perceive and value artist books.As this piece sits within the MFA, it connects to the museum's broader mission to celebrate artistic revolutions, reminding us that books can serve as powerful canvases for extraordinary intersections of art and literature.
