Joan Miró's "Le Lézard aux plumes d’or" is a masterful example of the illustrated book, a genre that thrives on collaboration. In 1971, Louis Broder, a renowned publisher, commissioned Miró to create this work, knowing the Spanish artist's ability to merge visual art with poetic narrative. Broder, who specialized in limited edition books, orchestrated a meticulous production process where the artist, poet, and printer worked closely together, often at the same atelier. This approach ensured that every element, from the typography to the placement of the 40 color lithographs, was carefully considered to enhance the overall impact of the book. The book's pages, printed on Japan paper and bound in publisher’s vellum, are alive with Miró's signature abstract forms and vibrant hues. The choice of materials adds a tactile quality that draws the viewer into the surreal world Miró envisioned. The lithographs themselves are not just illustrations but integral parts of the narrative, interacting with the text to evoke a dreamlike journey. Boris Fridman, a passionate collector of artist's books, gifted this work to the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston. His donation has significantly enriched the museum's collection, emphasizing the importance of the livre d'artiste as a medium where image and text coalesce into a singular artistic expression. This work exemplifies the exhibition's argument that such collaborations transformed books into unique art forms, blurring the lines between literature and visual art.In this way, "Le Lézard aux plumes d’or" is not simply a book, but a vivid exploration of the boundaries between reality and imagination.

Orientation: Stand beneath "Au Soleil du Plafond" and observe the interplay between light and the colors of the lithographs. This angle allows you to appreciate how the vibrant images synchronize with the flowing script, creating a unique visual dialogue.

This collaboration was brought to fruition through the efforts of Tériade, a renowned publisher known for his commitment to the art book as a unique form. Tériade commissioned the work, resulting in a set of 11 lithographs printed on Arches paper, which capture Gris's geometric mastery. These lithographs were meticulously printed by Mourlot Frères, a prestigious Parisian printer famed for their precise lithographic techniques. The work exemplifies the exhibition’s thesis that such books are not merely containers for art or literature, but artworks in themselves, integrating words and images into a singular artistic expression. As you reflect on this piece, consider the role of light, both as a subject within the lithographs and as an element that animates them, prompting us to contemplate its significance in our perception of the divine.

Stop 3: Moses and Monotheism by Salvador Dalí

Address: Avenue of the Arts, 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3395, -71.0877

Orientation: ** As you stand here, direct your attention to the set of ten illustrations on sheepskin, positioned to catch the subtleties of their texture. Notice how the lighting accentuates the interplay between image and text, drawing you into a narrative that might be missed from afar.

Dalí chose this project not only because he was intrigued by Freud’s work, but especially since he considered Freud’s exploration of monotheism a catalyst for broader religious and cultural dialogues. The outcome was a fusion of surrealist art with psychoanalytic theory, each image delving into Freud's provocative ideas and adding layers of visual complexity. Sigmund Freud, known for pioneering psychoanalysis, authored "Moses and Monotheism" in 1939. Within its pages, he suggested that Moses was not a Hebrew but an Egyptian nobleman. This claim challenged traditional narratives and sparked fierce debates in both the psychological and religious communities. Freud’s audacious hypotheses created the foundation upon which Dalí could weave his artistic interpretations, making these illustrations a visual conversation between two towering intellects. Together, Dalí's and Freud's collaboration exemplifies the exhibition's thesis that artists and authors revolutionized the book as an integrated art form. By engaging directly with such challenging material, Dalí advanced the art of the livre d'artiste, where text and image are inseparable.Looking ahead, this work provokes questions about our understanding of cultural narratives and the lasting impact of reimagining history through art.
