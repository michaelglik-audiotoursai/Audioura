Your first stop is Le Lézard aux plumes d’or (The Lizard with Golden Feathers). From this vantage point, you can see the interplay of vivid colors in Joan Miró's lithographs, allowing the mythical and natural themes to converge in bold and fantastical imagery.

Le Lézard aux plumes d’or, created in 1971 by Joan Miró, unfolds in an illustrated book with 40 color lithographs printed on publisher’s vellum. This work was commissioned by Louis Broder, who specialized in producing limited editions that required the close cooperation of artists and printers. The collaboration with Miró emphasized a harmony of image and poetry, advancing Broder's aim to revolutionize the book as an integrated work of art. Later donated this remarkable piece to the MFA, enriching the museum's collection with this vivid expression of the livre d'artiste tradition. In this context, Fridman's donation complements the museum's holdings, illustrating how the fusion of visual art and text propels the exhibition's thesis on the transformative potential of collaborative bookmaking.

Stop 3: Moses and Monotheism

Address: Avenue of the Arts, 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3395, -71.0896

Orientation: As you stand in front of the display for "Moses and Monotheism," take a moment to appreciate the unique perspective from which Dalí's illustrations leap into view, offering an unexpected surreal twist to Freud's daring theories on religious transformation. From this angle, the delicate interplay between text and image invites deeper insight into the artistic collaboration.

In a collaborative venture that epitomizes the fusion of text and illustration, Salvador Dalí created a set of ten drypoints and lithographs on sheepskin in 1974 for Sigmund Freud’s "Moses and Monotheism." Dalí’s decision to work on sheepskin was not merely an artistic choice but a symbolic one, enhancing Freud's exploration of the primal roots of monotheism. By opting for this material, Dalí intended to foster a tactile connection to ancient traditions, making Freud's complex theories more palpable to the observer. Freud, the father of psychoanalysis, had authored this provocative work in 1939, suggesting that Moses was born into an Egyptian household, a notion that challenged prevailing religious narratives and caused considerable controversy. Such works push the boundaries of artistic expression, influencing how we perceive the intersections of art, history, and belief.
