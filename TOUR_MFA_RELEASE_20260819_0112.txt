Louis Broder, a dedicated publisher, brought Joan Miró's remarkable vision to life by publishing "Le Lézard aux plumes d’or" in 1971. This illustrated book, featuring forty color lithographs, represented a significant artistic endeavor. Broder's commitment to the project ensured that Miró's vibrant fusion of visual art and poetic expression reached a wider audience. Through this publication, Broder played a crucial role in preserving and promoting Miró's distinctive artistic style. Boris Fridman, a passionate collector, donated this exceptional work to the Museum of Fine Arts in Boston, ensuring it would become a pivotal part of the museum's prestigious collection. This generous gift from Fridman allows visitors to engage with a key moment in modern art history. Displayed in the Linde Family gallery, the work is carefully situated within a broader narrative of modern art, inviting reflection on the dynamic collaboration between artists and publishers. Stop 2: Au Soleil du Plafond by Juan Gris

Address: Avenue of the Arts, 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3395, -71.0876

Orientation: Look for Au Soleil du Plafond in the galleries.

Gris embarked on a project titled "Au Soleil du Plafond," collaborating with French poet Pierre Reverdy. Gris's untimely death in 1927 at the age of 40 left the project incomplete. In 1955, the Louis Broder Tériade brought the project to fruition, using lithographs based on Gris’s works, thus ensuring Gris's vision was finally shared with the world. "Au Soleil du Plafond," a collection of 11 lithographs printed on Arches wove paper, exemplifies the transformative potential of the livre d'artiste. Here, Gris’s Cubist forms are interwoven with Reverdy’s poetic musings, creating an intricate dialogue between image and text. The lithographs, brought to life by the skilled hands at Mourlot Frères, a renowned lithography workshop in Paris, reflect a world where geometric abstraction meets lyrical prose. The collaboration between Gris and Reverdy, realized posthumously, advances the exhibition's argument that such partnerships revolutionized the book as an art form, blending visual and literary artistry in one cohesive whole.

Stop 3: Moses and Monotheism by Salvador Dalí

Address: Avenue of the Arts, 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3395, -71.0876

Orientation: As you stand in front of the "Moses and Monotheism" exhibit, take a step back to appreciate the full set of ten illustrations by Salvador Dalí, created for Sigmund Freud's controversial text. This vantage point allows you to see how Dalí's surrealist style weaves seamlessly with Freud's psychoanalytic theories, making the sheepskin medium appear almost ethereal under the exhibit lighting.

In 1938, Salvador Dalí met Sigmund Freud in London to sketch his portrait. Following this encounter, Freud published "Moses and Monotheism" in 1939, which would later inspire Dalí to create a series of illustrations for the book. These illustrations, completed around 1974-75, are part of the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston's exhibition, showcasing Dalí's engagement with Freud's exploration of religion and psychoanalysis. Dalí's illustrations, printed on sheepskin, form a set of ten that challenge traditional book art boundaries. The choice of sheepskin — a material often associated with sacred texts — enriches the mystical and religious themes Freud explores. Dalí's illustrations are not mere accompaniments but integral components of the book's narrative, enhancing Freud's exploration of the origins of monotheism through a surreal lens. This work exemplifies the exhibition's thesis: it revolutionizes the book as an art form through the collaboration of Dalí and Freud, merging images, words, and typography into a cohesive whole. The fusion of Dalí's illustrations and Freud's narrative has a lasting impact on our understanding of creation.
