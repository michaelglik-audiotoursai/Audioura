Stop 1: Crystal Lake

Address: Crystal Lake, Newton, MA 02459

Coordinates: 42.3255, -71.2015

Type/Specialty: Natural Lake

Specific Examples: Scenic views, walking trails, wildlife

Orientation: You are about to embark on a cycling journey through a loop from Crystal Lake to Paul Revere via Commonwealth Avenue Mall & Boston Common, MA. The route spans approximately 14 km from the serene waters of Crystal Lake to the historic Paul Revere House. As you pedal through coastal paths and urban gardens, each stop unveils the evolution of Boston, from the oldest city park in the U.S. at Boston Common to the colonial home of American patriot Paul Revere. Uncover over 300 years of history, from Puritan settlers to revolutionary heroes, in a city where past and present intertwine in a delicate dance. Your first stop is Crystal Lake. Head southwest on Charles St, then continue straight onto Beacon St — Marvel at the peaceful surroundings as you approach Crystal Lake. As you ride your bike along the tranquil edge of Crystal Lake in Newton, take a moment to notice the gentle ripple of water lapping against the sandy shore. The lake's serene surface creates an inviting atmosphere, yet its history tells of an unexpected industrial past.

In the mid-19th century, this picturesque body of water underwent a transformation from its original name, Baptist Pond, to the more enticing Crystal Lake. This renaming wasn't just about aesthetics; it was a strategic marketing move aimed at promoting the purity of the ice harvested from its surface. At a time when clean ice was a precious commodity, essential for preserving food and medicine, the new name suggested an image of pristine, disease-free ice. This clever rebranding bolstered the local ice industry, embedding the lake’s new identity into the community’s economic fabric. As you continue your tour, you’ll soon reach Commonwealth Avenue, a boulevard with its own unique tales. In the early 1910s, this elegant avenue witnessed a series of brazen high-speed motorized robberies. Socialites, targeted for their wealth, fell victim to armed robbers who used the speed of the burgeoning automobile age to their advantage, making their getaways toward the Newton town line. This criminal innovation marked a turning point in the history of crime in the area, prompting law enforcement to adapt to the capabilities and challenges presented by cars. Reflecting on these stories, Crystal Lake and Commonwealth Avenue reveal a dynamic past that contrasts sharply with their peaceful present. The lake, once a bustling center of ice commerce, now offers a serene retreat, while the avenue, once synonymous with danger and thrill, stands as a tree-lined path of urban elegance. As you pedal away, the lively heart of Boston beckons, promising further tales of the people and events that have shaped this vibrant region.

Directions: Head southeast from Crystal Lake and bike along Beacon Street. Enjoy the scenic ride through Coolidge Corner and pedal towards Kenmore Square. Continue riding along Commonwealth Avenue Mall until you reach your destination.

Stop 2: Commonwealth Avenue Mall

Address: Commonwealth Ave, Boston, MA 02116

Coordinates: 42.3510, -71.0736

Type/Specialty: Urban Park

Specific Examples: Tree-lined promenade, statues, benches

Orientation: As you glide along the tree-lined paths of Commonwealth Avenue Mall, take a moment to pause near the intersection of Dartmouth Street.

In 1887, a notable chapter of this grand boulevard's history was written when Eben Norton Horsford, a chemist with a fascination for Norse lore, commissioned a statue of Leif Erikson. Horsford was convinced that the legendary Viking settlement of Norumbega was located along the Charles River. This belief led him to commemorate Erikson, who he regarded as a significant figure in America's pre-Columbian history. The statue stands proudly at Charlesgate East, capturing Erikson’s adventurous spirit and Horsford's dream to cement Viking history within the American narrative. Commonwealth Avenue Mall holds more than just tales of distant voyages. In June 1962, the tranquility of this promenade was shattered by the chilling presence of the 'Boston Strangler'. Two victims, Mary Mullen and Nina Nichols, were found along this very stretch, sparking fear and hysteria throughout Boston. This crime spree left the city on edge, altering the serene fabric of this historic avenue. As you stand here, the weight of these stories is palpable. The avenue, inspired by the great boulevards of Paris, is more than a path through the city; it is a corridor through time, lined with statues that silently witness the unfolding of Boston's history. Pedal onwards, and the stories of Boston Common await—older than the city itself, they promise to deepen your journey's narrative.

Directions: Hop on your bike and pedal down Commonwealth Avenue Mall towards Arlington Street. Ride past the Public Garden and continue along the bike-friendly paths until you reach Boston Common. Enjoy the scenic route and the lush greenery as you cycle through the heart of Boston!

Stop 3: Boston Common

Address: 139 Tremont St, Boston, MA 02111

Coordinates: 42.3554, -71.0656

Type/Specialty: Public Park

Specific Examples: Pond, historic sites, swan boats

Orientation: As you stand here amidst the verdant expanse of Boston Common, look around at the 50 acres of history enveloping you, bounded by Tremont, Park, Beacon, Charles, and Boylston Streets. This park is part of the Emerald Necklace, a chain of parks that extends through Boston, connecting urban life with nature.

Boston Common is more than just a park; it is a historical canvas woven with tales of profound significance. Established in 1634, it holds the title of the oldest city park in the United States. Among its storied past is the tragic duel that occurred here on the night of July 3, 1728. Henry Phillips and Benjamin Woodbridge, two men embroiled in conflict, met under the veil of night. Their disagreement ended with Woodbridge's death, causing Phillips to flee to France. Another poignant episode unfolded on June 1, 1660, when Mary Dyer was publicly hanged on these grounds for her Quaker beliefs. Her execution was not just a local event but echoed across the Atlantic, prompting King Charles II to intervene, ultimately halting further executions of Quakers in the colony. Dyer's sacrifice became a pivotal moment in the struggle for religious freedom, sparking changes that would eventually shape the core values of what would become the United States. The Common also witnessed a grim discovery in 1895. As construction crews worked on North America's first underground subway system at Boylston and Tremont streets, they unearthed over 900 skeletal remains, a reminder of the many lives that have intersected with this historic land. Today, Boston Common continues to serve as a vital green space, where history and modern life converge. As you move forward from here, carry with you the weight of these stories, each a thread in the rich tapestry of Boston's past.

Directions: As you leave Boston Common, pedal along the scenic Commonwealth Avenue Mall towards Arlington Street. Ride past the gorgeous Victorian-era brownstones and continue towards the Boston Public Garden. Once you reach the Garden, take a leisurely bike ride around the iconic Swan Boats and lush greenery. Enjoy the tranquil surroundings before continuing your cycling adventure.

Stop 4: Boston Public Garden

Address: 4 Charles St, Boston, MA 02116

Coordinates: 42.3553, -71.0696

Type/Specialty: Public Garden

Specific Examples: Swan boats, statues, flower beds

Orientation: As your bike glides off the bustling Commonwealth Avenue Mall, where you paused earlier, you enter the serene embrace of the Boston Public Garden. This verdant expanse, once a muddy expanse of Back Bay, is bounded by the historic streets of Charles, Beacon, Arlington, and Boylston, forming a botanical sanctuary that speaks to both nature and history.

In the heart of this garden stands the Ether Monument, erected in 1867. It commemorates the medical breakthrough of ether anesthesia, a discovery shrouded in controversy. Dr. William Morton and Dr. Charles Jackson engaged in a bitter feud over the claim to this transformative medical advance. Morton's public demonstrations of ether's effectiveness revolutionized surgical practice, but Jackson insisted on his own role in the discovery. The garden also bore witness to the determined spirit of Julia Paget in 1878. Following her husband's death, Julia fought to retain the Swan Boats concession despite resistance from city commissioners. Her perseverance ensured that these charming vessels became a beloved feature of the garden, cherished by generations of visitors who glide across the lagoon to this day. In the 1910s, Mayor James Michael Curley proposed the garden's subdivision for private development, sparking an uproar among the residents of Beacon Hill and Back Bay. As you cycle through this historical tapestry, the garden's path leads you onward towards the echoes of Paul Revere's midnight ride, a reminder that every corner of this city holds stories of change and resilience.

Directions: Head out of Boston Public Garden and pedal along the charming Commonwealth Avenue Mall. Enjoy the tree-lined path as you ride towards Boston Common. Continue biking through the historic park until you reach Paul Revere House, soaking in the rich history along the way.

Stop 5: Paul Revere House

Address: 19 N Square, Boston, MA 02113

Coordinates: 42.3634, -71.0534

Type/Specialty: Historic House

Specific Examples: 18th-century house, period furnishings, Revere's silver work

Orientation: As you pedal into the North End, you'll reach 19 North Square, where the Paul Revere House stands as a testament to American history.

The Paul Revere House, a National Historic Landmark and part of the Boston National Historical Park, serves as a living museum, preserving the legacy of its most famous occupant, Paul Revere. Built around 1680, it is the oldest house in downtown Boston and once served as the residence of Paul Revere, a notable American patriot. During his time here, Revere, a silversmith by trade, became entangled in the fervor of revolution. This bold act not only fueled anti-British sentiments but also lined his pockets, showcasing his keen entrepreneurial spirit. The house's history extends beyond the American Revolution. Before Revere's time, the site belonged to the Second Church of Boston's parsonage, which was destroyed in the Great Fire of 1676. After Revere sold the property in 1800, the house transitioned into a sailors' boarding house and later, an immigrant tenement, reflecting the evolving community of the North End. As you stand in its small courtyard, you might imagine Revere preparing for his legendary midnight ride on April 18, 1775. On that night, he famously forgot his riding spurs, reportedly sending his loyal dog back to retrieve them. This detail, while small, humanizes the man behind the legend and connects us to the everyday rhythms of life during Boston's revolutionary past.
