Stop 1: Austrian State Treaty

Address:

Coordinates: 48.2051, 16.3728

Stand in the Marble Hall of the Upper Belvedere, where voices once echoed with the weight of a nation's future. Here, you are at the heart of a momentous event: the signing of the Austrian State Treaty on May 15, 1955. The Belvedere, traditionally a center for art and culture, became a symbol of newfound sovereignty on that spring day. The grandeur of Johann Lucas von Hildebrandt’s Baroque architecture lent gravity to the proceedings, and its selection as the venue underscored its importance.

Following the end of World War II, France, the United Kingdom, the United States, and the Soviet Union divided and occupied Austria into four separate zones. For more than eight years, Cold War tensions stalled diplomatic efforts to reach an agreement. A breakthrough in Moscow in April 1955 finally cleared the way, and on May 15, 1955, representatives from the four Allied powers and Austria signed the Austrian State Treaty at Belvedere Palace. Their actions ended a decade-long occupation, officially recognizing Austria as an independent and democratic state once again. Within these walls, the treaty stipulated the withdrawal of foreign troops, restoring freedom and autonomy to the Austrian people.

This decision had wider geopolitical implications. The Federal People's Republic of Yugoslavia later acceded to the treaty, highlighting its reach and significance. The Austrian State Treaty was a diplomatic achievement that extended beyond Austria's borders, influencing the political landscape of Eastern Europe. As you stand in this hall, imagine the palpable sense of hope and relief that enveloped Austria that day—a nation stepping out from the shadows of war into the light of self-determination.

Stop 2: Belvedere Palace Chapel

Address: Belvedere Palace, 1030 Vienna, Austria

Coordinates: 48.2051, 16.3728

Orientation: Stand before the Belvedere Palace Chapel's altarpiece, a treasure of Baroque ecclesiastical art. From this vantage, the interplay of light and shadow across the painting enhances its dynamic rendering of the Resurrection of Christ, pulling you into the spiritual fervor that once permeated this space.

Prince Eugene of Savoy built the Belvedere palace complex as his summer residence and, within this grand estate, commissioned his own dedicated palace chapel. Architect Johann Lukas von Hildebrandt designed the chapel with exclusivity in mind, reserving it for the secluded worship of Prince Eugene and a select circle of nobility, scholars, and artists. For nearly three decades after its completion in 1723, the Belvedere Palace Chapel served as a private sanctuary, closed to the public. This restricted access only heightened its aura of sanctity and intellectual prestige.

Solimena’s commission was a key addition to the chapel, not merely for embellishment but as a work pivotal to its artistic prestige. This contribution aligned the sacred space with the Baroque art movement, making it a landmark of cultural and religious significance. Scholars and historians have since closely studied the complex iconography chosen for the chapel. Standing here, amid the rich history embedded in these walls, one might ponder the other gatherings, beyond the spiritual, that these walls have quietly witnessed across centuries. Here, history and art converge, echoing the resonant discussions and solemn prayers of its earliest inhabitants.

Stop 3: First Vienna Award

Address:

Coordinates: 48.2051, 16.3728

Orientation: As you stand here, reflect on the distant echo of footsteps and conversations that took place in this very hall, where the First Vienna Award was signed.

On November 2, 1938, German Foreign Minister Joachim von Ribbentrop and Italian Foreign Minister Galeazzo Ciano met at the Belvedere Palace to sign the First Vienna Award. This arbitration, which followed directly after the Munich Agreement's partition of Czechoslovakia, redrew the borders of Central Europe on an ethnic basis. The decision forced Czechoslovakia to surrender more than 11,900 square kilometers of southern Slovakia and Carpathian Ruthenia to Hungary. The First Vienna Award was more than just a diplomatic act; it became a pivotal moment that influenced politics during a volatile period. Its decree served as a poignant reminder of sovereignty and identity lost and regained. Standing in this historic space, we can see how moments like these have shaped the region's narrative, leading to subsequent events that brought both reunifications and divisions that echo into our own time.

This tour highlights the intersection of diplomacy and cultural significance within the walls of Belvedere Palace. Understanding these pivotal moments reveals how the past continues to shape national identity and the political landscape of Central Europe today. That's 3 stops in all.
