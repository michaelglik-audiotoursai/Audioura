Stop 2: Moses and Monotheism by Salvador Dalí

Address: 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3391, -71.0887

Orientation: Stand at the right side of the display case containing "Moses and Monotheism." From this vantage point, the intricate detailing of Dalí's illustrations aligns with the accompanying text, revealing how image and word intertwine.

In 1974 and 1975, Salvador Dalí produced a deluxe illustrated edition of Sigmund Freud’s final 1939 book, Moses and Monotheism. Dalí engraved his designs by drawing directly onto massive plates with a diamond stylus. While some records note that the illustrations were printed on lambskin, others describe the sheets as sheepskin and silk. The loose, unbound folios were then gathered inside acrylic sleeves and enclosed in a velvet case featuring a sculpted bas-relief metal cover depicting Moses. This project marked a significant change in Dalí's approach as he engaged deeply with Freud's exploration of the origins of monotheism. By interpreting Freud's complex psychoanalytic ideas through his unique artistic lens, Dalí produced a series of illustrations that offered a new visual dimension to the text. Sigmund Freud, the founder of psychoanalysis, authored the text, proposing the controversial theory that Moses was an Egyptian priest. Dalí's illustrations became an integral part of the exhibition “Dalí: Disruption and Devotion” at the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, curated in the Torf Gallery, which is named in honor of a significant patron. The Hogarth Press originally published Freud's text in 1939, furthering the collaborative spirit of the livre d'artiste tradition that the exhibition seeks to highlight.

Stop 3: Au Soleil du Plafond by Juan Gris

Address: 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3391, -71.0887

Orientation: "Au Soleil du Plafond," a collaborative work by Juan Gris and Pierre Reverdy, is a fascinating piece within the context of the "Picasso, Miró, Dalí: Unbound" exhibition.

In 1916, art dealer Léonce Rosenberg brought together the poet Pierre Reverdy and the painter Juan Gris to collaborate on an illustrated volume titled Au Soleil du Plafond. The original plan paired twenty of Reverdy's poems with plates by Gris, but work came to an abrupt halt when Gris died of kidney failure in 1927 at just forty years old. Gris left behind only eleven completed lithographs. Nearly thirty years later, publisher Tériade revived and reconceived the abandoned project. When the portfolio finally appeared in 1955, Reverdy issued the text as a tribute to the memory of the friend who had died decades before. The work is composed of 11 lithographs, printed by Mourlot Frères, a renowned Parisian printing house, which was known for its high-quality work and collaborations with prominent artists. Reverdy's text, combined with Gris's visual artistry, exemplifies how images, words, and typography intersect to create a unified whole. This piece reveals Gris's view on the convergence of the earthly and divine, capturing the transcendence of human experience in a celestial tapestry.
