Your first stop is BU Masters Show 2026 | Traces: Pursuing Process. The hush of footsteps on polished floorboards leads you into the Griffin Museum of Photography’s gallery, where BU Masters Show 2026 | Traces: Pursuing Process holds the space with a quiet intensity. Stand a few paces back from the central display wall. From here, the mosaic of images by Ava Harper, Emma Pfannenstiel, Ethan Stumbo, and Nanxi Zhang unfolds as a collective surface—fifty glimpses into the contemporary mind, each framed print carrying the residue of the artist’s pursuit. This vantage allows you to see how the arrangement of photographs echoes the idea of “traces”—each image a fragment, but together forming a larger, unresolved question.

The Griffin Museum of Photography had long been a venue for diverse photographic exhibitions, showcasing both emerging and established artists. As a result, the exhibition not only enriched the museum's offerings but also provided a platform for these artists to engage with a broader audience, reinforcing the museum's role as a pivotal space for photographic exploration. A second layer of the story is woven into the very premise of the exhibition: Traces: Pursuing Process took its inspiration from a British television crime drama, an unlikely source for a fine art photography show. The series, created by Val McDermid and Amelia Bullmore and first aired in December 2019, centers around the methodical search for evidence—every fragment, every overlooked detail a potential key to the truth. For the artists and curators, this connection meant treating the photographic medium itself as a kind of forensic landscape. The consequence was a show that did not seek resolution, but instead foregrounded process, uncertainty, and the act of searching. The works themselves—fifty selected images from over 340 submitted—form a temporary archive, their physical presence in the Griffin’s gallery as precise as the tools of investigation that inspired them. Each print, presented in the standard photographic medium, stands as both record and residue, a material trace of the artist’s engagement with the world. In the context of the Griffin’s wider programming, Traces: Pursuing Process signals a turn toward the future of photography, where the boundaries between art, narrative, and evidence are pried apart, and the act of looking becomes a process of discovery.

Stop 2: Earth, Wind & Fire

Address: 67 Shore Rd, Winchester, MA 01890

Coordinates: 42.4525, -71.1447

Orientation: Stand just beyond the midpoint of the north wall—here, the title “Earth, Wind & Fire” anchors your gaze above a suite of pigment prints, their matte surfaces catching what little light sifts through. From this vantage, the sequence becomes legible as a meditation on the passage of time, each print drawing you across the elemental boundaries that separate earth, air, and flame.

November 1, 2026 marked the opening of “Earth, Wind & Fire”, juried by Executive Director Crista Dix. Dix assembled the exhibition as part of the Griffin Museum’s State of Our Union program, gathering works that dwell on how natural forces—long before their echoes became musical—shape the American landscape and memory. In the weeks leading up to the show, Dix collaborated with her curatorial team to draft a prospectus that named its touchstone: “Long before they were a force in music, earth, wind, and fire was a force of nature.” That sentence became both an invitation and a challenge; its consequence was a call for images that would move beyond mere depiction, embracing a metaphorical tracing of change. The exhibition’s namesake, Maurice White, founded the band Earth, Wind & Fire and distinguished himself by fusing genres—jazz, R&B, soul, funk, disco, pop, Latin, and Afro-pop—to create a new language for popular music. White’s signature approach, as recounted in the prospectus, provided a conceptual anchor for the curators: the exhibition’s name itself was chosen to echo White’s transformation of elemental motifs into artistic innovation. Here, physical pigment prints—unframed and roughly the size of ledger books—replace musical scores, their granular texture recalling the work you encountered in the BU Masters Show 2026 down the corridor, where process and trace became the subject itself. Displayed in a sequence rather than isolation, the prints reveal the passage of time not just as record but as process—wind-blown grasses, scorched trunks, dry riverbeds—each testifying to the way memory persists in matter. One print’s matte surface glances light unevenly across its scarred terrain, emphasizing the photograph as both image and object, an idea reinforced by the curatorial decision to leave glass off the frames. This physical immediacy—materials left vulnerable to the room’s own air—underlines the exhibition’s metaphor: natural forces and human memory endure not in spite of change, but through it. In the end, nature here is not mere background but the very register of persistence.

Stop 3: Tabitha Soren | An Artist Life

Address: 67 Shore Rd, Winchester, MA 01890

Coordinates: 42.4525, -71.1447

Orientation: As you stand in front of "Tabitha Soren | An Artist Life," move just a half-step closer—here, you can glimpse the exhibition panels arranged to showcase the intersection where written narratives from Soren’s keynote appearance meet selected photographic works on loan. This arrangement, best viewed from eye level so that the text and images share your line of sight, reveals how the museum is using wall space itself as both archive and invitation, blurring the boundary between artist’s biography and communal memory.

Tabitha Soren, an American fine-art photographer, was invited to be the keynote speaker for the New England Portfolio Review program at the Griffin Museum of Photography. This invitation marked a significant moment in Soren's relationship with the museum, as she transitioned from being primarily recognized as a former journalist to being celebrated as a fine-art photographer. Her participation in this event underscored the museum's recognition of her artistic contributions and provided her with a platform to engage with a community of photographers. As a result, the Griffin Museum of Photography strengthened its ties with Soren, elevating its status as a venue that supports and showcases contemporary photographers. The details here are subtle—and, frankly, that’s what makes this all the more intriguing. The exhibition "Tabitha Soren | An Artist Life" highlights the impact of Soren’s keynote and the conversations she sparked, alongside selected photographic works. Soren’s presence persists through documentation and the ongoing dialogue she inspired during her keynote, a rarity when most stops, like the photographs in the exhibitions you passed earlier, can be approached as self-contained worlds. Here, what is at stake is the slippery nature of artistic legacy: the emphasis is less on a single object and more on a living connection and a strengthened community. This draws a subtle line between the exhibit and other stops you’ve visited. With Traces: Pursuing Process, you encountered artworks focused on material process; with Earth, Wind & Fire, you walked through elements embodied. Here, process manifests as event—fleeting, but formative—and what remains isn’t glass, paper, or pigment, but the impression of a relationship between artist and institution. The story that unfolds is not about what hangs on the wall, but about the ongoing articulation of identity—an artist staking a claim in a community, and a museum investing in dialogue rather than mere display. The connection between personal history and collective memory, in this case, is ongoing—not what’s preserved behind glass, but what’s set in motion by presence.

Orientation: Stand just across from the largest projection surface, facing toward the central axis of "Lua Kobayashi | The Persistence of Memories." From here, the blend of analog and digital elements—some sharply rendered, others half-seen in shifting light—forms a layered visual field that comes into focus only from this vantage.

Lua Kobayashi's "The Persistence of Memories" found its home at the Griffin Museum of Photography as a result of her participation in the 2025/2026 Cummings Residency. The museum’s curatorial team selected Kobayashi’s multidisciplinary approach for its capacity to cultivate dialogue around memory. The exhibit features traditional and digital art forms, producing an immersive viewing environment. This presence strengthened the museum’s ongoing engagement with contemporary art. While the precise materials remain unlisted, the visible interplay of media echoes the evolving discourse of photographic practice. Arthur Griffin—known as an American photographer—founded this museum to foster experimentation, a legacy extended in Kobayashi’s installation and her focus on memory’s shifting forms.
