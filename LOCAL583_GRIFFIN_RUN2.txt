The curatorial team at the Griffin Museum collaborated with artists Emma Pfannenstiel, Ethan Stumbo, and Nanxi Zhang to distill hundreds of perspectives into a single narrative arc. The result is a mosaic where each work is a trace—of labor, of experimentation, of the artist’s hand. The prints are arranged with careful spacing, allowing each artist’s vision room to breathe. Techniques span both traditional analog and contemporary digital methods, reflecting the artists’ willingness to blur boundaries and test conventions. Ava Harper, Emma Pfannenstiel, Ethan Stumbo, and Nanxi Zhang each bring a distinct sensibility forged through months of work in Boston University’s MFA program, culminating in this joint exhibition at the Griffin Museum. Their inclusion here—out of hundreds—marks a moment of recognition and risk: the acceptance of new visions into the museum’s dialogue with photography. The gallery foregrounds the process, with the traces of making visible in every print. The exhibition’s title, "Traces: Pursuing Process," points directly to this openness. What is at stake is not just the finished image, but the evidence of how it came to be—what remains, what is lost, and what emerges in the act of selection. This approach aligns with the Griffin Museum’s ongoing commitment to showcasing contemporary photographic practices and fostering new voices. Here, the stakes are high: images wrestled from hundreds, methods that risk failure, and the push to depict the world with fresh eyes. This is the pulse of the BU Masters Show 2026—a window into hidden processes and the beginnings of new ways of seeing.

Stop 2: Earth, Wind & Fire

Address: 67 Shore Road, Winchester, MA 01890

Coordinates: 42.4525, -71.137

Orientation: Stand near the central wall where the “Earth, Wind & Fire” exhibition panels converge. From this vantage, the sequence of images is legible as a whole—each photograph aligned to echo the elements in the exhibition’s title. The interplay of arrangement and spacing becomes clear here, revealing patterns that would remain hidden from an oblique angle.

In 1969, Maurice White founded the band Earth, Wind & Fire in Chicago, assembling musicians whose skill would allow him to explore new directions in American popular music. White crafted a sound that drew deliberately on jazz, R&B, soul, funk, and Latin influences—bringing together genres that, until then, were largely kept apart both in radio programming and in the expectations of their audiences. As a direct result, listeners who had previously stayed within one musical tradition encountered a blend that crossed boundaries of style and community, changing the texture of mainstream listening in the United States. White’s leadership extended beyond music; he made crucial decisions about the image and ethos of Earth, Wind & Fire, ensuring that the name itself referenced more than just the band—invoking the ancient elements that underpin the exhibition before you. The consequence of that naming was to tie the group’s artistic output to universal forces, which now echo in the visual language chosen for this exhibition. On these gallery walls, the photographs gathered under “Earth, Wind & Fire” do not illustrate the band, but rather interpret the primordial elements as visual motifs. Each work presents a concrete depiction of either earth, wind, or fire: cracked soil rendered in high-contrast monochrome, swirling smoke frozen mid-motion, or the blurred orange streak of flame in a nightscape. The curatorial decision to sequence these images in triptychs—an echo of the band’s triadic name—reinforces the elemental theme and draws attention to the continuity between different photographic processes. Some images are printed using traditional gelatin silver, their matte surfaces absorbing light, while others use modern archival pigment, their hues calibrated to suggest the intensity or subtlety of the element they depict. White’s choice to found his band at a specific historical moment—when American popular music was undergoing rapid transformation—parallels the act of assembling these photographs at this time, in this place, under this title. Each selection and placement within the exhibition is a quiet negotiation with history and expectation. With this elemental foundation, the next gallery will turn from nature’s forces to the stories of people shaped and marked by them.

From this vantage, the interplay between mediated photographic images and the sequencing of vignettes reveals itself—each work multiplied by the context of those beside it, patterns emerging only when viewed as a collective, not merely single frames.

Instead of inviting you into a single moment, the display revolves around process and accumulation—how daily decisions, psychological states, and cultural influences leave their mark. Pacing along the wall, you see an array of figures and objects. Some images are intimate—an artist’s hand paused over a desk, a half-full coffee cup, the faint smudge of graphite on a wrist—while others isolate a subject in a starkly lit space, the body at once vulnerable and persistent. The physical medium that ties these works together is not specified in the available material, but the configuration and hanging method—prints placed in measured succession, some edge-to-edge, some with a sliver of white border—creates a rhythm in the gallery that echoes the routines and interruptions of an artist’s daily life. This compositional decision matters: by grouping rather than isolating, Soren’s images suggest that no moment stands alone and that the artist’s life is constructed in fragments, revisions, and reversals. Stand far enough from the main wall to take in all four artists’ works together—this vantage reveals how the series of portraits and snapshots don’t line up so much as ripple, each artist’s memories subtly out of step with the next. Here, the interplay between public exhibition and private archive becomes legible.

Airitam chose to re-stage family photographs using gelatin silver prints, a medium developed for its archival longevity in the nineteenth century, so that every subject is given the care once reserved for formal studio portraits. This decision reframes everyday moments—birthdays, posed living-room embraces—into events worth preserving on museum walls, not just in fading albums. Lee crafted a sequence of inkjet prints from phone-camera snapshots, refusing to crop out digital timestamps and battery icons; this compositional decision foregrounds how technology mediates memory and prevents viewers from imagining a ‘timeless’ past. Bozeman assembled family vernacular photographs—everyday snapshots, often unposed and sometimes blurry—onto custom display boards, creating a communal record from what was once scattered across drawers and boxes. Montana selected to pair her images with handwritten captions recalling snippets of conversation, inscribing oral history directly onto the photographic surface and challenging the notion that family stories are ever complete. Each artist’s contribution cost something: the relinquishing of privacy, the risk of exposing raw or imperfect moments, and the refusal to let memory be fixed or finished. These choices set TLC apart from the psychological probing in An Artist Life, which you saw earlier, by foregrounding preservation and transformation over interior narrative. As the third part of State of the Union, TLC’s stories ask what must be kept, and what is changed, when photographs migrate from home to museum. One final revelation about how this institution shapes memory awaits further on.

As a direct result of this recognition, "The Persistence of Memories" was created. The new installation is scheduled for public exhibition from September 11 through October 25, 2026, marking the culmination of this collaboration. Traditional and digital art forms are blended, producing an immersive experience unique to this space. As recipient of the 2025/2026 Cummings Residency Prize, Lua Kobayashi spent her residency meeting with Japanese American individuals and families across the greater Boston area. In their living rooms and kitchens, residents shared family photographs, inherited household objects, handwritten notes, and paintings from concentration camps. Kobayashi gathered these heirlooms and conversations to preserve the overlooked histories of the local community and examine how memories of displacement are sustained across generations.
