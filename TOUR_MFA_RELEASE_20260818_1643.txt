Joan Miró’s Le Lézard aux plumes d’or, created in 1971, is an illustrated book that features 40 color lithographs on publisher’s vellum. This work was published by Louis Broder, a figure renowned for his expertise in producing artist books, or livres d'artiste, that meld text and images into a singular artistic experience. Broder's decision to commission Miró was driven by his desire to create limited-edition works that emphasized direct collaboration between artist, poet, and Mourlot Frères. Mourlot Frères, a prestigious printing atelier, executed the lithographs, ensuring Miró's vivid colors and surreal imagery were captured flawlessly. Later donated this extraordinary piece to the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, enriching its collection of artist books and reinforcing the museum's dedication to showcasing collaborative works of art. This collaboration exemplifies the exhibition's argument that books can transcend their textual roots to become visual masterpieces. The Lizard with Golden Feathers continues to challenge perceptions of what a book can be, merging the literary and the artistic into a single, compelling form.

Address: 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Orientation: Stand just beneath the exhibit "Au Soleil du Plafond" to fully appreciate its intricate details and the way it engages the space around it. From this vantage point, the interplay of light and shadow on the lithographs becomes strikingly apparent.

In 1955, Éditions Verve and Mourlot Frères, a renowned lithography workshop in Paris, published "Au Soleil du Plafond," a compelling livre d'artiste by Juan Gris, composed of 11 lithographs on Arches wove paper. Gris, a pivotal figure in Cubism, collaborated with French poet Pierre Reverdy, integrating Reverdy's text into the visual landscape. Reverdy's choice of words complements Gris's imagery, resulting in a harmonious blend that reflects the intellectual currents of mid-20th-century Paris. The lithographs are rarely shown, usually resting in archives, but now they grace the Museum of Fine Arts in Boston, within. This exhibition underscores the museum's dedication to showcasing significant collaborative pieces that revolutionized the art book as a form. As visitors explore these works, they are invited to consider the transformative impact of light not only within the artwork but also when applied to belief and spirituality, themes echoed in Le Lézard aux plumes d’or.

Stop 3: Moses and Monotheism

Address: 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Orientation: As you stand before Salvador Dalí's illustrations for "Moses and Monotheism," take a moment to appreciate the unique positioning that grants you a full view of the sheepskin's textural richness. This vantage point highlights the interplay between Dalí's surreal imagery and Sigmund Freud’s provocative text.

In 1974, Salvador Dalí illustrated Sigmund Freud’s "Moses and Monotheism" with ten drypoints and lithographs on sheepskin. Freud's 1939 text proposed that Moses was an Egyptian priest, challenging traditional beliefs. Dalí’s visual homage to Freud showcased the Surrealist movement's inspiration from psychoanalysis. The Hogarth Press published this edition, blending Dalí's art with Freud’s narrative to revolutionize books as integrated works of art through collaboration. The art reflects shifts in belief systems, akin to those Freud explored in his writings.
