From this vantage point, the intricate interplay between the vibrant lithographs and the delicate vellum becomes strikingly apparent — a testament to the meticulous craftsmanship that defines this work.

Joan Miró originally created a series of lithographs to illustrate his own poem in 1967, printed in Paris by publisher Louis Broder. Shortly after printing, Miró and Broder discovered a defect in the paper that distorted the colors across the sheets. Because the original lithographic stones had already been erased, Miró was forced to redraw an entirely new set of compositions from scratch. While early catalogues claimed the entire flawed 1967 print run was destroyed, Sotheby's later revealed that impressions from the initial run survived in private collections. Miró completed the brand-new series of plates four years later, finally releasing the finished edition in 1971. This action marked the culmination of a significant collaboration, resulting in a limited edition book featuring forty lithographs by Miró. The publication of this book not only showcased Miró's vibrant artistic style but also solidified Broder’s reputation as a distinguished publisher of fine art books. Because of Broder's decision to publish this work, the book remains a celebrated piece in the art world, ultimately finding its way into the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, where it continues to be admired. This extraordinary piece is part of the "Picasso, Miró, Dalí: Unbound" exhibition at the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, housed in the Linde Family Gallery. The book is an exquisite fusion of art and literature, exemplifying the livre d'artiste tradition, where images, words, and typography intersect seamlessly. Miró’s forty color lithographs, printed on both wove and Louis Broder’s vellum, dance across the pages with a whimsical energy. This work not only highlights Miró's unique artistic voice but also underscores the collaborative spirit that the exhibition celebrates. The gift of Boris Fridman, this piece enriches the museum's collection, inviting visitors to explore the possibilities of artistic transformation within the framework of the book.

Stop 2: Au Soleil du Plafond by Juan Gris

Address: 465 Huntington Ave, Boston, MA 02115

Coordinates: 42.3376, -71.0941

Orientation: Stand close to "Au Soleil du Plafond" to fully appreciate the interplay of color and form within the eleven lithographs. From this vantage point, the intricate geometry of Juan Gris's Cubist style emerges vividly, inviting you to explore how these elements interact with Pierre Reverdy's poetic expressions.

Around 1916, art dealer Léonce Rosenberg brought together painter Juan Gris and poet Pierre Reverdy to collaborate on a book project. The original plan called for Gris to illustrate twenty of Reverdy's poems, but the work stalled and came to an abrupt halt when Gris died of kidney failure in 1927 at age forty. At the time of his death, Gris had completed only eleven of the planned compositions in gouache. Nearly thirty years later, the Louis Broder Tériade and Reverdy revived the unfinished endeavor to honor their late friend. Published in 1955 under the title Au Soleil du Plafond, the completed volume paired Reverdy's poems with color lithographs made from Gris's surviving works. Together, Gris and Reverdy transformed the concept of the livre d'artiste, a genre that integrates visual art with text, into a compelling narrative format where both elements echo each other’s themes and aesthetics. The eleven lithographs, housed in are rarely on view and are usually kept in archives, offering a unique glimpse into this harmonious blend of text and imagery."Au Soleil du Plafond" thus advances the exhibition's argument by showcasing how transformation—in a collaborative artistic sense—can extend life and meaning beyond an artist's original timeline.

Orientation: As you stand here, position yourself near the center display case to fully appreciate how Salvador Dalí’s illustrations for "Moses and Monotheism" interact with Sigmund Freud’s text. This vantage point allows you to notice the intricate details in Dalí's artwork, which provide a visual narrative to Freud's complex theories.

In 1939, Sigmund Freud published his final work, arguing that Moses was born an Egyptian noble rather than a Hebrew slave. Salvador Dalí took up Freud's text in 1974, producing an edition of prints and engravings to illustrate the psychoanalyst's controversial thesis. Depending on the account, Dalí had these images printed onto sheets of sheepskin, lambskin, or parchment. Freud, renowned for pioneering psychoanalysis, authored the text, delving into the origins of monotheistic belief systems, which challenged traditional narratives. Published by The Hogarth Press, this collaboration illustrates the intertwined relationship between text and imagery — a core theme of the exhibition that showcases how artists like Dalí revolutionized the book as an art form. As you contemplate these illustrations, consider how the intersection of art and psychology challenges our understanding of cultural narratives, offering insights still relevant today.
