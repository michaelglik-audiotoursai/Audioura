Stop 1: Portrait de Madame Cézanne

Address: Musée Granet, 20 Rue Granet, 13100 Aix-en-Provence, France

Coordinates: 43.5297, 5.4549

Museum Information: Closed on Monday

Orientation: Welcome to the Musée Granet in Aix-en-Provence. Prepare to delve into the heart of this institution, tracing the collection's evolution through time. Encounter three iconic works: Portrait de Madame Cézanne by Paul Cézanne, Leicester Square, la nuit by Claude Monet, and Portrait de François-Marius Granet by Jean Auguste Dominique Ingres. Your first stop is Portrait de Madame Cézanne. Standing at mid-distance from "Portrait de Madame Cézanne," the light filtering through the gallery windows strikes the canvas at an angle, bringing out the subtle variations in tone on Marie-Hortense Fiquet’s face. From this vantage, the gaze of the sitter meets yours directly, and the surface texture of Cézanne’s brushwork becomes visible—each stroke deliberate, layered, a quiet record of the artist’s hand.

Portrait de Madame Cézanne was initially acquired by the French state in 1982 through a process known as dation, a legal mechanism allowing inheritance tax to be paid with artworks. This acquisition led to the painting being assigned to the Musée d'Orsay in Paris. However, in 1984, the painting was transferred from the Musée d'Orsay to the Musée Granet in Aix-en-Provence. This move brought the artwork closer to its origins, as Paul Cézanne, the artist, spent much of his life in Aix-en-Provence, thereby deepening the local connection to his legacy. Marie-Hortense Fiquet, Cézanne’s wife, sits at the center of this oil painting, created between 1885 and 1886. The sitter’s presence is striking not for any overt expression, but for the stillness—a quality repeated across the series of four portraits Cézanne made of her around the time of their marriage. She is known for never smiling in these works, her expression reserved, enigmatic, and closed to the viewer. Cézanne met Hortense when she was working as an artist’s model in Paris. Over the years, she would become both his frequent subject and the mother of his son. The first portrait Cézanne painted of her is now lost, which makes the surviving canvases, including this one, all the more significant. The museum’s acquisition of Cézanne’s only known portrait of his friend, the writer Émile Zola, in 2011, marked another turning point for the collection. With each addition, the Musée Granet reinforced its role as a keeper of Cézanne’s legacy in the city where he lived and worked. The muted palette and careful modeling of Hortense’s face give the work a sense of intimacy, yet also a distance—the effect heightened by the absence of a smile. The painting is an unvarnished record of Cézanne’s search for truth in form and character, a study of presence rendered with almost mathematical attention. As the museum built upon these foundations, other treasures followed, deepening the story of art in Aix-en-Provence.

Address: Musée Granet, 20 Rue Granet, 13100 Aix-en-Provence, France

Coordinates: 43.5297, 5.4549

Orientation: Stand directly before "Leicester Square, la nuit," allowing your gaze to meet the painting at eye level. From this vantage, the interplay of light and shadow—Monet’s brush capturing the city’s glow—becomes legible. Closer inspection reveals the layered texture of oil on canvas, and the subtle gradations of color that dissolve the boundaries between figure and atmosphere. The view evokes the vantage from The Green Room’s window, where Monet himself stood in 1901, and renders visible the passage of time—both in the city he painted, and in the museum that now preserves his vision.

In 1901, Claude Monet painted "Leicester Square, la nuit" during his visits to London, capturing the vibrant scene illuminated by the relatively new electric and artificial street lighting. This was a departure from his usual focus on the Thames and iconic London landmarks during the day. The painting, catalogued as Wildenstein 1615, eventually became part of et Suzanne Planque's collection and was placed on long-term loan to the Musée Granet in Aix-en-Provence in 2010. Monet created this work in oil on canvas, standing at 80.5 centimeters in height—a scale that immerses viewers in the urban nightscape. The artist chose a singular vantage: a window of a small room at The Green Room, a club in Leicester Square. From that perch, Monet observed the city transformed by gas and electric light, a spectacle still rare in 1901. The painting does not describe the square in precise architectural detail; instead, it breathes with the restless motion of crowds and the haze of illuminated air. Monet’s rapid, broken brushwork and layered glazes dissolve the contours of buildings and figures, rendering the city’s energy rather than its edges. At this moment in Monet’s career, he was intent on exploring the effects of artificial illumination—how modern lighting remade the city for the eye and for the imagination. Critics and scholars have remarked on how the painting’s surface flickers between legibility and abstraction: a record of both a place and a sensation. The cost, for Monet, was the risk of forsaking familiar subjects of river and bridge, and of translating the ephemeral experience of night into permanent color. "Leicester Square, la nuit" marks a turning point for Monet—a willingness to enter the pulse of the modern city at its most uncertain hour. The mood it conjures is one of wonder: the city alive with novelty, the painter awake to fleeting phenomena. The museum’s foundations, once shaped by local histories, now open onto the electric night of the twentieth century—each new acquisition reframing what it means to preserve, to look, to belong in a world always changing.

Directions: Your final stop in Musée Granet: Portrait de François-Marius Granet.

Stop 3: Portrait de François-Marius Granet

Address: Musée Granet, 20 Rue Granet, 13100 Aix-en-Provence, France

Coordinates: 43.5297, 5.4549

Orientation: As you stand here, position yourself just a step back from the gilded frame of Portrait de François-Marius Granet. From this close, the subtle play of light across Granet’s face and the sweep of Roman architecture behind him come into focus—details that vanish if you view the painting from an angle or through a crowd.

Jean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres painted this portrait of his friend François-Marius Granet in Rome, though records debate the precise year: the Musée Granet dates the canvas to 1807, while records at the Musée Ingres Bourdelle point to 1809. Ingres gave the painting directly to Granet, who held onto it for the rest of his life. Upon his death in 1849, Granet bequeathed the portrait alongside his personal art collection. Now, lean in and examine the surface: oil on canvas, Neoclassical in its restraint but quietly radical in one respect. The folds of his clothing are rendered with a clarity that suggests both friendship and mutual respect; Ingres and Granet were not only colleagues but former classmates under Jacques-Louis David, the famed Neoclassical master. Critics have noted how the setting—the forum’s ancient columns—mirrors Granet’s own nostalgia for home and the classical world he studied. Unlike the intimate, domestic air of the Portrait de Madame Cézanne you stopped at a moment ago, this work opens outward: Granet is framed by Rome itself, a city that shaped his career and friendships. And where Leicester Square, la nuit drew from collections first assembled -Vincens, here the story turns inward, to the legacy of those who built the museum identity from within. There’s a certain reverence in standing before this canvas: Granet chose to keep the portrait for decades, only releasing it to the museum after his death. It is both a personal relic and a cornerstone of the collection—a quiet reminder that the museum’s origins are bound to the dedication and vision of the artists whose names now fill these rooms.
