Stop 1 is the Salem Maritime National Historic Site.

Address: 160 Derby St, Salem, MA 01970

Coordinates: 42.5204, -70.8953

Type/Specialty: Historic Site

Specific Examples: Historic buildings, beautiful waterfront views, informative exhibits.

Orientation: You are about to embark on a cycling journey through Salem, MA. Salem Maritime National Historic Site showcases Salem's significance as a trading port from the late 18th through the early 19th centuries. Your first stop is The House of the Seven Gables. From the Salem MBTA station, head southeast on Washington St, turn right onto Derby St, and continue until you reach the site. You might notice the charming historic architecture as you approach the waterfront. Here, the red-brick Custom House stands watch over the water, and the salt wind carries the call of gulls across the open wharves. Pause where the wharf juts into Salem Harbor, with the twelve historic structures of the Salem Maritime National Historic Site rising behind you and the Atlantic stretching ahead.

The living pulse of Salem beats strongest along this waterfront. The warehouses and Custom House here once echoed with the daily shuffle of dockworkers tallying barrels of molasses, spices, and textiles from ships that crossed the Atlantic and beyond. The cycle of arrival, unloading, and departure set the rhythm for the community—the tides not just of water, but of fortune and custom. The Custom House holds a rich history. One of its most famous clerks, Nathaniel Hawthorne, worked here as a surveyor. During his time amidst shipping routines, Hawthorne drew inspiration to write 'The Scarlet Letter,' a novel that became a cornerstone of American literature. The desk he used is still preserved in an upstairs office, symbolizing the blend of daily work and artistic expression.

Directions: Hop on your bike and pedal along Derby Street towards the water. Follow the scenic coastal route past Derby Wharf and continue biking until you reach The House of the Seven Gables on Turner Street.

Stop 2: The House of the Seven Gables

Address: 115 Derby St, Salem, MA 01970

Coordinates: 42.5208, -70.8955

Type/Specialty: Historic House Museum

Specific Examples: The iconic gabled house, beautiful gardens, guided tours of the interior.

Orientation: Dismount your bicycle at the foot of Turner Street, and let your gaze rise above the salt grass to where the gables climb against the sky, their angles sharp even after generations of wind from the harbor. The best position for viewing is just before the main entry, where the full breadth of the house and its layered rooftops reveal themselves beyond the garden wall.

In 1668, on this ground bordering Salem Harbor, Capt. John Turner—a merchant and ship-owner—built this house. Three generations of the Turner family lived and died beneath its steep-pitched gables. The timber and clapboard you see today have weathered countless seasons, enduring the slow churn of fortune and time. With its seven gables jutting at odd and ambitious angles, the house became more than just a family dwelling.

In the 1800s, Susannah Ingersoll owned the house. Her cousin, Nathaniel Hawthorne, visited her here. Local tradition holds that the structure and histories Hawthorne encountered within these rooms—the dark staircases, hidden spaces, and the creak of old wood underfoot—inspired his 1851 novel, "The House of the Seven Gables." The book gave this place its lasting name. Hawthorne’s inspiration, drawn from his cousin’s house rather than invention alone, reminds us that fiction sometimes grows from the grain of real boards and the hush of real halls.

In 2007, the government recognized the house’s layered past by designating the House of the Seven Gables campus as a National Historic Landmark District. This official act placed the house among the select few in the nation whose preservation is considered essential for the country itself. The air here carries the tang of the harbor, and the sound of gulls echoes against the old wood. The house’s architecture conceals as much as it reveals; look closely at the angles and the shadows.

Stop 3: Salem Willows Park
Address: 167 Fort Ave, Salem, MA 01970
Coordinates: 42.5155, -70.8958 Type/Specialty: Public Park Specific Examples: Amusement rides, arcade games, picnic areas by the water. The willows’ pale leaves cast shifting shadows on the path, and the sweep of the bay opens just ahead. Pause at the edge of the oldest stand of trees—this is the vantage from which generations have watched Salem Willows Park change. In 1858, the city designated this area as a public park, naming it for the white willow trees that still line the water’s edge. Those trees were planted to make this breezy peninsula a place of gathering rather than industry, marking a turning point in Salem’s use of its coastline. The willows themselves, the arcades, and even the hum of bumper cars all carry the memory of that shift. Picture a summer in 1904, when painter Maurice Prendergast set up his easel at Salem Willows Park. Prendergast, inspired by the play of crowds and sunlight, created his painting 'Salem Willows' here. His work captures the swirl of visitors, the flicker of willow shadows, and the sense of movement that still animates the park. A men’s cottage was built nearby. Beyond the laughter and games, Salem Willows Park stands as a living record of Salem’s transformation—one you can see in every willow-shadowed step.
Directions: Head out of Salem Willows Park and pedal along Fort Avenue towards the coast. Enjoy the scenic views as you ride past Winter Island Park. Continue biking along Derby Street until you reach Salem Common, where you can take a break and explore the historic area.

Stop 4: Salem Common

Address: Washington Square, Salem, MA 01970

Coordinates: 42.5202, -70.8950

Type/Specialty: Public Park

Specific Examples: Lush green spaces, historic monuments, community events.

Orientation: The best vantage is just off the main walkway, where the lawn stretches wide and the city’s daily life drifts across—joggers, children, neighbors crossing between errands—while history’s layers remain underfoot.

The Salem Common, established in the 17th century, stands as a living stage for the city’s evolving identity. Its story is marked by ritual and repetition, where ordinary days and extraordinary moments have blended for centuries. One account that anchors this place in American tradition dates to 1637. That year, the first muster took place here: a regiment of local militia assembled and drilled together for the first time. The men who gathered—farmers, artisans, townsfolk—did so not as professional soldiers, but as neighbors charged with the community’s defense. This first muster laid the groundwork for what would become the National Guard, a tradition of citizen-soldiers that continues today. The cause was necessity: the frontier was uncertain, and the act of coming together for shared protection shaped Salem’s sense of unity and responsibility. Centuries later, the Common again served as a gathering ground when the Great Salem Fire in 1914 displaced hundreds. The open space provided refuge for families and a focal point for communal aid. The rhythms of the Common—drills, celebrations, and crisis—have always been marked by people moving together, whether in military formation or in the slow, supportive tide of neighbors helping one another. Crickets and footsteps are part of the present soundscape, but the shape of the traditions forged here remains visible. This park is not just a patch of green—it is the city’s memory in motion, its rituals continuing in every season, as constant as the salt in the air that lingers from the Willows you saw before. The route ahead will carry you toward the Salem Witch Museum, where a different kind of community reckoning played out.

Directions: Ride along Essex St and continue until you reach the Salem Witch Museum on your left. Enjoy the scenic route and immerse yourself in the history of Salem as you pedal towards your destination. It is about a minute's walk away.

Stop 5: Salem Witch Museum

Address:

Coordinates: 42.5201, -70.8956

Type/Specialty: Museum

Specific Examples: Exhibits on the witch trials, life-size dioramas, informative presentations.

Orientation: Standing near the entrance of the Salem Witch Museum, the red Gothic windows loom overhead, their panes catching the gray New England light. The hum of passing traffic on Washington Square North mingles with the voices of visitors clustering at the threshold.

Founded in 1972, the Salem Witch Museum stands on ground that, in 1692, held the home of Reverend John Higginson, the elder minister of Salem. Inside, the museum’s first presentation draws visitors into the year 1692 and the harrowing story of Giles Corey. Corey, a farmer, refused to plead when accused of witchcraft. For his silence, the authorities resorted to a brutal measure: they pressed him to death, laying heavy stones upon his chest until, after days beneath the weight, he expired. Giles Corey’s refusal to confess or deny—his final words lost to history—became an emblem of resistance, and his fate is depicted with stark clarity in the museum’s immersive retelling.

Another story told within these walls is that of George Burroughs, a former minister. In 1692, at the height of the hysteria, Burroughs was hanged for witchcraft. His execution, like Corey’s death, is presented not as distant legend but as a moment when fear, accusation, and the machinery of justice converged with deadly result.

Salem, a coastal city in Essex County, Massachusetts, settled by English colonists in 1626, holds many such stories. Today, the witch museum’s Gothic façade faces out across the city’s historic neighborhoods, where echoes of past judgments blend with the present. As you step out, ponder how these narratives have shaped the streets you walk on, and the city that endures.

Together, these stops reveal the enduring narratives of community, resilience, and transformation that have shaped Salem over centuries. As you reflect on these stories, consider how they continue to resonate in the lives of those who walk these streets today. That's 4 stops in all.
