Step-by-Step Audio Guided Tour: Musée d'Orsay, Paris, France - Museum Tour

Stop 1: L'Origine du monde

Address: Musée d'Orsay, 1 Rue de la Légion d'Honneur, 75007 Paris, France

Coordinates: 48.8606, 2.3266

The museum is open Tuesday to Sunday from 9:30 to 6:00, and closed on Mondays. Admission is 16 euros for adults; under-18s go free.

Type/Specialty: Peinture

Museum Information:

Orientation: The transformation of the former gare d'Orsay into a museum under President Valéry Giscard d'Estaing embodies a commitment to preserving and showcasing the arts of the 19th century. Your first stop is L'Origine du monde. Stand slightly to the left of L’Origine du monde’s center, where the light softly reveals the textures and tones of the oil paint on canvas. From this angle, the layered brushwork that models flesh and shadow becomes legible, exposing the physical presence Courbet imbued in this intimate portrayal.

L’Origine du monde depicts a close-up view of a woman’s nude torso from the waist to the upper thighs, focusing on her genitalia in a direct, unidealized manner. The artist’s technique involves meticulous layering of flesh tones and shadows to achieve a tactile realism, emphasizing texture and volume rather than abstraction or mythological disguise. This focus on corporeal reality was a deliberate departure from the traditional allegorical or romanticized depictions of the nude that were prevalent in 19th-century art. The artist sought to challenge academic conventions by representing subjects stripped of idealization or symbolism. L’Origine du monde marks a pivotal moment in his career, as he pushed the boundaries of what could be shown and accepted in art. The intimacy and explicitness of the painting confront societal taboos and artistic norms, reflecting the artist’s commitment to honesty in representation. The work evokes curiosity about origins, nature, and the body’s role in identity and art, compelling attention to the materiality of flesh and the tensions between concealment and revelation.

The Turkish-Egyptian diplomat Khalil-Bey likely commissioned L’Origine du monde and became its first owner. As a diplomat known for his eclectic art collection, Khalil-Bey kept the painting private, and it remained unknown to the public for over a century. This long concealment and its eventual public unveiling underline the painting’s controversial status and the evolving public discourse on sexuality and artistic freedom. The delayed exposure altered the trajectory of the painting’s reception and positioned it uniquely within the Musée d’Orsay’s collection, where it stands among other 19th-century works that redefined representation and perception in art.

Stop 2: Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe (Luncheon on the Grass)

Address: Musée d'Orsay, 1 Rue de la Légion d'Honneur, 75007 Paris, France

Coordinates: 48.8606, 2.3266

Orientation: Stand slightly to the left and at a distance that allows your eyes to take in the entire canvas of Édouard Manet’s Luncheon on the Grass (Le Déjeuner sur l’herbe).

Luncheon on the Grass marks a turning point in Manet’s career. In this oil on canvas, he deliberately placed a nude woman alongside fully dressed men in a natural setting, referencing but also defying the Renaissance tradition of the pastoral nude. The painting is notable for its broad, visible brushstrokes and stark contrasts, rejecting the smooth finish prized by the academies. Manet’s technique emphasizes flatness and the surface of the canvas, underscoring his break from illusionistic depth. The scene—two men conversing and a nude woman gazing directly at the viewer—evokes a tension between familiarity and estrangement, inviting contemplation of social conventions and the role of the nude in art.

Before creating this work, Manet had been navigating the Parisian art scene with a growing desire to depict contemporary life in ways that broke with classical ideals. The painting was initially rejected by the official Paris Salon, the dominant exhibition venue for artists, because it defied the accepted rules of composition, subject matter, and decorum. Manet then exhibited it at the Salon des Refusés in 1863, a showcase for works refused by the Salon jury. This public display drew intense attention and controversy, signaling a rupture in the art world’s acceptance of traditional styles and paving the way for modern art. The shock felt at the Salon des Refusés came from the painting’s candid presentation, which unsettled viewers accustomed to allegory and myth.

Manet’s refusal to conform extended beyond Luncheon on the Grass to another work, L’Origine du monde. Both paintings confront the viewer with unidealized nudity, but while L’Origine du monde offers an intimate, close-up study, Luncheon on the Grass situates its figures in a public, social space. This progression illustrates how Manet continuously tested boundaries. The controversy surrounding Luncheon on the Grass compelled other artists and critics to reconsider what could be shown, gradually loosening the strictures of the Salon and allowing Impressionists and Post-Impressionists to emerge, shifting artistic priorities toward modern life and perception.

Today, Luncheon on the Grass stands as a visible moment of rupture, where the past’s conventions began to give way beneath the brushstrokes of a new era.

Directions: From here, make your way to Bal du moulin de la Galette.

Stop 3: Bal du moulin de la Galette

Address: Musée d'Orsay, 1 Rue de la Légion d'Honneur, 75007 Paris, France

Coordinates: 48.8606, 2.3266

Orientation: As you stand just a few paces from Bal du moulin de la Galette, shift your gaze slightly to catch the full breadth of this canvas. From this vantage point, the layering of figures, the interplay of light through the dappled leaves above, and the subtle movements within the crowd become legible all at once, revealing the scene’s lively atmosphere that a more direct, head-on look might flatten.

Bal du moulin de la Galette depicts a lively Sunday afternoon at the original Moulin de la Galette in Montmartre, where working-class Parisians dressed up to dance, drink, and eat galettes—thin pancakes that gave the place its name. Renoir’s brushwork is fluid, with quick dabs and sweeps of paint that capture the flickering sunlight filtering through the trees and the sense of movement in the crowd. The loose technique allows patches of brightness and shadow to play across faces and dresses, emphasizing the momentary nature of leisure and social life. At this point in his career, Renoir was exploring the Impressionist focus on light and movement, moving away from the studio for open-air scenes that emphasized everyday life. Bal du moulin de la Galette marks a moment when he combined this interest with a more complex composition, arranging dozens of figures in an open-air dance hall to evoke the convivial spirit of Parisian society. The painting’s depiction of working-class leisure complicates the usual narratives of Parisian nightlife by showing joy and sociability as accessible, not reserved for the elite. Faces turn toward each other in conversation and dance, while the trees overhead filter sunlight into a mosaic of gold and green. The scene becomes a celebration not only of the people but of light itself, a hallmark of Impressionism’s study of ephemeral effects. The figures here are not posed icons but participants in a moment of shared celebration—a human mosaic caught between shadow and sun. This scene quietly documents a rhythm of daily life that influenced how artists after Renoir depicted movement, light, and social experience in the rapidly changing modern city.

Bal du moulin de la Galette hung in the Louvre from 1929, where it was part of the national collection but less accessible to the public. Later, it was placed in a context dedicated to its time and style, making it more visible and central to the museum’s narrative. This relocation reshaped the public’s experience of the painting, embedding it firmly within the narrative of 19th-century Parisian art and society. One lesser-known episode connected to this work involves Gustave Caillebotte, a painter and collector who played a vital role in preserving Impressionist art.

Stop 4: Une moderne Olympia (A Modern Olympia)

Address: Musée d'Orsay, 1 Rue de la Légion d'Honneur, 75007 Paris, France

Coordinates: 48.8606, 2.3266

Orientation: Stand at a distance where you can take in the entire canvas of Une moderne Olympia at once.

Thanks to curator Anne Montfort-Tanguy, the Musée Picasso included the painting in its 2024 exhibition Picasso iconophage, renewing scholarly and public attention to Cézanne’s reinterpretation of Manet’s Olympia. It depicts a white woman reclining, reminiscent of Manet’s provocative Olympia from 1865, which the Musée d’Orsay already displays nearby. Alongside her stands a black servant, a figure commonly portrayed in 19th-century French art that reflects the prevailing social and racial hierarchies of the era. At this moment in his career, Cézanne was responding to the shock Manet had once caused, wrestling with new ways to depict form and space while engaging with modern subjects. His modern Olympia is not just homage or imitation but a deliberate dialogue, a means to explore the shifting definitions of art and society. Standing before it, one senses the tension between surface and social commentary—a gesture that refigures a scandalous image into a quiet but charged statement about modern art’s evolving legacy.

Directions: From here, make your way to La Nuit étoilée (Starry Night Over the Rhône).

Stop 5: La Nuit étoilée (Starry Night Over the Rhône)

Coordinates: 48.8606, 2.3266

Orientation: From this vantage point, the rhythmic brushwork and the luminous interplay between stars and water reveal themselves fully, which vanishes if viewed too close or head-on.

In September 1888, Vincent van Gogh set up his canvas outdoors on the bank of the Rhône in Arles, positioning himself directly beneath a gas jet. Working in the dark just a short walk from his rented house, he took on the challenge of capturing the sparkling night sky alongside the new artificial illumination cast across the river. He chose to depict these artificial lights merging with natural starlight to explore the interplay of human presence and nature’s vastness. Van Gogh sent a written description and a sketch of the completed composition to his brother Theo. Here, the sharp contours and vivid hues translate the wonder he found in nocturnal scenes, intertwining nature’s grandeur with human intimacy, seen in the figures strolling along the shore. The painter’s decision to portray the river at night—traditionally a difficult subject due to low light conditions—opened new possibilities for exploring emotional depth through paint.

This painting later entered the Musée d’Orsay’s collection in Paris, where it is preserved alongside the museum’s extensive Impressionist and post-Impressionist holdings. The museum itself was originally the Gare d’Orsay, a railway station constructed at the turn of the 20th century, now repurposed to showcase artworks like this one and connecting histories of movement—both mechanical and artistic. Thanks to the efforts of collector Fridman, who assembled works like this to enrich Parisian collections, Starry Night Over the Rhône is accessible here for sustained contemplation about the passage from Impressionism to Post-Impressionism and its lasting impact on modern art.
